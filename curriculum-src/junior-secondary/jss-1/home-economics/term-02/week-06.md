## Hook
Have you ever visited the famous ancient Kofar Mata dye pits in Kano, or walked through the vibrant fabric markets of Itoku in Abeokuta, Oshodi in Lagos, or Main Market in Onitsha? Everywhere you look, people are wearing radiant, intricately patterned fabrics bursting with cobalt blues, rich indigos, vibrant yellows, and earthy terracottas. These celebrated Nigerian fabrics—traditionally known as **Adire** or **Kampala**—are not produced by massive multi-billion-dollar foreign textile factories! They are crafted by local artisans using simple white cotton cloth, strings, stones, and basins of colorful dye! What if you could learn this exact same practical craft right now in school, transform a cheap piece of plain white calico into a designer fabric worth five times the price, and sell it to friends, family, and tourists for a handsome profit? Textile crafts like **Tie and Dye** are not just school art projects; they are powerful engines of vocational self-reliance and entrepreneurship. In this lesson, you will master the chemistry, tying techniques, dyeing procedures, and safety rules of Tie and Dye!

## Key Terms
- Term: Income-Yielding Crafts
- Definition: Practical, decorative, and functional handmade articles (such as tie-and-dye fabrics, beadwork, basketry, and pottery) produced using artistic skills to generate financial income and self-employment.
- Term: Tie and Dye (*Adire*)
- Definition: A traditional resist-dyeing method where portions of fabric are tightly bound, folded, knotted, or stitched with strings to prevent dye penetration, creating contrasting artistic patterns.
- Term: Resist Dyeing
- Definition: Any textile coloring technique that uses mechanical barriers (strings, wax, clamps, paste) to block dye from reaching specific areas of a fabric.
- Term: Destarching
- Definition: The essential preliminary process of washing new, unwashed fabric in hot water without soap to remove factory sizing, starches, and chemicals that hinder dye absorption.
- Term: Caustic Soda (Sodium Hydroxide - NaOH)
- Definition: A strong, highly alkaline chemical used in vat dyeing to swell the cotton fibers and activate the dye so it can penetrate the yarn structure.
- Term: Sodium Hydrosulphite (Hydros)
- Definition: A powerful chemical reducing agent that converts insoluble vat dye powder into a soluble, reduced form capable of chemically bonding with cotton cellulose.
- Term: Vatting
- Definition: The process of dissolving vat dye powder, caustic soda, and sodium hydrosulphite in hot water to create an active, chemically reactive dye bath.
- Term: Oxidation
- Definition: The natural chemical reaction where dyed fabric exposed to atmospheric oxygen oxidizes the dye back to its permanent, insoluble, wash-fast color state.

## Core Concept
### 1. Fundamentals of Tie and Dye Textile Craft
[IMAGE: alt="Educational diagram and authentic visual representation of Production of Income-Yielding Crafts" caption="Production of Income-Yielding Crafts: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration"]

Tie and dye is one of West Africa's most ancient, celebrated textile art forms, deeply rooted in Nigerian culture (*Adire Oniko* in Yoruba):
- **Core Principle:** Dye only colors the exposed fabric; tightly bound or compressed areas resist the dye solution, remaining pure white (or the original base color).
- **Fabric Selection:** Always use **100% natural cellulose fabrics** (cotton, calico, poplin, or guinea brocade). Synthetic fabrics (such as 100% polyester or nylon) do not absorb traditional vat dyes easily and will produce faded, patchy results.

### 2. Materials, Tools, and Chemicals Required
1. **Textile Materials:** 1 to 2 yards of 100% plain white or unbleached cotton fabric.
2. **Resist Materials:** Heavy twine, raffia fibers, thick rubber bands, needle and thread, marbling stones, bottle caps, or coins.
3. **Dyeing Utensils:** Plastic or stainless steel basins/buckets (never use aluminum basins, as caustic soda corrodes aluminum!), wooden stirring rods, measuring spoons, scissors.
4. **Protective Equipment (PPE):** Heavy rubber elbow-length gloves, plastic apron, chemical face mask, and eye goggles.
5. **The Active Chemicals:**
   - **Vat Dye Powder:** Concentrated synthetic pigment (e.g., royal blue, emerald green, scarlet red).
   - **Caustic Soda (Sodium Hydroxide - NaOH):** Highly alkaline pellets or flakes.
   - **Sodium Hydrosulphite (Hydros):** White crystalline reducing agent powder.
   - *Standard Mixing Proportion per 1 Yard of Cotton:*
     - 1 level tablespoon Caustic Soda
     - 1 level tablespoon Sodium Hydrosulphite
     - 1 level teaspoon Vat Dye Powder
     - Dissolved in approximately 2 to 3 liters of hot water.

### 3. Tying Methods (Pattern Creation)
Different methods of manipulating the fabric produce distinctive artistic effects:
1. **Pleating / Accordion Folding:**
   - The fabric is folded back and forth like an accordion fan, then bound tightly with twine at regular intervals.
   - *Result:* Crisp, parallel vertical, horizontal, or diagonal stripes.
2. **Knotting:**
   - The fabric is gathered into long ropes and tied into tight, solid overhand knots.
   - *Result:* Irregular, jagged, organic radial bursts.
3. **Marbling / Crumpling:**
   - The entire dry fabric is scrunched and crumpled into a compact, chaotic ball and wrapped tightly in all directions with heavy twine like a spider's web.
   - *Result:* An intricate, veined stone-like marble pattern.
4. **Circle / Sunburst Binding (With Objects):**
   - Marbles, small round stones, or coins are pushed into the fabric and wrapped tightly with string around their base.
   - *Result:* Concentric circles and diamond sunburst patterns.
5. **Stitching (*Adire Alabere*):**
   - Intricate designs are sewn by hand using needle and thread with running stitches, then pulled tightly like a drawstring before dyeing.
   - *Result:* Exquisite, delicate figurative and geometric motifs.

---

### 4. Step-by-Step Dyeing and Finishing Procedure
1. **Destarching:** Immerse new fabric in hot water and rub vigorously to remove factory starch. Wring damp; damp fabric absorbs dye more evenly than bone-dry fabric.
2. **Binding:** Execute your chosen tying method firmly. **The tighter the string is wrapped, the cleaner and whiter the resist lines will be!**
3. **Preparing the Dye Bath (Vatting):**
   - Boil water in a pot.
   - Pour hot water into a plastic basin. Add the measured vat dye powder, caustic soda, and sodium hydrosulphite.
   - Stir gently with a wooden stick until completely dissolved. The solution will change color (often to a yellowish-green tint), indicating the dye has been chemically reduced.
4. **Dyeing:**
   - Immerse the tied fabric into the dye bath.
   - Turn and agitate the fabric gently with wooden rods for **10 to 20 minutes**, ensuring all exposed areas absorb dye evenly.
5. **Oxidation:**
   - Lift the fabric out of the dye bath with wooden sticks and place it on an open surface for 5 to 10 minutes.
   - Watch the chemical miracle happen! As atmospheric oxygen hits the fabric, it oxidizes the reduced dye back to its permanent, brilliant color (e.g., turning from yellowish-green to deep royal blue).
6. **Rinsing and Untying:**
   - Rinse the still-tied fabric in multiple changes of clean cold water until the rinse water runs completely clear.
   - Carefully snip the tying strings with a scissors or razor blade, taking care **not to cut the fabric cloth**!
   - Unfold the fabric to reveal your magnificent design. Give it a final rinse and spread it flat to **dry in the shade** (drying in direct scorching sun can fade fresh dyes).

### 5. Chemical Safety Rules and Commercial Enterprise
- **Caustic Soda Safety:** Caustic soda is a severe chemical corrosive. It can cause permanent blindness if splashed into the eyes and chemical burns on skin.
  - **Always wear heavy rubber gloves, protective goggles, and a face mask!**
  - If caustic soda splashes onto bare skin, immediately flush with abundant cold running water and apply mild vinegar or lemon juice to neutralize the alkali.
- **Commercial Potential:** Tie-and-dye articles (bedsheets, pillowcases, shirts, silk scarves, table runners) have high profit margins. A ₦2,000 piece of plain white cotton fabric can be transformed through tie-and-dye into an artisan tablecloth sold for ₦8,000 to ₦12,000, creating immediate teenage self-employment!

## Common Mistakes
- **Skipping the Destarching Stage:** Dyeing brand-new, unwashed fabric straight from the market stall. Factory starches and chemical coatings block the dye from penetrating the cotton fibers, causing the dye to wash completely out during the first laundry cycle!
- **Using Metal Aluminum Basins for Dyeing:** Mixing caustic soda in aluminum pots. Caustic soda chemically attacks and dissolves aluminum, releasing hydrogen gas and ruining both the pot and the dye bath! Always use sturdy plastic basins.
- **Handling Caustic Soda with Bare Hands:** Scooping chemical crystals with bare fingers. Caustic soda reacts with skin moisture to produce severe chemical burns; always wear heavy rubber gloves!
- **Untying Fabric While the Dye is Still Unoxidized:** Cutting the strings immediately after pulling the fabric out of the dye bath. The dye must be exposed to air to oxidize into its insoluble permanent state; otherwise, running dye will bleed into the white resist areas, ruining the crisp design!

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Formulating a Chemical Recipe for Fabric Dyeing
**Scenario:** A Home Economics student wants to dye **3 yards** of plain white cotton fabric to make a set of decorative dining table mats and napkins.
- **Task:** Calculate the exact quantities of vat dye powder, caustic soda, and sodium hydrosulphite required, based on the standard single-yard recipe (1 tablespoon caustic soda, 1 tablespoon hydrosulphite, 1 teaspoon dye per yard).
- **Solution:**
  1. *Caustic Soda required:* $3\text{ yards} \times 1\text{ tablespoon} = \mathbf{3\text{ tablespoons}}$ of caustic soda.
  2. *Sodium Hydrosulphite required:* $3\text{ yards} \times 1\text{ tablespoon} = \mathbf{3\text{ tablespoons}}$ of hydrosulphite.
  3. *Vat Dye Powder required:* $3\text{ yards} \times 1\text{ teaspoon} = \mathbf{3\text{ teaspoons}}$ (or 1 level tablespoon) of dye powder.
  - The chemicals should be dissolved in approximately 6 to 9 liters of boiling water.

### Example 2: Troubleshooting Blurred, Muddy Resist Patterns
**Question:** A student attempts circle tie-and-dye with bottle caps, but when untied, the circles are pale, blurry, and smeared with dye instead of showing crisp, sharp white lines. Explain what went wrong and how to fix it next time.
- **Solution:**
  - *Cause of Failure:* The tying string was wrapped **too loosely** around the bottle caps, allowing the liquid dye solution to seep beneath the bindings.
  - *Corrective Action:* Next time, the student must wrap the heavy twine or rubber bands **much more tightly** and wind the string around the base multiple times, pulling with firm tension. The tighter the mechanical compression, the more impervious the resist barrier will be, guaranteeing razor-sharp white patterns.
