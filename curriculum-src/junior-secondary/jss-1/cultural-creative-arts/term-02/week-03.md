## Hook
From the majestic coral bead crowns (*Ade*) and royal collars worn by Yoruba *Obas* and the *Oba* of Benin Kingdom, to the melodic, colorful waist beads (*Jigida*) worn by Hausa, Fulani, and Igbo maidens, beads have stood at the very heart of Nigerian nobility, fashion, and spirituality for centuries! But did you know that the English word "bead" actually comes from the ancient Anglo-Saxon word *bede*, which literally means "prayer"? Centuriers ago, pilgrims counted their prayers using small perforated stones strung on cords! Even more exciting: right here in Nigeria, in the historic city of Bida in Niger State, Nupe glassmakers have been world-famous for over two hundred years for melting discarded glass bottles inside fiery clay kilns to create dazzling, colorful glass beads. You do not need expensive royal coral to create beadwork; with waste paper, drinking straws, and glue, you can become an artisan! In this lesson, you will master the rich history of beadwork, explore traditional and modern bead types, and learn how to construct your own stylish beads from recycled paper!

## Key Terms
- Term: Bead
- Definition: A small decorative piece of stone, bone, shell, glass, wood, clay, plastic, or paper, pierced with a central hole for stringing, threading, or sewing into ornaments.
- Term: Beadwork
- Definition: The artistic craft of assembling, stringing, or stitching beads together or attaching them onto fabric to create jewelry, royal regalia, ceremonial garments, and ornamental objects.
- Term: Bede
- Definition: The Middle English and Old English root word meaning "prayer" or "petition," from which the modern word *bead* originated because small pierced stones were used to count prayers on rosaries.
- Term: Coral Beads (*Iyun* / *Ivie*)
- Definition: Precious organic beads harvested from marine coral skeletons, revered across Southern Nigeria (Yoruba, Edo, Urhobo, Igbo) as the ultimate symbol of royal authority, nobility, and ancestral blessings.
- Term: Bida Glass Beads
- Definition: Renowned traditional glass beads crafted by Nupe artisans in Bida, Niger State, using clay kilns, recycled glass, and blowpipes.
- Term: Jigida (Waist Beads)
- Definition: Traditional strands of colorful beads worn around the hips and waist by Nigerian women and girls to signify femininity, enhance body posture, and mark rites of passage.
- Term: Findings
- Definition: The small metal components used in jewelry making to join, link, or fasten beaded items, including clasps, jump rings, headpins, and crimp beads.
- Term: Monofilament Nylon (Fishing Line)
- Definition: A strong, single-strand translucent synthetic cord widely used in bead stringing due to its strength, durability, and flexibility.
- Term: Improvised Beads
- Definition: Beads constructed from non-traditional or recycled materials such as rolled paper, drinking straws, seeds, bottle caps, or dried clay.
- Term: Crimp Bead
- Definition: A tiny, soft metal bead squashed with flat-nose pliers to hold stringing wire securely in place and lock clasps without bulky knots.

## Core Concept
### 1. Meaning and Historical Origins of Bead Work
[IMAGE: alt="Educational diagram and authentic visual representation of Bead Work" caption="Bead Work: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration"]

A **bead** is a small decorative object that has a central hole (perforation) through which a thread, wire, or cord can pass.
- **Etymological Origin:** The word comes from the Middle English word **bede**, meaning *prayer*. In medieval Europe, devout Christians counted repetitive prayers by sliding pierced wooden or bone beads along a string (the Rosary). Over time, the name for the prayer itself (*bede*) became transferred to the physical perforated object.
- **Deep Antiquity:** Archaeological excavations reveal that humans have made and worn beads for over 75,000 years! The earliest known beads were crafted from pierced marine snail shells, ostrich eggshells, animal teeth, and hollow bird bones.

### 2. Beadwork in Nigerian Cultural Heritage
In Nigerian culture, beads are not merely decorative trinkets; they carry profound social, spiritual, and political prestige:
1. **Royal Regalia & Authority:**
   - In Yoruba culture, coral beads (*Iyun*) are sacred royal ornaments. Only crowned kings (*Obas*) and titled chiefs (*Ijoye*) may wear complete coral beaded crowns (*Ade*), beaded slippers (*Bata Ileke*), and beaded horse-tail whisks (*Irukere*).
   - In the ancient Benin Kingdom, the *Oba* and royal court wear majestic high-collared neckpieces and shirts composed entirely of cylindrical coral beads (*Ivie*), symbolizing inviolable imperial dignity.
2. **Feminine Identity & Rites of Passage (*Jigida*):**
   - Worn around the waist across Northern, Eastern, and Western Nigeria, waist beads (*Jigida* in Hausa and Igbo, *Bebedi* in Yoruba) serve multiple cultural roles:
     - They celebrate female beauty and modesty.
     - They serve as body-monitoring gauges (tightening when the wearer gains weight and loosening when weight is lost).
     - They represent milestone gifts during puberty, marriage, and childbirth ceremonies.
3. **The Master Glassmakers of Bida (Nupe Heritage):**
   - The Nupe glassmakers of Bida, Niger State (known as the *Masaga* guild), represent one of Africa's most remarkable glassworking traditions.
   - For generations, master artisans have melted crushed scrap glass bottles inside beehive-shaped clay furnaces. Using iron rods, they skillfully wind molten glass into multi-colored striped beads, bangles, and rings that are exported across West Africa.
4. **Historical Currency & Trade:**
   - Before paper naira or metal coins existed, cowrie shells (*Ewo* / *Kudi*) and Venetian glass chevron beads were used as legal tender for purchasing goods and paying bride prices across West Africa.

### 4. Essential Tools and Materials for Beadwork
To produce modern beadwork items (such as necklaces, bracelets, anklets, and keyholders), the bead artisan uses specific tools and supplies:
- **Stringing Cords:**
  - *Fishing Line (Monofilament Nylon):* Strong, waterproof, and near-invisible; ideal for structured collars and bracelets.
  - *Elastic Stretch Cord:* Flexible silicone rubber cord used for clasp-free pull-on bracelets.
  - *Tiger Tail Wire:* Nylon-coated stainless steel wire offering exceptional kink-resistant strength for heavy stone and glass beads.
- **Findings (Hardware):**
  - *Lobster Clasp / Barrel Clasp:* Metal mechanical closures attached to the ends of necklaces and bracelets.
  - *Jump Rings:* Small split metal loops used to connect clasps to stringing wires.
  - *Crimp Beads:* Small soft metal cylinders squeezed flat with pliers to firmly anchor wires to clasps.
- **Hand Tools:**
  - *Round-Nose Pliers:* For bending wire into smooth loops and eyelets.
  - *Flat-Nose / Chain-Nose Pliers:* For flattening crimp beads and opening jump rings.
  - *Wire Cutters:* For cutting metal wire cleanly without fraying.
  - *Beading Needles:* Ultra-slim flexible needles that slip through tiny bead perforations.
  - *Bead Board (Velvet Mat):* A grooved flocked tray that prevents round beads from rolling away during layout.

### 5. Practical Step-by-Step Construction of Rolled Paper Beads
Recycled paper bead making is an exceptional eco-friendly craft that transforms waste magazines, old calendars, and wrapping paper into elegant, stone-like jewelry beads:

```
Step 1: Drafting the Templates
Draw long, isosceles triangular strips on colorful magazine paper:
- Base width: 1.5 cm to 2.5 cm (this determines the length of the finished bead).
- Strip length: 25 cm to 30 cm (longer strips yield fatter, rounder beads).

Step 2: Cutting the Strips
Cut cleanly along the marked triangular lines using sharp scissors or a paper cutter.

Step 3: Rolling around the Core
Place the wide base of the triangle against a toothpick, thin skewer, or beading needle.
Roll the paper tightly and evenly around the skewer, keeping the strip centered.

Step 4: Gluing the Tip
When 2 cm of the tapered tip remains, apply a thin dab of PVA paper glue.
Roll the tip down firmly and hold for 10 seconds until sealed.

Step 5: Glazing and Waterproofing
Apply 2 coats of clear wood varnish, clear nail polish, or diluted PVA glue over the bead.
Allow the beads to dry completely while still suspended on the toothpick.

Step 6: Stringing and Finishing
Slide the dried beads off the skewers and thread them onto nylon line, alternating with
small spacer beads, and attach a lobster clasp with crimp beads.
```

## Common Mistakes
1. **Rolling Paper Beads Loosely:** When the initial wraps around the skewer are loose, the bead collapses or slides apart when strung. Always pull paper strips taut from the first turn.
2. **Applying Excessive Liquid Glue:** Flooding paper beads with watery glue dissolves the paper fibers and bleeds ink colors. Apply only a tiny dab of PVA glue at the tip.
3. **Using Blunt, Thick Needles:** Forcing standard household sewing needles into small bead holes will split delicate glass and ceramic beads. Always use thin, flexible beading needles.
4. **Neglecting Crimp Beads on Wire:** Attempting to tie rigid fishing line or tiger tail wire into ordinary granny knots causes necklaces to slip open under tension. Always use crimp beads squashed with flat-nose pliers.
5. **Confusing Bead Material Types:** Mistaking plastic beads for authentic coral or stone beads; coral is cool to the touch and shows natural organic striations, while plastic warms quickly and shows mold seam lines.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Calculating Bead Count for a Choker Necklace
*Problem:* An artisan wants to string a standard 40 cm (400 mm) choker necklace using round Bida glass beads that are 8 mm in diameter each. The metal clasp assembly takes up 24 mm of space. How many glass beads are required to complete the necklace?
- **Step 1: Determine effective bead length:** Total length minus clasp length = $400\text{ mm} - 24\text{ mm} = 376\text{ mm}$.
- **Step 2: Calculate bead count:**
  $$\text{Number of Beads} = \frac{376\text{ mm}}{8\text{ mm/bead}} = 47\text{ beads}$$
- **Conclusion:** The artisan needs exactly 47 glass beads (or 46 beads plus spacers) to complete the 40 cm choker.

### Example 2: Selecting Appropriate Stringing Cord
*Scenario:* A student is designing two items: (A) A stretchy slip-on wristband for active daily school sports, and (B) A heavy multi-strand royal ceremonial collar featuring 12 mm brass and stone beads. Which stringing material is appropriate for each?
- **Item A:** Elastic stretch cord (0.7 mm to 1.0 mm silicone cord) is required because it allows the bracelet to expand over the hand without needing clasps.
- **Item B:** Tiger tail wire (nylon-coated 7-strand steel wire) with crimp beads and a heavy lobster clasp is essential to safely support the heavy weight of brass and stone without breaking or sagging.
