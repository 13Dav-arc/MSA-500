## Hook
When you open a tap or draw water from a deep community borehole, you expect the fluid to be transparent, odorless, and completely refreshing. Yet throughout history, communities drinking from untreated rivers and contaminated shallow wells have suffered devastating outbreaks of cholera, typhoid fever, and dysentery. Water may appear crystal clear to the naked eye while harboring millions of lethal microscopic bacteria and dissolved chemical poisons. Understanding the natural sources of water, its physical and chemical properties, and the rigorous scientific methods used to purify it protects not just your personal health, but the survival of your entire community.

## Key Terms
- Term: Potable Water
  Definition: Water that is safe, clean, and wholesome for human consumption, completely free from disease-causing pathogens, toxic chemicals, and suspended sediments.
- Term: Coagulation
  Definition: The water treatment process where chemical agents like alum (aluminum sulfate) are added to neutralize charges and clump microscopic suspended dirt particles into larger flakes called flocs.
- Term: Sedimentation
  Definition: The physical settling process where heavy suspended particles and chemical flocs sink to the bottom of a storage basin under the force of gravity.
- Term: Chlorination
  Definition: The chemical disinfection process of adding measured amounts of chlorine or chlorine compounds to water to kill pathogenic bacteria and viruses.
- Term: Water Table
  Definition: The upper subterranean boundary of groundwater saturation beneath the earth's surface where soil and rock pores are completely filled with water.

## Core Concept
### 1. Natural and Artificial Sources of Water
Water is the most abundant natural compound on the planet, covering more than 70% of the Earth's surface. In basic science, water resources are grouped into three primary natural categories:
1. **Surface Water:**
   - Includes rivers, streams, lakes, ponds, and oceans.
   - Surface water is easily accessible but is the most vulnerable to bacterial pollution, agricultural runoff (fertilizers and pesticides), and industrial waste.
2. **Groundwater (Subterranean Water):**
   - Rainwater that percolates downward through permeable soil layers and rock crevices until it accumulates above an impermeable rock layer, forming the water table.
   - Accessed through deep boreholes, hand-dug wells, and natural artesian springs.
   - Groundwater is naturally filtered through sand and gravel layers, making it generally cleaner than surface water, though it can still be contaminated if latrines or refuse dumps are sited improperly uphill.
3. **Atmospheric Water (Rainwater):**
   - Fresh water collected during precipitation.
   - In rural, unpolluted areas, rainwater collected in clean vessels is very pure; however, in heavily industrialized cities, it can absorb airborne sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides to form harmful acid rain.

### 2. Physical and Chemical Properties of Pure Water
Pure water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) possesses distinct scientific characteristics that distinguish it from solutions and contaminated samples:
- **Physical Properties:**
  - *Appearance:* Completely colorless, odorless, and tasteless when free of impurities.
  - *Boiling Point:* Boils at exactly $100^\circ\text{C}$ at standard atmospheric pressure ($760\text{ mmHg}$). Dissolved impurities raise the boiling point.
  - *Freezing Point:* Freezes into solid ice at exactly $0^\circ\text{C}$ at standard atmospheric pressure. Dissolved impurities lower the freezing point.
  - *Density:* Has a maximum density of $1.0\text{ g/cm}^3$ (or $1000\text{ kg/m}^3$) at $4^\circ\text{C}$.
  - *Surface Tension:* High surface tension allows small insects to walk across still water.
- **Chemical Properties:**
  - *Neutrality:* Pure distilled water is chemically neutral with a pH value of $7.0$, turning neither red nor blue litmus paper.
  - *Universal Solvent:* Dissolves more substances (salts, sugars, gases, minerals) than any other known liquid due to its polar molecular structure.

### 3. Methods of Water Purification
Water drawn directly from nature is rarely sterile. Purification techniques range from simple domestic measures to advanced municipal engineering:
- **Domestic / Household Purification Methods:**
  - *Boiling:* Bringing water to a vigorous, rolling boil for at least 5 to 10 minutes denatures bacterial enzymes and kills pathogenic microorganisms (viruses, bacteria, and protozoan cysts). It is the most universally accessible household method.
  - *Filtration:* Passing turbid water through porous barriers (such as clean cloth, porcelain ceramic filters, or alternating layers of fine sand, charcoal, and gravel) to trap undissolved dirt, silt, and parasites. Filtration removes turbidity but does not destroy dissolved microbes unless combined with boiling or chlorination.
  - *Chemical Disinfection:* Adding controlled household water-purification tablets (such as sodium hypochlorite / WaterGuard) kills pathogens.
  - *Safe Storage:* Purified water must be stored in a covered, clean vessel with a narrow neck or tap, or drawn using a dedicated clean ladle to prevent hand re-contamination.
- **Municipal Water Works Pipeline (Urban Water Treatment):**
  1. *Screening:* Raw river water passes through metal bars to catch large floating debris (branches, leaves, plastic bags).
  2. *Aeration:* Water is sprayed into air or bubbled with oxygen to expel foul gases (like hydrogen sulfide) and oxidize dissolved iron.
  3. *Coagulation & Flocculation:* Alum (aluminum sulfate) is added and gently stirred; positive ions neutralize negative charges on clay particles, binding them into visible flakes called flocs.
  4. *Sedimentation:* Water rests in vast clarifier tanks where heavy flocs settle to the bottom as sludge.
  5. *Rapid Sand Filtration:* Clarified water trickles down through thick beds of graded sand and gravel, trapping fine residual particles.
  6. *Chlorination:* Precise doses of chlorine gas or liquid are injected to kill all remaining bacteria and maintain residual disinfection throughout the city pipe distribution network.

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Domestic and Municipal Water Purification Methods
- Label: Household Boiling
  Title: Thermal Disinfection
  Body: Raises water to 100°C to reliably destroy biological pathogens, but does not remove suspended solids, mud, or heavy metal chemical pollutants.
- Label: Sand Filtration
  Title: Physical Particulate Removal
  Body: Traps suspended sediment, organic silt, and parasite eggs through porous matrix layers, but cannot kill microscopic bacteria or neutralize dissolved toxins.
- Label: Municipal Chlorination
  Title: Chemical Sterilization
  Body: Injects measured chlorine to eliminate microscopic pathogens and maintains residual protection in pipelines, but requires precise dosing to prevent toxic chemical byproducts.
- Label: Distillation
  Title: Evaporation & Condensation
  Body: Boils water into steam and condenses it into pure liquid, removing all minerals, salts, and microbes, but is too expensive and energy-intensive for community-wide domestic supply.

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Believing that clear, transparent water from a natural mountain stream or hand-dug well is automatically safe to drink without treatment.
  Fact: Pathogenic microorganisms like Salmonella typhi and Vibrio cholerae are completely microscopic and transparent; water clarity has no correlation with biological sterility.
- Mistake: Assuming that freezing contaminated water into ice blocks destroys harmful bacteria and makes it safe for beverages.
  Fact: Freezing merely causes bacteria to become dormant; once ice melts in a drink, pathogens resume normal metabolic activity and transmit infection.
- Mistake: Siting a residential water well or borehole on low-lying ground downhill from a pit latrine or refuse dump.
  Fact: Groundwater flows down the hydraulic gradient; leachate and fecal pathogens seep through porous subterranean rock layers, contaminating down-slope aquifers.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Calculating Daily Household Drinking Water Requirement
Context: The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that a growing secondary school student consume approximately 2.5 litres of clean potable water daily for optimal metabolic function.
Problem: If a boarding school dormitory houses 60 JSS 1 students, how many 20-litre jerrycans of purified water must the school management provision every single day?
Fix: Total daily water requirement = $60 \text{ students} \times 2.5 \text{ litres/student} = 150 \text{ litres}$. Number of 20-litre jerrycans = $150 \div 20 = 7.5 \text{ jerrycans}$. Management must provision at least 8 full 20-litre jerrycans daily.

### Example 2: Determining Latrine Siting Relative to a Domestic Borehole
Context: A compound landlord intends to drill a borehole and construct a new pit latrine on a sloping residential plot of land.
Problem: An uncertified mason advises drilling the borehole 3 metres directly downhill from the newly dug pit latrine to save piping costs.
Fix: This advice violates public health sanitation laws. The borehole must be drilled at least 15 to 30 metres away and positioned strictly uphill (up-gradient) from any pit latrine, septic soakaway, or refuse pit to prevent gravity-driven subterranean leachate from poisoning the drinking aquifer.

### Example 3: Reconstituting a Household Water Disinfection Solution
Context: A family in a rural community draws turbid river water during the rainy season and uses alum for coagulation and household bleach for disinfection.
Problem: The family adds bleach immediately to muddy water without prior sedimentation or filtration.
Fix: High organic turbidity neutralizes chlorine compounds before they can kill pathogens. The family must first add alum to settle the mud, decant or filter the clear supernatant water through a clean cloth, and only then add the measured chlorine disinfectant.

Feedback: Under standard atmospheric pressure (760 mmHg), pure water boils at exactly 100°C; the presence of dissolved impurities elevates this temperature.

Stem: At what temperature does pure water achieve its maximum density of 1.0 g/cm³?
A) 4°C (correct)
B) 0°C

Feedback: Water exhibits anomalous expansion; it reaches its maximum density of 1.0 g/cm³ at 4°C before expanding as it freezes into ice.

### Q15 [true-false]
Statement: Freezing water into ice blocks completely kills all pathogenic bacteria and guarantees the ice is safe for drinking.
Answer: false
Feedback: Freezing does not sterilize water; it merely causes bacteria to become dormant, and they resume infectious activity when the ice melts.

### Q16 [true-false]
Statement: Pure water is referred to as the "universal solvent" because its polar molecular structure allows it to dissolve more chemical substances than any other liquid.
Answer: true
Feedback: Because of its molecular dipole and hydrogen bonding capability, water dissolves a wider variety of solutes than any other liquid.
