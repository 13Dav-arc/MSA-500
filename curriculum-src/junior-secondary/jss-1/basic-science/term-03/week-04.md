## Hook
Have you ever thrown a stone as high into the sky as you possibly could and wondered why it always stops in mid-air and falls right back down to the ground? Now imagine being an astronaut inside a spacecraft where you let go of your pen and, instead of falling, it simply floats in the air beside you! That invisible pull keeping our feet planted on the ground—and the strange floating feeling when you escape its grip in outer space—is the story of gravitation and weightlessness.

## Key Terms
- Term: Gravitation
  Definition: A non-contact force of attraction that acts on a body across a distance, through which the Earth pulls all objects downward toward its centre.
- Term: Weight
  Definition: The measure of the downward gravitational pull exerted on an object by the Earth or another heavenly body.
- Term: Weightlessness
  Definition: A state or condition of not experiencing the pulling effects of gravity, causing objects and astronauts in orbit to float freely.
- Term: Orbit
  Definition: The curved, repeating path that a planet takes around the Sun, or that a moon or satellite takes around a planet, held in place by gravity.
- Term: Universal Force
  Definition: A force that exists everywhere in the universe, acting between the Earth and objects as well as between distant planets and stars.

## Core Concept
### Meaning of Gravitation
**Gravitation** (or gravitational force) is a non-contact force that acts on a body across a distance without any physical touch between the source and the body. The Earth exerts a constant gravitational pull on all objects, drawing them toward its centre. 
- This downward pull is why ripe fruits fall from trees to the ground instead of rising into the sky.
- It is also why a stone thrown upward slows down, stops its upward movement, and falls back to the Earth.

### Factors That Determine Gravitational Pull
Gravity is a **universal force**—it exists between the Earth and the objects on it, as well as between giant planets and stars in space. The strength of the gravitational force pulling on an object depends on three main things:
1. **The mass of the object:** Heavier objects experience a stronger gravitational pull than lighter objects.
2. **The mass of the Earth (or planet):** A larger, more massive planet exerts a stronger pull than a smaller body like the Moon.
3. **The distance between the centre of the object and the centre of the Earth:** The farther an object moves away from the Earth's centre into space, the weaker the pull of Earth's gravity becomes.

### Effects and Importance of Gravitation
Without gravitation, life on Earth would be impossible because everything would float off into space. Gravitation has several vital effects:
- **Gives Objects Weight:** The pull of gravity on an object's mass determines how much it weighs.
- **Keeps Planets in Orbit:** The Sun's strong gravity keeps the Earth and all other planets revolving smoothly in their orbits around the Sun.
- **Holds the Moon in Path:** Earth's gravity keeps the Moon in its steady orbit around the Earth and along its path in the sky.
- **Allows Aeroplanes to Fly:** Aeroplanes fly in the sky by using powerful engines and wings to do work against the downward pull of gravity.
- **Drives Tides and Convection:** Gravitation between the Earth, Moon, and Sun causes the rise and fall of ocean tides, and helps drive convection currents where warm, lighter fluids rise while cooler, denser fluids sink.

### Weightlessness and Gravity on the Moon
**Weightlessness** is the state of not feeling or experiencing the effects of gravity. 
- **Floating in Orbit:** When astronauts travel into orbit in outer space, they experience weightlessness and float inside their spacecraft. Experiencing weightlessness does not mean the astronauts have lost their body mass or truly have zero weight—they simply do not feel the downward sensation of gravity while in orbit.
- **Weight on the Moon:** Because the Moon is much smaller and less massive than the Earth, its gravitational pull is much weaker. The weight of any object on the Moon is exactly **one-sixth ($\frac{1}{6}$)** of its weight on Earth:
  $$\text{Weight on the Moon} = \frac{1}{6} \times \text{Weight on Earth}$$
  For example, if an astronaut weighs $60\text{ N}$ on Earth, that same astronaut will weigh only $10\text{ N}$ on the Moon.

  Body: A stone thrown upward is pulled straight back to the ground by gravity, whereas the Moon travels fast enough sideways that gravity bends its path into a continuous orbit around Earth.

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Believing that an astronaut experiencing weightlessness in space has lost all their body mass and matter.
  Fact: Weightlessness only means the astronaut is not feeling the effect of gravity. Their body still contains the exact same amount of bone, muscle, and mass (in kilograms).
- Mistake: Thinking that gravity only exists on planet Earth and nowhere else in the universe.
  Fact: Gravity is a universal force that exists between all planets, moons, and stars throughout the entire universe.

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Finding an Astronaut's Weight on the Moon
Context: An astronaut wearing a spacesuit steps on a scale on Earth and records a total weight of $60\text{ N}$ before blasting off to the Moon.
Problem: What will the astronaut's weight be when standing on the surface of the Moon?
Fix: The weight of an object on the Moon is one-sixth ($\frac{1}{6}$) of its weight on Earth. Divide the Earth weight by $6$:
$$\text{Weight on Moon} = \frac{60\text{ N}}{6} = 10\text{ N}$$
The astronaut will weigh $10\text{ N}$ on the Moon.

### Example B: Calculating Earth Weight from Moon Weight
Context: A lunar rover (space car) weighs $120\text{ N}$ when measured on the surface of the Moon.
Problem: How much did the lunar rover weigh when it was built in a factory on Earth?
Fix: Since weight on the Moon is $\frac{1}{6}$ of weight on Earth, an object on Earth weighs $6$ times more than it does on the Moon. Multiply the Moon weight by $6$:
$$\text{Weight on Earth} = 120\text{ N} \times 6 = 720\text{ N}$$

### Example C: Explaining Why a Thrown Ball Comes Back Down
Context: During break time, a student kicks a football high into the air. The ball rises quickly, slows down at the top of its path, stops briefly, and falls back to the field.
Problem: Why doesn't the football keep flying upward into outer space forever?
Fix: As the ball rises, the Earth's gravitational force continuously pulls it downward toward the centre of the Earth. This downward pull slows the upward motion until the ball stops and falls back to the ground.
