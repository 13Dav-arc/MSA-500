## Hook
Have you ever played a game of tug-of-war on the school field where both teams pulled on the rope with all their strength, yet the ribbon in the middle did not move an inch? Or have you noticed how much harder it is to carry a heavy bucket of water up a flight of stairs than to walk up empty-handed? When forces are equal and opposite, objects stay still; when one force is stronger—or when you lift a load upward against Earth's gravity—motion happens and real physical work is done.

## Key Terms
- Term: Balanced Forces
  Definition: Equal and opposite forces acting on an object at the same time so that the object remains at rest or does not change its motion.
- Term: Unbalanced Forces
  Definition: Unequal forces acting on an object, causing it to move in the direction of the stronger force.
- Term: Gravitational Force
  Definition: The force of attraction with which the Earth pulls all objects downward toward its centre.
- Term: Weight
  Definition: The downward gravitational force exerted on a body by the Earth, measured in Newtons (N).
- Term: Spring Balance
  Definition: The calibrated laboratory instrument used to measure gravitational force (weight) in Newtons.

## Core Concept
### Balanced and Unbalanced Forces
More than one force usually acts on an object at the same time:
- **Balanced Forces:** When a body is at rest (stationary), all the forces acting upon it are equal in size and opposite in direction. For example, a textbook resting on a classroom desk is pulled downward by gravity while the desk pushes upward with an equal force. Because the forces balance out, the book does not move.
- **Unbalanced Forces:** When the forces acting on a body are not equal, they are called unbalanced forces. The object will start moving or speed up in the exact direction of the stronger (unbalanced) force.

### Measuring and Calculating Gravitational Force (Weight)
Gravitational force (also called the force of gravity) is the pull with which the Earth draws all objects toward its centre. 
- **Instrument:** We measure gravitational force and weight using a **spring balance**.
- **Unit of Measurement:** All forces, including weight, are measured in **Newtons (N)**, named after Sir Isaac Newton, who first discovered the laws of gravity.
- **Acceleration Due to Gravity ($g$):** Near the Earth's surface, the standard value of acceleration due to gravity ($g$) is **$9.8\text{ m/s}^2$** (often rounded to **$10\text{ m/s}^2$** for easy calculation).
- **Formula for Gravitational Force / Weight:**
  $$F = m \times g$$
  Where **$F$** is gravitational force (or weight) in Newtons ($\text{N}$), **$m$** is the mass of the object in kilograms ($\text{kg}$), and **$g$** is acceleration due to gravity ($\text{m/s}^2$).

### Work Done Against Gravity
Whenever you lift an object upward from the ground, you are opposing the downward pull of Earth's gravity and doing physical work. The amount of work done is measured in **Joules (J)** and can be calculated in two ways depending on what information you are given:

1. **When Mass ($m$), Gravity ($g$), and Height ($h$) are given:**
   $$\text{Work Done } (W) = m \times g \times h$$
   - **$m$** = Mass of the object in kilograms ($\text{kg}$)
   - **$g$** = Acceleration due to gravity in $\text{m/s}^2$
   - **$h$** = Vertical height the object is lifted in metres ($\text{m}$)

2. **When Force ($F$) and Distance/Height ($h$) are already given:**
   $$\text{Work Done } (W_d) = \text{Force } (F) \times \text{Height } (h)$$
   - **$F$** = Force applied in Newtons ($\text{N}$)
   - **$h$** = Height or distance moved in metres ($\text{m}$)

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Force States and Gravitational Quantities
- Label: Force Equilibrium
  Title: Balanced vs Unbalanced Forces
  Body: Balanced forces are equal and opposite so a stationary body stays at rest, whereas unbalanced forces are unequal and cause the body to move toward the stronger force.
- Label: Matter vs Pull
  Title: Mass vs Weight
  Body: Mass is the amount of matter in an object measured in kilograms (kg), whereas weight is the downward gravitational pull of the Earth on that object measured in Newtons (N).
- Label: Calculation Formulas
  Title: Gravitational Force vs Work Done
  Body: Gravitational force calculates the downward pull on a mass ($F = mg$ in Newtons), while work done calculates the energy used to lift that mass through a vertical height ($W = mgh$ in Joules).
- Label: Given Parameters
  Title: Using $W = mgh$ vs $W = Fh$
  Body: Use $W = mgh$ when the object's mass is given in kilograms (kg), and use $W = Fh$ when the applied force or weight is already given in Newtons (N).

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Writing the unit of work done as Newtons (N) instead of Joules (J).
  Fact: Force and weight are measured in Newtons (N), but work done against gravity is a measure of energy transferred and must always be written in Joules (J).
- Mistake: Multiplying by gravity ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$) again when the force is already given in Newtons (N).
  Fact: A value in Newtons already includes gravity ($F = mg$); if a question gives force in Newtons, simply multiply the force by the height ($W = F \times h$).

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Calculating Gravitational Force on a Falling Mango
Context: A heavy basket of mango fruits with a total mass of $10\text{ kg}$ is pulled downward with an acceleration of $5\text{ m/s}^2$.
Problem: Calculate the gravitational force acting on the basket.
Fix: Use the gravitational force formula $F = m \times a$ (or $F = mg$). Substitute $m = 10\text{ kg}$ and acceleration $= 5\text{ m/s}^2$:
$$F = 10 \times 5 = 50\text{ Newtons (N)}$$

### Example B: Work Done Lifting a Cement Block
Context: A bricklayer lifts a solid concrete block of mass $30\text{ kg}$ straight up through a vertical height of $3\text{ metres}$. Take acceleration due to gravity $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
Problem: What is the work done by the bricklayer in lifting the block?
Fix: Since mass ($m = 30\text{ kg}$), gravity ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$), and height ($h = 3\text{ m}$) are given, apply the formula $W = mgh$:
$$\text{Work Done} = 30 \times 10 \times 3 = 900\text{ Joules (J)}$$

### Example C: Work Done When Force is Given in Newtons
Context: A student applies an upward force of $50\text{ N}$ to throw a stone straight up across a vertical height of $5\text{ metres}$.
Problem: Calculate the work done in the process.
Fix: Here, the force is already stated in Newtons ($F = 50\text{ N}$) and the height is $h = 5\text{ m}$. Use the direct formula $\text{Work Done} = \text{Force} \times \text{Distance}$ ($W_d = F \times h$):
$$W_d = 50\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = 250\text{ Joules (J)}$$
