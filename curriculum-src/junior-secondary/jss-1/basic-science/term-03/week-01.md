## Hook
Have you ever kicked a stationary football across the school field, helped push a stalled motor car along the street, or watched a ripe mango drop straight from a high branch to the ground? None of these movements happen by magic—they are all caused by an invisible action called force. Whether you are walking on the road without slipping or picking up tiny iron pins with a magnet, forces are working all around you every single second.

## Key Terms
- Term: Force
  Definition: Any push or pull action that makes an object move, stops it, or changes its uniform motion in a given direction.
- Term: Contact Force
  Definition: A force whose source must physically touch the object to which it is applied, either directly or indirectly.
- Term: Force Field (Non-Contact Force)
  Definition: A force that acts on an object across empty space without any physical contact between the source and the object.
- Term: Gravitational Force
  Definition: The non-contact force of attraction with which the Earth pulls all objects downward toward its centre.
- Term: Magnetic Force
  Definition: The force that exists around a magnet, enabling it to attract magnetic materials like iron, steel, pins, and nails.

## Core Concept
### Meaning and Mathematical Expression of Force
In science, a **force** is any action (a push or a pull) that moves an object from rest, stops a moving body, or alters its uniform motion in a given direction. Force can also change the shape or size of an object when squeezed or stretched.
- Mathematically, force is the product of the mass of an object and its acceleration:
  $$\text{Force } (F) = \text{mass } (m) \times \text{acceleration } (a)$$
- The greater the mass of an object, the greater the force needed to move or accelerate it.
- The standard unit for measuring all forces is the **Newton (N)**, named after the famous scientist Sir Isaac Newton.

### Types of Forces
All forces in nature are grouped into two main classes:

#### 1. Contact Forces
Contact forces occur only when two bodies physically touch each other. Contact can be grouped into two forms:
- **Direct Contact Forces:** Examples include **push** (such as pushing a stalled car or opening a door) and **friction** (the rubbing force between car tyres and a tarred road).
- **Indirect Contact Forces:** Examples include **pull** and **tension** (such as pulling a heavy table with a rope or stretching a spring).

#### 2. Non-Contact Forces (Force Fields)
A **force field** is a force whose source does not require physical contact with the object to which it is applied. It acts across empty space between objects that are separated from each other. There are three main examples:
1. **Gravitational Force:** The force with which the Earth pulls all objects downward toward its centre. It gives objects their weight and causes ripe fruits to fall from trees. If a heavy stone and a light feather are dropped in air at the same time, the stone reaches the ground first because air resistance slows the light feather more than the heavier stone.
2. **Magnetic Force:** The pulling or pushing power that exists around a magnet (within its magnetic field). A magnet's field is strongest at its two ends (poles) and attracts objects made of iron or steel, such as nails and pins.
3. **Electric (Electrostatic) Force:** The force that exists between two electric charges. **Unlike charges attract** each other (positive and negative pull together), while **like charges repel** each other (two positive charges or two negative charges push each other away).

### Differences Between Gravitational Force and Magnetic Force
- **Nature of Pull:** Gravitational force is the pull exerted by the Earth on all objects with mass, whereas magnetic force is the property of magnets to attract iron or steel objects.
- **Direction:** Gravitational force always acts straight downward toward the centre of the Earth, while magnetic force acts toward the poles of the magnet.
- **Weight:** Gravitational force gives every object its weight, whereas magnetic force does not give weight to objects.
- **Everyday Effect:** Gravitational force causes fruits to fall from trees to the ground, whereas magnetic force cannot pull fruits down from trees.

### Everyday Uses of Force
- **Moving and Stopping:** Force is required to set stationary objects in motion and allows a moving car to slow down or stop when the brakes are applied.
- **Walking and Road Grip:** Friction between the soles of our shoes and the ground lets us walk and run without slipping, and stops car wheels from skidding on tarred roads.
- **Workshop & Repair Work:** Watch repairers and technicians use magnetic force to pick up tiny iron screws and pins that are too small for fingers.
- **Separating Mixtures:** Magnetic force is used in industry and laboratories to separate magnetic iron dust from non-magnetic substances like sulphur and sand.

  Body: Contact forces require physical touch between bodies (such as pushing a car or friction), whereas non-contact forces act across empty space without touching (such as gravity and magnetism).
- Label: Contact Delivery
  Title: Direct vs Indirect Contact Force
  Body: Direct contact forces happen when a source presses straight against a surface (push and friction), while indirect contact forces act through an connecting link like a rope or cable (pull and tension).
- Label: Force Field Types
  Title: Gravitational vs Magnetic Force
  Body: Gravity pulls all objects with mass downward toward the Earth's centre to give them weight, while magnetic force pulls iron and steel materials toward the poles of a magnet.
- Label: Charge Interaction
  Title: Attraction vs Repulsion in Electric Force
  Body: Unlike electric charges (positive and negative) attract and pull toward each other, whereas like electric charges (positive-positive or negative-negative) repel and push apart.

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Thinking that a force is only acting when an object is touching something else.
  Fact: Non-contact forces (force fields) like gravity and magnetism pull or push objects across empty space without any physical contact.
- Mistake: Believing that magnets attract all shiny metals, including gold, copper, and aluminium.
  Fact: Magnetic force primarily attracts ferromagnetic materials such as iron, steel, pins, and iron nails, leaving non-magnetic materials behind.

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Calculating Force from Mass and Acceleration
Context: A student pushes a toy cart with a mass of $4\text{ kg}$, causing it to accelerate across a smooth classroom floor at $3\text{ m/s}^2$.
Problem: Calculate the amount of force applied to the cart.
Fix: Force is the product of mass and acceleration ($F = m \times a$). Multiply the mass ($4\text{ kg}$) by the acceleration ($3\text{ m/s}^2$):
$$F = 4 \times 3 = 12\text{ Newtons (N)}$$

### Example B: Helping a Technician Find Dropped Screws
Context: A clock repairer accidentally knocks over a small tray, spilling tiny steel screws into a pile of yellow sulphur powder and floor sand.
Problem: Picking out the tiny steel screws one by one with fingers is slow and difficult.
Fix: Pass a bar magnet over the pile. Because steel screws are magnetic and sand/sulphur are non-magnetic, the magnetic force field pulls the screws right onto the magnet ends without touching the sand.

### Example C: Identifying Electric Charge Behaviour
Context: In a science demonstration, two plastic balloons are rubbed with wool so that both carry negative electric charges. When brought near each other, the balloons swing away from one another.
Problem: Why do the two balloons push each other apart instead of sticking together?
Fix: Both balloons carry the exact same type of charge (negative and negative). Under electric force rules, like charges repel each other, creating a repulsive electric force field between them.
