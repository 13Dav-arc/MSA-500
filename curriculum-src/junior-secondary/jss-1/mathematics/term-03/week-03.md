## Hook
If you want to tile a classroom floor, lay fresh carpet in a living room, or fertilize an agricultural plot, knowing the distance around the boundary fence is not enough[cite: 17, 34]. You need to measure the total amount of flat surface space enclosed inside the boundaries[cite: 31, 34]. That internal surface measurement is known as area, and calculating it accurately ensures that builders, farmers, and decorators purchase the exact amount of materials without expensive waste[cite: 31, 34].

## Key Terms
- Term: Area
  Definition: The measure of surface space enclosed within the boundary lines of a closed two-dimensional plane figure, expressed in square units such as $\text{cm}^2$ or $\text{m}^2$[cite: 31, 34].
- Term: Altitude (Height)
  Definition: The perpendicular distance measured at right angles ($90^\circ$) from the base of a geometric figure to its opposite vertex or parallel side[cite: 30, 33].
- Term: Radius
  Definition: The straight-line distance from the centre of a circle to its outer circumference, used in calculating circular area via $\pi r^2$[cite: 8, 29].
- Term: Composite Shape
  Definition: A complex or irregular plane figure formed by combining two or more basic geometric shapes, such as squares, rectangles, and triangles[cite: 34, 35].

## Core Concept
### Area of Circles
The area of a circle represents the measure of flat surface bounded by its circumference[cite: 29, 31]. It is derived using the constant ratio $\pi$ ($\approx \frac{22}{7}$ or $3.142$) and the radius $r$[cite: 18, 29]:
$$\text{Area of a Circle} = \pi r^2$$[cite: 29, 30]
Because the diameter $d$ is twice the radius ($d = 2r \implies r = \frac{d}{2}$), the formula can also be expressed as $\pi \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$[cite: 18, 29].
- When given the area of a circle, the radius is calculated by solving for $r$: $r = \sqrt{\frac{\text{Area}}{\pi}}$[cite: 30].
- For example, if a circular flowerbed has a radius of $3.5\text{ m}$ ($\frac{7}{2}\text{ m}$), its area is $\frac{22}{7} \times \frac{7}{2} \times \frac{7}{2} = 38.5\text{ m}^2$[cite: 30].

### Area of Triangles
Any rectangle can be divided along its diagonal into two congruent right-angled triangles[cite: 30]. Therefore, the area of any triangle is half the area of a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height[cite: 30]:
$$\text{Area of a Triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{b \times h}{2}$$[cite: 30, 31]
- In a right-angled triangle, the base and perpendicular height are the two sides containing the $90^\circ$ angle[cite: 30].
- When the area and base are known, the height is found by rearranging the formula: $h = \frac{2 \times \text{Area}}{\text{base}}$[cite: 30, 31].

### Area of Quadrilaterals
1. **Square:** All four sides are equal ($L$)[cite: 12, 31].
   $$\text{Area of a Square} = L \times L = L^2 \quad \implies \quad \text{Length of side } (L) = \sqrt{\text{Area}}$$[cite: 31]
2. **Rectangle:** Formed by length ($l$) and breadth ($b$)[cite: 21, 32].
   $$\text{Area of a Rectangle} = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} = l \times b$$[cite: 32]
   $$\text{Length} = \frac{\text{Area}}{\text{breadth}}, \quad \text{Breadth} = \frac{\text{Area}}{\text{length}}$$[cite: 32]
3. **Parallelogram:** A slanted quadrilateral where opposite sides are parallel[cite: 13, 33]. Its area equals the product of its base ($b$) and its perpendicular height ($h$)[cite: 32, 33]:
   $$\text{Area of a Parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{height} = b \times h$$[cite: 33]
4. **Trapezium:** A quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides ($a$ and $b$) separated by perpendicular height $h$[cite: 12, 33]. A diagonal splits the trapezium into two triangles of height $h$, giving[cite: 33]:
   $$\text{Area of a Trapezium} = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h$$[cite: 33]

### Area of Composite and Irregular Shapes
Irregular rectilinear shapes cannot be solved with a single standard formula[cite: 34, 35]. To determine their area:
1. Divide the complex shape into familiar regular shapes (such as squares, rectangles, or triangles) by drawing dotted construction lines[cite: 34, 35].
2. Calculate the area of each individual sub-region using the appropriate formula[cite: 35].
3. Sum the areas of all sub-regions to find the total composite area[cite: 35].

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Formulas and Properties Across Plane Figures
- Label: Dimensional Dependency
  Title: Parallelogram vs Rectangle
  Body: Both multiply base by height ($b \times h$), but a rectangle uses its side length as height, whereas a parallelogram requires an internal perpendicular height measured at $90^\circ$ to the base[cite: 32, 33].
- Label: Fractional Multiplier
  Title: Triangle vs Parallelogram
  Body: A triangle is half of a parallelogram on the same base and height, meaning its area includes a half factor ($\frac{1}{2} b h$) while the parallelogram is simply $b \times h$[cite: 30, 33].
- Label: Boundary Curvature
  Title: Circle vs Square
  Body: A circle relies on the ratio $\pi$ multiplied by radius squared ($\pi r^2$)[cite: 29, 30], whereas a square squares its straight linear side ($L^2$)[cite: 31].
- Label: Parallel Base Summation
  Title: Trapezium vs Parallelogram
  Body: A parallelogram has equal opposite bases ($b \times h$)[cite: 13, 33], while a trapezium averages two unequal parallel bases before multiplying by height ($\frac{a + b}{2} \times h$)[cite: 12, 33].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using the slant side instead of the perpendicular height when calculating the area of a parallelogram or triangle[cite: 33].
  Fact: Area calculations require the perpendicular height ($90^\circ$ altitude) dropped directly to the base line, never the slanted edge[cite: 30, 33].
- Mistake: Confusing the formula for circumference ($2\pi r$) with the formula for area ($\pi r^2$)[cite: 17, 29].
  Fact: Circumference is a one-dimensional linear measure ($2\pi r$ in $\text{cm}$), while area measures two-dimensional surface space ($\pi r^2$ in $\text{cm}^2$)[cite: 17, 29].
- Mistake: Forgetting to square the radius in the circle area formula (e.g., computing $\pi \times r \times 2$).
  Fact: $r^2$ means multiplying the radius by itself ($r \times r$), not multiplying by 2[cite: 29, 30].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Finding the Radius from Circular Area
Context: A circular concrete pad has an area of $154\text{ m}^2$[cite: 35].
Problem: Calculate the radius of the pad using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$[cite: 29, 30].
Fix:
$$\text{Area} = \pi r^2 \implies 154 = \frac{22}{7} r^2$$[cite: 29, 30]
Multiply both sides by $7$ and divide by $22$[cite: 30]:
$$r^2 = \frac{154 \times 7}{22} = 7 \times 7 = 49$$[cite: 30]
$$r = \sqrt{49} = 7\text{ m}$$[cite: 30]

### Example B: Determining the Height of a Triangle
Context: A triangular metal bracket has an area of $54\text{ cm}^2$ and a base measuring $12\text{ cm}$[cite: 30].
Problem: Calculate the perpendicular height of the bracket[cite: 30, 31].
Fix:
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times b \times h \implies 54 = \frac{1}{2} \times 12 \times h$$[cite: 30, 31]
$$54 = 6h \implies h = \frac{54}{6} = 9\text{ cm}$$[cite: 30, 31]
The perpendicular height of the bracket is $9\text{ cm}$[cite: 30].

### Example C: Area of a Trapezium
Context: A cross-section of a drainage channel is in the shape of a trapezium with parallel bases measuring $13\text{ cm}$ and $6\text{ cm}$, and a perpendicular depth of $8\text{ cm}$[cite: 33].
Problem: Calculate the cross-sectional area of the channel[cite: 33].
Fix:
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h$$[cite: 33]
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(13 + 6) \times 8 = \frac{1}{2}(19) \times 8 = 19 \times 4 = 76\text{ cm}^2$$[cite: 33]
The cross-sectional area is $76\text{ cm}^2$[cite: 33].
