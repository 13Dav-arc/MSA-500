## Hook
Look around your classroom: the flat chalkboard, the surface of your desk, the floor tiles, and the circular wall clock are all flat boundaries[cite: 7, 8]. Because they can be measured in only two directions—length and breadth—mathematicians call these flat surfaces planes, and the two-dimensional closed figures drawn on them are called plane shapes[cite: 7, 15]. Mastering plane shapes allows engineers, architects, and surveyors to design solid structures, plan football pitches, and map land accurately[cite: 7, 21].

## Key Terms
- Term: Plane Shape
  Definition: A flat, two-dimensional geometric figure enclosed by straight or curved lines that has length and breadth but no thickness[cite: 7, 15].
- Term: Circumference
  Definition: The outside boundary or distance around the edge of a circle[cite: 8, 17].
- Term: Polygon
  Definition: A two-dimensional closed plane figure bounded by three or more straight line segments called sides[cite: 15].
- Term: Line of Symmetry
  Definition: An imaginary line passing through a plane shape that divides it into two identical, matching halves[cite: 8, 12].
- Term: Quadrilateral
  Definition: Any four-sided closed polygon whose interior angles always sum up to $360^\circ$[cite: 12, 14, 15].

## Core Concept
### Identification of Plane Shapes
A flat surface such as a tabletop, whiteboard, wall, or football field is referred to as a plane because it is measured in only two dimensions (length and breadth)[cite: 7]. Plane figures are classified into[cite: 7]:
- **Regular Shapes:** Shapes with definite lengths, breadths, and standard geometric properties (e.g., squares, rectangles, circles, triangles)[cite: 7].
- **Irregular Shapes:** Shapes that do not have definite standard lengths and breadths (e.g., leaves, stones)[cite: 7].

### Circles and Their Parts
A circle is a plane shape traced out such that every point on its boundary is equidistant from a fixed centre point[cite: 7, 8]. Key parts of a circle include:
- **Circumference:** The entire curved outer boundary of the circle[cite: 8].
- **Radius ($r$):** The straight line joining the centre of the circle to any point on its circumference[cite: 8].
- **Diameter ($d$):** A straight line passing through the centre that joins two opposite points on the circumference ($d = 2r$), dividing the circle into two equal semi-circles[cite: 8, 18, 29].
- **Chord:** A straight line connecting any two points on the circumference without necessarily passing through the centre, dividing the circle into two segments[cite: 8].
- **Arc:** A portion or curved section of the circumference[cite: 8].
- **Sector:** The interior region bounded by two radii and an arc (like a slice of pie)[cite: 8].
- **Segment:** The region bounded by a chord and an arc[cite: 8].
- **Lines of Symmetry:** A circle has an infinite number of lines of symmetry passing through its centre[cite: 8].

### Triangles and Their Classifications
A triangle is a closed plane figure made of three line segments and three angles[cite: 8, 9]. Basic properties include:
- The sum of interior angles in any triangle is always $180^\circ$ (the angle-sum property)[cite: 9].
- The sum of the lengths of any two sides is always greater than the third side[cite: 9].
- The side opposite the largest angle is the longest side (e.g., the hypotenuse in a right-angled triangle)[cite: 9, 11].

**Classification Based on Sides[cite: 9, 10, 11]:**
1. **Equilateral Triangle:** All three sides are equal, all three angles equal $60^\circ$, and it has 3 lines of symmetry[cite: 9].
2. **Isosceles Triangle:** Two sides are equal in length, the two angles opposite the equal sides are equal, and it has 1 line of symmetry[cite: 9, 10].
3. **Scalene Triangle:** All three sides have different lengths, all angles are different, and it has no line of symmetry[cite: 10].

**Classification Based on Angles[cite: 10, 11]:**
1. **Acute-angled Triangle:** All three interior angles are acute (less than $90^\circ$)[cite: 10].
2. **Obtuse-angled Triangle:** Exactly one interior angle is obtuse (greater than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$)[cite: 10].
3. **Right-angled Triangle:** Exactly one interior angle is a right angle ($90^\circ$)[cite: 10, 11].

### Properties of Common Quadrilaterals
Quadrilaterals are four-sided plane shapes whose interior angles add up to $360^\circ$[cite: 12, 14, 15]:
- **Square:** All 4 sides are equal, all 4 angles are $90^\circ$, opposite sides are parallel, diagonals are equal and bisect each other at right angles ($90^\circ$), with 4 lines of symmetry[cite: 12].
- **Rectangle:** Opposite sides are equal and parallel, all 4 angles are $90^\circ$, diagonals are equal and bisect each other, with 2 lines of symmetry[cite: 12].
- **Parallelogram:** Opposite sides are equal and parallel, opposite angles are equal, diagonals bisect each other, with rotational symmetry of order 2 and no line of symmetry[cite: 13].
- **Rhombus:** A parallelogram with all 4 sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisect each other at right angles ($90^\circ$), with 2 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 2[cite: 13].
- **Trapezium:** Has exactly one pair of opposite sides parallel while the other two sides are not, with no line of symmetry (unless isosceles)[cite: 12, 14].
- **Kite:** Has two pairs of adjacent sides of equal length, diagonals cross at right angles ($90^\circ$), and it has 1 line of symmetry[cite: 13].

### Polygons
Polygons are named according to their number of straight sides[cite: 15]:
- $3\text{ sides}$: Triangle[cite: 15]
- $4\text{ sides}$: Quadrilateral[cite: 15]
- $5\text{ sides}$: Pentagon[cite: 15]
- $6\text{ sides}$: Hexagon[cite: 15]
- $7\text{ sides}$: Heptagon[cite: 15]
- $8\text{ sides}$: Octagon[cite: 15]
- $9\text{ sides}$: Nonagon[cite: 15]
- $10\text{ sides}$: Decagon[cite: 15]
- $20\text{ sides}$: Icosagon[cite: 16]

  Body: A square has 4 lines of symmetry, a rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry, while a general parallelogram has no line of symmetry[cite: 12, 13].

  Title: Rhombus vs Rectangle Diagonals
  Body: The diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles ($90^\circ$)[cite: 13], whereas the diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other without meeting at right angles[cite: 12].
- Label: Side Congruence
  Title: Rhombus vs Square
  Body: Both have four equal sides, but all four angles of a square are right angles ($90^\circ$)[cite: 12], whereas a rhombus has slanted oblique angles where only opposite angles are equal[cite: 13].
- Label: Parallel Pairs
  Title: Parallelogram vs Trapezium
  Body: A parallelogram has two pairs of opposite parallel sides[cite: 13], while a trapezium has only one pair of opposite parallel sides[cite: 12, 14].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Assuming that a general parallelogram has two lines of symmetry.
  Fact: A standard parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2, but it has no line of symmetry[cite: 13].
- Mistake: Confusing the sector of a circle with a segment.
  Fact: A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc (slice shape), whereas a segment is bounded by a chord and an arc[cite: 8].
- Mistake: Thinking that all quadrilaterals have equal diagonals.
  Fact: Only squares, rectangles, and isosceles trapeziums have diagonals of equal length; rhombuses, kites, and standard parallelograms have diagonals of different lengths[cite: 12, 13].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Angle-Sum Property in a Triangle
Context: An isosceles triangle $ABC$ has two equal base angles measuring $55^\circ$ each[cite: 10].
Problem: Calculate the size of the third vertex angle[cite: 9, 10].
Fix: The sum of interior angles in any triangle is $180^\circ$[cite: 9].
$$\text{Vertex angle} = 180^\circ - (55^\circ + 55^\circ) = 180^\circ - 110^\circ = 70^\circ$$[cite: 10]

### Example B: Determining Circle Dimensions
Context: The radius of a circular water tank cover is $7\text{ cm}$[cite: 8, 18].
Problem: State the diameter of the tank cover and explain its relationship to the radius[cite: 8, 18].
Fix: The diameter is the full straight distance through the centre and is twice the radius ($d = 2r$)[cite: 8, 18, 29]:
$$d = 2 \times 7\text{ cm} = 14\text{ cm}$$[cite: 18]

### Example C: Identifying Polygon and Symmetry Properties
Context: A student identifies a regular polygon with 8 equal sides and equal angles[cite: 15, 16].
Problem: Name the polygon and state how many lines of symmetry it has[cite: 15, 16].
Fix: An eight-sided polygon is an **octagon**[cite: 15]. Because it is a regular polygon with 8 equal sides, it has exactly 8 lines of symmetry[cite: 15, 16].
