## Core Concept
### 1. Plane Shapes, Perimeter, and Area (Weeks 01–03)
- **Circles:** A circle has an infinite number of lines of symmetry[cite: 8]. Its perimeter is its circumference ($C = 2\pi r = \pi d$)[cite: 8, 17], and its surface area is $A = \pi r^2$[cite: 29, 30].
- **Triangles:** Sum of interior angles is $180^\circ$[cite: 9]. Perimeter is $P = a + b + c$ (for equilateral, $P = 3s$)[cite: 18, 19], and area is $A = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$[cite: 30, 31].
- **Quadrilaterals:** Sum of interior angles is $360^\circ$[cite: 12].
  - Square: $P = 4l$, $A = l^2$, with 4 lines of symmetry[cite: 12, 20, 31].
  - Rectangle: $P = 2(l + b)$, $A = l \times b$, with 2 lines of symmetry[cite: 12, 21, 32].
  - Parallelogram: $P = 2(x + y)$, $A = \text{base} \times \text{height}$, with rotational symmetry of order 2 and no lines of symmetry[cite: 13, 23, 33].
  - Trapezium: $A = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h$[cite: 33].
  - Rhombus: 4 equal sides, diagonals bisect each other at $90^\circ$[cite: 13].

### 2. Three-Dimensional Shapes, Nets, and Volume (Week 04)
- **Prisms:** Solids with uniform cross-sections[cite: 41]. A cube has 6 congruent square faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices, and volume $V = s^3$[cite: 42, 49]. A cuboid has 6 rectangular faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices, and volume $V = l \times b \times h$[cite: 42, 50].
- **Curved Solids:** Cylinders have 2 circular flat faces, 1 curved surface, 2 circular edges, and 0 vertices[cite: 43]. Cones have 1 circular base, 1 curved surface, 1 circular edge, and 1 apex vertex[cite: 44]. Spheres have 1 continuous curved surface with 0 edges and 0 vertices[cite: 45].
- **Nets:** 2D polygon layouts that fold into 3D closed solids[cite: 46].

### 3. Geometric Constructions and Angle Relationships (Weeks 06–07)
- **Constructions:** Erecting perpendicular lines and bisecting line segments using compass arcs greater than half the segment length[cite: 60, 62, 63]. Constructing $90^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ angles, and bisecting them to form $45^\circ$, $30^\circ$, and $15^\circ$ angles[cite: 63, 64, 67].
- **Angle Theorems:**
  - Vertically opposite angles are equal[cite: 72, 73].
  - Adjacent angles on a straight line sum to $180^\circ$[cite: 73, 75].
  - Angles meeting around a point sum to $360^\circ$[cite: 74, 75].
  - Complementary angles sum to $90^\circ$; supplementary angles sum to $180^\circ$[cite: 69, 72].
- **Parallel Lines & Transversals:** Alternate angles are equal (Z-shape)[cite: 72, 73], corresponding angles are equal (F-shape)[cite: 72, 74], and co-interior angles sum to $180^\circ$ (C-shape)[cite: 72].

### 4. Statistics and Averages (Week 08)
- **Presentation:** Organizing data into rank-ordered lists, frequency tables with tally bundles of five, pictograms, bar charts, and circular pie charts ($360^\circ$)[cite: 89, 90, 91, 92].
- **Averages:**
  - Mean ($\bar{x}$): Sum of all values divided by count $n$ ($\frac{\sum x}{n}$ or $\frac{\sum fx}{\sum f}$)[cite: 101].
  - Median: The middle number when values are arranged in ascending or descending order (or the average of the two middle numbers for an even count)[cite: 102].
  - Mode: The value or category that appears most frequently[cite: 103].

  Body: Plane shapes possess length and breadth enclosing flat surface area ($\text{cm}^2$)[cite: 7, 31], while 3D solids possess length, breadth, and height enclosing internal volume space ($\text{cm}^3$)[cite: 41, 49].

  Title: Triangles vs Quadrilaterals
  Body: The interior angles of any triangle sum to $180^\circ$ (two right angles)[cite: 9, 76], while the interior angles of any quadrilateral sum to $360^\circ$ (four right angles)[cite: 12, 14].
- Label: Transversal Behavior
  Title: Alternate vs Co-Interior Angles
  Body: Alternate angles lie on opposite sides of a transversal and are equal[cite: 72, 73], whereas co-interior angles lie on the same side and are supplementary ($180^\circ$)[cite: 72].
- Label: Statistical Sensitivity
  Title: Mean vs Median
  Body: The mean is calculated from every numerical value and is vulnerable to extreme outliers[cite: 101, 103], whereas the median depends strictly on central position and resists outlier distortion[cite: 102, 106, 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using linear or square units for solid capacity instead of cubic units[cite: 49].
  Fact: Volume measures three-dimensional space and must always be expressed in cubic units ($\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$)[cite: 49].
- Mistake: Altering the compass radius between striking arcs when bisecting a line segment or angle[cite: 62].
  Fact: The compass radius must remain fixed from both reference endpoints to ensure a true midpoint and symmetrical bisection[cite: 62].
- Mistake: Selecting the middle number from an unsorted list of numbers when finding the median[cite: 102].
  Fact: You must arrange the numbers in ascending or descending rank order before identifying the middle value[cite: 89, 102].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Comprehensive Mensuration Problem
Context: A circular metal plate has a diameter of $14\text{ cm}$[cite: 18, 35].
Problem: Calculate (i) its circumference and (ii) its area using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$[cite: 18, 29, 30].
Fix:
- Radius $r = \frac{d}{2} = \frac{14}{2} = 7\text{ cm}$[cite: 18, 29].
- Circumference: $C = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 44\text{ cm}$[cite: 17, 18].
- Area: $A = \pi r^2 = \frac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7 = 154\text{ cm}^2$[cite: 29, 30].

### Example B: Angle Calculation on Intersecting Lines
Context: In a geometric layout, two straight lines intersect at point $O$[cite: 72, 73]. One of the acute angles formed is $42^\circ$[cite: 77].
Problem: State the size of its vertically opposite angle, and determine the size of the adjacent supplementary angle on the straight line[cite: 69, 72, 73].
Fix:
- Vertically opposite angles are equal, so the opposite angle measures $42^\circ$[cite: 72, 73].
- Adjacent angles on a straight line sum to $180^\circ$[cite: 73, 75]:
  $$\text{Adjacent angle} = 180^\circ - 42^\circ = 138^\circ$$[cite: 73, 75]

### Example C: Statistical Analysis of a Dataset
Context: A student scored the following marks across seven short tests: $12, 15, 11, 15, 18, 14,$ and $13$[cite: 101, 103].
Problem: Determine the mode, median, and mean mark[cite: 101, 102, 103].
Fix:
- **Mode:** The number $15$ appears twice, while all other numbers appear once. $\text{Mode} = 15$[cite: 103].
- **Median:** Arrange the 7 numbers in ascending order: $11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 15, 18$[cite: 102]. The 4th number is the exact middle: $\text{Median} = 14$[cite: 102].
- **Mean:** Add all marks and divide by 7[cite: 101]:
  $$\text{Mean} = \frac{11 + 12 + 13 + 14 + 15 + 15 + 18}{7} = \frac{98}{7} = 14$$[cite: 101]
