## Hook
When an aircraft pilot adjusts a flight heading by a few degrees, an architect designs a slanting roof to shed tropical rainwater, or a carpenter bevels the joint of a picture frame, they are all working with angles[cite: 68, 70]. An angle measures the amount of turn or rotation between two intersecting straight lines meeting at a point[cite: 72]. Understanding angle types and their geometric relationships allows us to calculate unknown corners and ensure structures are balanced and stable[cite: 68, 72].

## Key Terms
- Term: Angle
  Definition: The amount of turn or rotation between two straight lines (called arms) that meet at a common point called the vertex[cite: 72].
- Term: Transversal
  Definition: A straight line that intersects or cuts across two or more parallel lines[cite: 72].
- Term: Supplementary Angles
  Definition: Two angles whose measures add up to exactly $180^\circ$ (two right angles)[cite: 69, 72].
- Term: Complementary Angles
  Definition: Two angles whose measures add up to exactly $90^\circ$ (one right angle)[cite: 69, 72].
- Term: Vertically Opposite Angles
  Definition: The pairs of opposite angles formed when two straight lines intersect, which are always equal to each other[cite: 72, 73].

## Core Concept
### Naming and Classification of Angles
As the amount of rotation between two lines increases, the name and classification of the angle change[cite: 69]:
1. **Acute Angle:** An angle whose measure is strictly less than $90^\circ$[cite: 69].
2. **Right Angle:** An angle that measures exactly $90^\circ$[cite: 69].
3. **Obtuse Angle:** An angle that is greater than $90^\circ$ but less than $180^\circ$[cite: 69].
4. **Straight Angle:** An angle that measures exactly $180^\circ$[cite: 69].
5. **Reflex Angle:** An angle that is greater than $180^\circ$ but less than $360^\circ$[cite: 69, 75].
6. **Full Rotation (Angle at a Point):** An angle representing a complete turn that measures exactly $360^\circ$[cite: 69, 74, 75].

### Angle Pairs: Complementary and Supplementary
- **Complementary Angles:** Two angles are complementary when their sum is $90^\circ$[cite: 69, 72]. For example, $60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$, or $5^\circ$ and $85^\circ$[cite: 70].
- **Supplementary Angles:** Two angles are supplementary when their sum is $180^\circ$[cite: 69, 72]. For example, $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$, or $93^\circ$ and $87^\circ$[cite: 69].

### Measuring Angles with a Protractor
A protractor is a geometric tool used to measure and construct angles in degrees ($^\circ$)[cite: 70]. It typically features two circular scales[cite: 70]:
- An **inner scale** that reads anti-clockwise rotation[cite: 70].
- An **outer scale** that reads clockwise rotation[cite: 70].
To measure an angle $A\hat{O}B$, place the centre point of the protractor directly over the vertex $O$ such that the baseline aligns with arm $OA$, then read the degrees where arm $OB$ passes through the scale starting from zero[cite: 70, 71].

### Angle Theorems and Intersecting Lines
When straight lines intersect, distinct geometric relationships emerge:
- **Vertically Opposite Angles:** When two lines intersect at a point $O$, the angles opposite each other are equal[cite: 72, 73]. For intersecting lines forming angles $X\hat{O}V$ and $U\hat{O}Y$, $X\hat{O}V = U\hat{O}Y$[cite: 73].
- **Adjacent Angles on a Straight Line:** When a straight line meets or stands on another straight line, the adjacent angles formed add up to $180^\circ$ ($a + b = 180^\circ$)[cite: 73, 75].
- **Angles at a Point:** The sum of all angles meeting around a common point is always $360^\circ$ ($p + q + r + s = 360^\circ$)[cite: 74, 75].
- **Sum of Angles in a Triangle:** The interior angles of any triangle always sum to $180^\circ$ ($a + b + c = 180^\circ$)[cite: 9, 76].

### Angles Between Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal
When a transversal intersects a pair of parallel lines, three key principles apply[cite: 72]:
1. **Corresponding Angles:** Angles that occupy the same relative position at each intersection (forming an F-shape) are equal[cite: 72, 74].
2. **Alternate Angles:** Angles that lie on opposite sides of the transversal between the parallel lines (forming a Z-shape) are equal[cite: 72, 73, 74].
3. **Co-Interior (Allied) Angles:** Angles that lie on the same side of the transversal between the parallel lines (forming a C- or U-shape) are supplementary and add up to $180^\circ$[cite: 72].

  Body: Complementary angles sum to one right angle ($90^\circ$)[cite: 69, 72], whereas supplementary angles sum to two right angles or a straight line ($180^\circ$)[cite: 69, 72].
- Label: Transversal Configurations
  Title: Alternate vs Corresponding Angles
  Body: Alternate angles lie on opposite sides of the transversal in a Z-configuration and are equal[cite: 73, 74], while corresponding angles lie on the same side in matching positions in an F-configuration and are equal[cite: 74].
- Label: Co-Interior Contrast
  Title: Alternate vs Co-Interior Angles
  Body: Alternate angles are equal to each other[cite: 72, 73], whereas co-interior (allied) angles on the same side of the transversal are supplementary and add up to $180^\circ$[cite: 72].
- Label: Intersection Scope
  Title: Straight Line vs Point Rotation
  Body: Angles on a straight line add up to $180^\circ$[cite: 73, 75], while angles meeting completely around a single point sum to a full rotation of $360^\circ$[cite: 69, 74, 75].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Reading the wrong scale on a protractor (e.g., recording an obtuse angle of $140^\circ$ as an acute angle of $40^\circ$)[cite: 70, 71].
  Fact: Always check whether the angle being measured is acute ($<90^\circ$) or obtuse ($>90^\circ$) before recording the value, and start counting from the $0^\circ$ mark that rests along the baseline[cite: 69, 71].
- Mistake: Assuming that alternate and corresponding angles are equal even when lines are not parallel[cite: 72].
  Fact: Alternate and corresponding angles are only equal when the transversal intersects lines that are strictly parallel[cite: 72].
- Mistake: Believing that complementary or supplementary angles must always share a common vertex or side[cite: 69].
  Fact: Two angles do not need to be adjacent; they are complementary or supplementary as long as their numerical measures add up to $90^\circ$ or $180^\circ$ respectively[cite: 69].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Finding Adjacent Angles on a Straight Line
Context: A straight line $AB$ has a ray standing on it such that one of the adjacent angles is $35^\circ$ and the other is marked $P$[cite: 75].
Problem: Calculate the value of the unknown angle $P$[cite: 75].
Fix: Adjacent angles on a straight line sum to $180^\circ$[cite: 73, 75]:
$$35^\circ + P = 180^\circ$$[cite: 75]
$$P = 180^\circ - 35^\circ = 145^\circ$$[cite: 75]
The value of angle $P$ is $145^\circ$[cite: 75].

### Example B: Calculating the Complement and Supplement of an Angle
Context: An acute angle measures $63^\circ$[cite: 77].
Problem: Determine (i) its complement and (ii) its supplement[cite: 69, 77].
Fix:
- (i) Complementary angles sum to $90^\circ$[cite: 69]:
  $$\text{Complement} = 90^\circ - 63^\circ = 27^\circ$$[cite: 69, 77]
- (ii) Supplementary angles sum to $180^\circ$[cite: 69]:
  $$\text{Supplement} = 180^\circ - 63^\circ = 117^\circ$$[cite: 69, 77]

### Example C: Calculating an Unknown Angle in a Triangle
Context: A triangle has two known interior angles measuring $64^\circ$ and $88^\circ$, with an unknown third angle marked $x$[cite: 76].
Problem: Calculate the value of angle $x$[cite: 76].
Fix: The sum of interior angles in any triangle is $180^\circ$[cite: 9, 76]:
$$x + 64^\circ + 88^\circ = 180^\circ$$[cite: 76]
$$x + 152^\circ = 180^\circ$$[cite: 76]
$$x = 180^\circ - 152^\circ = 28^\circ$$[cite: 76]
The size of angle $x$ is $28^\circ$[cite: 76].
