## Hook
Have you ever unpacked a brand-new pair of shoes, unfolded a cardboard carton into a flat sheet, or wondered how much water a rectangular overhead tank can store[cite: 41, 46, 50]? Unlike flat paper drawings, real-world objects like tins of milk, dice, chalk boxes, and footballs occupy physical space and possess length, width, and height[cite: 41, 42, 45, 48]. These solid figures are called three-dimensional (3D) shapes, and understanding their properties, flat patterns (nets), and volumes is the basis of manufacturing, packaging, and structural architecture[cite: 41, 46, 49].

## Key Terms
- Term: Three-Dimensional (3D) Solid
  Definition: Any geometric figure that occupies physical space and has three measurable dimensions: length, width (breadth), and height (depth)[cite: 41, 48, 49].
- Term: Face
  Definition: Any flat or curved surface that forms part of the exterior boundary of a solid geometric shape[cite: 42, 43].
- Term: Edge
  Definition: The straight or curved line segment formed where two faces of a solid meet[cite: 42, 43].
- Term: Vertex
  Definition: A corner point where three or more edges intersect in a solid figure (plural: vertices)[cite: 42].
- Term: Net of a Solid
  Definition: A flat, two-dimensional pattern of joined polygons that can be folded along its edges to form a three-dimensional closed solid[cite: 46].

## Core Concept
### Identification and Properties of Prisms
A solid figure is anything that occupies space and has a definite shape[cite: 41]. Geometric solids with uniform cross-sections along their length are called **prisms**[cite: 41, 42]:
- A prism has identical, parallel base and top faces, with side faces whose opposite edges are equal and parallel[cite: 41].
- For any prism with an $n$-sided base, it has $n + 2$ faces, $3n$ edges, and $2n$ vertices[cite: 42].

**Common Prisms[cite: 41, 42, 43]:**
1. **Cube:** A special regular cuboid where all edges are equal in length[cite: 42]. It has $6$ congruent square faces, $12$ edges, and $8$ vertices[cite: 42]. Examples include dice, sugar cubes, and seasoning cubes[cite: 42].
2. **Cuboid (Rectangular Prism):** A solid bounded by $6$ rectangular faces where opposite faces are identical and parallel[cite: 42]. It has $12$ edges and $8$ vertices[cite: 42]. Examples include brick blocks, books, desks, and matchboxes[cite: 42, 51].
3. **Cylinder:** A circular prism with two parallel circular flat faces and one curved surface connecting them[cite: 43]. It has $3$ faces, $2$ circular edges, and $0$ vertices[cite: 43]. Examples include milk tins and water drums[cite: 43].

### Properties of Pyramids, Cones, and Spheres
- **Pyramid:** A solid figure whose base is a polygon and whose triangular lateral faces converge to a single common point called the apex or vertex[cite: 44]. A pyramid is named after the shape of its base (e.g., a square-based pyramid has $5$ faces, $8$ edges, and $5$ vertices; a triangular-based pyramid or tetrahedron has $4$ faces, $6$ edges, and $4$ vertices)[cite: 41, 44, 45].
- **Cone:** A solid with a circular flat base and a curved lateral surface that tapers to a single pointed vertex (apex)[cite: 44]. A cone has $2$ faces (one flat circular, one curved), $1$ circular edge, and $1$ vertex[cite: 44]. Examples include sharpened pencil tips, funnels, and ice-cream cones[cite: 44].
- **Sphere:** A perfectly round three-dimensional solid in which every point on its surface is equidistant from its fixed centre[cite: 45, 48]. A sphere has $1$ continuous curved surface, $0$ edges, and $0$ vertices[cite: 45]. Examples include tennis balls and globe models[cite: 45].

### Nets of Solids
The net of a solid is a flat 2D layout that folds into the 3D shape[cite: 46]:
- **Cube Net:** Consists of $6$ congruent squares arranged so that, when folded, opposite faces meet without overlapping[cite: 42, 46].
- **Cuboid Net:** Made of $3$ matching pairs of rectangles representing the top/base, front/back, and two side faces[cite: 42, 46].
- **Cylinder Net:** Formed by one central rectangle (which rolls into the curved body of height $h$ and length $2\pi r$) flanked by two identical circular bases[cite: 46].
- **Cone Net:** Consists of a sector of a circle (curved slant surface) attached to one full circular base[cite: 46, 48].
- **Pyramid Net:** A central polygonal base surrounded by hinged triangular faces[cite: 46, 47].

### Volume of Cubes and Cuboids
Volume is the measure of internal three-dimensional space occupied by a solid body, measured in cubic units such as $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$[cite: 49].
- **Volume of a Cube:** If the edge of a cube is $s$[cite: 49]:
  $$\text{Volume} = \text{side} \times \text{side} \times \text{side} = s^3$$[cite: 49]
  $$\text{Edge length } (s) = \sqrt[3]{\text{Volume}}$$[cite: 49]
- **Volume of a Cuboid:** Given length ($l$), width/breadth ($b$), and height ($h$)[cite: 50]:
  $$\text{Volume} = l \times b \times h = \text{Base Area} \times \text{height}$$[cite: 50]
  $$\text{Height } (h) = \frac{\text{Volume}}{l \times b} = \frac{\text{Volume}}{\text{Base Area}}$$[cite: 50, 51]

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Structural Properties of Core Three-Dimensional Solids
- Label: Prisms vs Pyramids
  Title: Uniform Cross-Section vs Converging Apex
  Body: Prisms have two congruent parallel bases connected by rectangular lateral faces[cite: 41, 48], whereas pyramids have a single polygonal base whose triangular faces taper upward to meet at a single apex[cite: 44, 48].
- Label: Vertex Count
  Title: Cylinder vs Cone
  Body: A cylinder has two circular edges and zero vertices[cite: 43], while a cone tapers to a single pointed vertex at its top[cite: 44].
- Label: Face Geometry
  Title: Cube vs Cuboid
  Body: A cube is bounded by 6 identical square faces with all edges equal[cite: 42], while a cuboid is bounded by 6 rectangular faces where opposite faces are identical in pairs[cite: 42].
- Label: Curvature
  Title: Polyhedra vs Curved Solids
  Body: Cubes and cuboids have completely flat planar faces[cite: 42], whereas cylinders, cones, and spheres incorporate smooth curved surfaces[cite: 43, 44, 45].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Stating that a cylinder has vertices where the circular faces meet the curved body[cite: 43].
  Fact: A vertex requires three or more straight intersecting edges; a cylinder has 2 circular edges and 0 vertices[cite: 42, 43].
- Mistake: Using square units ($\text{cm}^2$) instead of cubic units ($\text{cm}^3$) when stating volume[cite: 49].
  Fact: Area measures two-dimensional flat surface in $\text{cm}^2$, while volume measures three-dimensional space in $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$[cite: 31, 49].
- Mistake: Confusing the slant height of a cone or pyramid with its vertical perpendicular height in calculations[cite: 46, 56].
  Fact: The perpendicular height is the vertical distance measured at $90^\circ$ from the base to the apex, whereas the slant height runs along the inclined outer slope[cite: 46, 56].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Volume and Edge of a Cube
Context: A sugar manufacturer packages cubic sugar cubes with an edge length of $4\text{ cm}$[cite: 42, 49].
Problem: Calculate the volume of one cube, and find the edge of a larger container whose total volume is $27\text{ cm}^3$[cite: 49].
Fix:
- For edge $s = 4\text{ cm}$[cite: 49]:
  $$\text{Volume} = s^3 = 4\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} = 64\text{ cm}^3$$[cite: 49]
- For a volume of $27\text{ cm}^3$, find the cube root[cite: 49]:
  $$s = \sqrt[3]{27\text{ cm}^3} = \sqrt[3]{3 \times 3 \times 3} = 3\text{ cm}$$[cite: 49]

### Example B: Volume of a Cuboid with a Square Base
Context: A storage box has a square base of side $9\text{ cm}$ and is $10\text{ cm}$ deep[cite: 50, 51].
Problem: Calculate the total internal capacity (volume) of the box[cite: 50, 51].
Fix:
- Calculate base area: $\text{Base Area} = 9\text{ cm} \times 9\text{ cm} = 81\text{ cm}^2$[cite: 50].
- Calculate volume using $\text{Base Area} \times \text{height}$[cite: 50]:
  $$\text{Volume} = 81\text{ cm}^2 \times 10\text{ cm} = 810\text{ cm}^3$$[cite: 51]

### Example C: Determining Missing Height of a Cuboid
Context: A rectangular water tank has a length of $12\text{ cm}$, a width of $8\text{ cm}$, and a total volume of $624\text{ cm}^3$[cite: 51].
Problem: Determine the vertical height of the tank[cite: 51].
Fix:
$$\text{Volume} = l \times w \times h \implies 624 = 12 \times 8 \times h$$[cite: 51]
$$624 = 96h$$[cite: 51]
$$h = \frac{624}{96} = 6.5\text{ cm}$$[cite: 51]
The height of the cuboid is $6.5\text{ cm}$[cite: 51].
