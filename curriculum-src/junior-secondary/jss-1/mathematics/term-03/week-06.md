## Hook
Have you ever wondered how architects and civil engineers ensure that the walls of a skyscraper stand perfectly upright at $90^\circ$ without leaning, or how dual-carriage highways maintain the exact same distance apart for hundreds of kilometres[cite: 57, 60]? In technical drawing and architecture, guessing or drawing freehand is not allowed[cite: 57]. Geometric construction teaches you how to create razor-sharp lines, exact right angles, and balanced bisectors using only a pencil, a straightedge ruler, and a pair of compasses[cite: 57, 59, 62].

## Key Terms
- Term: Geometric Construction
  Definition: The accurate drawing of geometric figures, lines, and angles using only specialized drawing instruments such as a ruler, pencil, set-square, and compasses[cite: 57].
- Term: Parallel Lines
  Definition: Straight lines in the same plane that never meet or intersect, no matter how far they are extended, remaining equidistant from each other[cite: 57].
- Term: Perpendicular Lines
  Definition: Two intersecting straight lines that meet or cross each other at an exact right angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 60].
- Term: Bisection
  Definition: The geometric process of dividing a line segment or an angle into two exactly equal halves[cite: 62].
- Term: Perpendicular Bisector
  Definition: A straight line that cuts across a given line segment at its exact midpoint at an angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 62, 63].

## Core Concept
### Instruments and Principles of Construction
To construct a figure in geometry implies drawing it accurately using standard measuring and drawing instruments: ruler, pencil, set-squares, and compasses[cite: 57]. Before beginning any construction, it is good practice to make a rough sketch of the expected figure[cite: 57].

### Construction of Parallel Lines
Parallel lines lie in the same direction and remain the same distance apart without meeting[cite: 57]. There are two primary methods to construct parallel lines:
1. **Using a Ruler and Set-Square (Sliding Method):** Align one edge of a set-square along the given line $MN$[cite: 58]. Place a straight ruler firmly along another edge of the set-square[cite: 58]. Slide the set-square along the ruler until its edge reaches the desired point $O$, then draw the line with a sharp pencil[cite: 58].
2. **Using a Ruler and Compasses:** Choose two points $A$ and $B$ on line $MN$[cite: 59]. With the compass opened to radius $AB$, place the compass point at $O$ and draw an arc[cite: 59]. With the compass opened to radius $AO$, place the point at $B$ and draw an arc intersecting the first arc at $P$[cite: 59]. Draw a line through $O$ and $P$; line $OP$ is parallel to $MN$[cite: 59].

### Construction of Perpendicular Lines
Two lines are perpendicular when they intersect at $90^\circ$[cite: 60].
- **From a Point on a Line:** Given a point $B$ on line $AC$, place the compass point at $B$ and strike arcs on both sides to cut $AC$ at points $P$ and $Q$[cite: 63]. With points $P$ and $Q$ as centres and equal radii greater than half $PQ$, strike intersecting arcs above the line at point $R$[cite: 63]. Draw a line through $B$ and $R$; line $BR$ is perpendicular to $AC$ ($R\hat{B}A = R\hat{B}C = 90^\circ$)[cite: 63].
- **From an External Point:** Given a point $O$ outside line $XY$, place the compass point at $O$ and strike an arc cutting line $XY$ at two distinct points[cite: 61, 62]. From these two intersection points, strike intersecting arcs on the opposite side of the line[cite: 62]. Draw a straight line from $O$ through this intersection to meet $XY$ at $90^\circ$[cite: 62].

### Bisection of a Line Segment
A line segment $AB$ is the straight path between points $A$ and $B$[cite: 62]. To bisect $AB$ into two equal parts[cite: 62]:
1. Open the compass to a radius greater than half the length of $AB$[cite: 62].
2. Place the sharp compass point at $A$ and strike two arcs, one above and one below the line segment[cite: 62].
3. Without changing the radius, place the compass point at $B$ and strike arcs intersecting the first two arcs at points $P$ and $Q$[cite: 62].
4. Draw a straight line joining $P$ and $Q$[cite: 62]. The line $PQ$ cuts $AB$ at its exact midpoint $M$ at right angles ($90^\circ$), forming the perpendicular bisector[cite: 62, 63].

### Construction of Special Angles ($90^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $30^\circ$)
- **Angle $90^\circ$:** Constructed by erecting a perpendicular line on a straight line at a given point using compass arcs[cite: 63].
- **Angle $60^\circ$:** With the compass point on the vertex $B$ of a straight line $BC$, draw a wide arc cutting $BC$ at point $X$[cite: 63]. Without altering the compass radius, place the point at $X$ and strike an arc cutting the first arc at point $A$[cite: 64]. Draw a straight line through $B$ and $A$[cite: 64]. The angle $A\hat{B}C = 60^\circ$ (based on the properties of an equilateral triangle)[cite: 64, 67].
- **Angle $45^\circ$:** Formed by constructing a $90^\circ$ right angle and bisecting it with compass arcs ($90^\circ \div 2 = 45^\circ$)[cite: 64, 65, 67].
- **Angle $30^\circ$:** Formed by constructing a $60^\circ$ angle and bisecting it with compass arcs ($60^\circ \div 2 = 30^\circ$)[cite: 64, 67].

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Construction Methods and Geometric Angles
- Label: Instrument Requirements
  Title: Ruler & Set-Square vs Ruler & Compasses
  Body: A ruler and set-square rely on sliding along a straight reference edge to create parallel and perpendicular lines[cite: 58, 61], whereas compasses construct lines and angles using intersecting circular arcs of fixed radii[cite: 59, 63].
- Label: Angle Origin
  Title: Construction of $60^\circ$ vs $90^\circ$
  Body: An angle of $60^\circ$ is constructed from an equilateral triangle framework by preserving the initial arc radius[cite: 63, 64], while $90^\circ$ is constructed by erecting a perpendicular bisector across equidistant points on a straight line[cite: 63].
- Label: Bisected Offspring
  Title: Angle $30^\circ$ vs Angle $45^\circ$
  Body: An angle of $30^\circ$ is obtained by bisecting a $60^\circ$ angle[cite: 64, 67], whereas an angle of $45^\circ$ is obtained by bisecting a $90^\circ$ right angle[cite: 64, 65, 67].
- Label: Line Function
  Title: Line Bisection vs Perpendicular Line
  Body: Line bisection must pass through the exact midpoint of a segment[cite: 62], whereas a perpendicular line can meet a line at any specified point or from an external point at $90^\circ$[cite: 60, 61, 62].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Changing the compass radius between striking the first and second arcs when bisecting a line segment[cite: 62].
  Fact: The compass radius must remain constant from both endpoints; changing the radius displaces the intersection and ruins the midpoint[cite: 62].
- Mistake: Setting the compass radius to less than half the length of the line segment during bisection[cite: 62].
  Fact: If the radius is less than half the length of the line segment, the arcs will never meet or cross each other[cite: 62].
- Mistake: Using a dull, blunt pencil lead during geometric construction[cite: 58].
  Fact: Thick pencil lines create measurement errors of several millimetres or degrees; constructions require a sharply pointed pencil[cite: 58].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Bisecting a Line Segment
Context: A technical student is given a horizontal line segment $AB = 8\text{ cm}$[cite: 62].
Problem: Divide the line segment into two equal parts using a straightedge and compasses only[cite: 62].
Fix:
1. Draw the line segment $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ using a ruler[cite: 62].
2. Open the pair of compasses to a radius of approximately $5\text{ cm}$ (greater than half of $8\text{ cm}$)[cite: 62].
3. Place the compass point at $A$ and draw an arc above and below the line[cite: 62].
4. Place the compass point at $B$ with the same radius and draw arcs intersecting the first arcs at points $P$ and $Q$[cite: 62].
5. Join $P$ and $Q$ with a straight line cutting $AB$ at $M$[cite: 62].
Measuring $AM$ and $MB$ confirms that both halves equal exactly $4\text{ cm}$[cite: 62].

### Example B: Constructing a $60^\circ$ Angle
Context: An engineering diagram requires an angle of inclination of $60^\circ$ at base vertex $B$ on a line $BC$[cite: 63, 64].
Problem: Construct the angle without using a protractor[cite: 63, 64].
Fix:
1. Draw line $BC$[cite: 63].
2. With centre $B$ and any convenient radius, draw an arc intersecting $BC$ at point $X$[cite: 63].
3. Without changing the radius, place the compass point at $X$ and strike an arc cutting the first arc at point $A$[cite: 64].
4. Draw a straight line from vertex $B$ through $A$[cite: 64].
Checking with a protractor verifies that $A\hat{B}C = 60^\circ$[cite: 64].

### Example C: Constructing a $45^\circ$ Angle by Bisection
Context: A carpenter needs a mitre joint cut at an exact angle of $45^\circ$[cite: 64, 65].
Problem: Construct a $45^\circ$ angle using compasses and a straightedge[cite: 64, 65].
Fix:
1. Construct a $90^\circ$ right angle at point $B$ on a straight line[cite: 63].
2. Place the compass point at the vertex $B$ and strike an arc cutting both perpendicular arms at points $D$ and $E$[cite: 63].
3. Place the compass point at $D$ and $E$ in turn to draw two arcs intersecting in the interior of the right angle[cite: 63].
4. Draw a straight line from $B$ through this intersection[cite: 63].
Bisecting the $90^\circ$ right angle produces two equal angles of $45^\circ$ each ($90^\circ \div 2 = 45^\circ$)[cite: 64, 65, 67].
