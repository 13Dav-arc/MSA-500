## Hook
Have you ever looked at a massive classroom table and guessed its weight to be around 500 grammes[cite: 3], or looked down the road and estimated that your school is about 3 kilometres away from your house without using a measuring tape[cite: 3]? Making sensible, nearly correct guesses about measurements without using calculating tools is called estimation[cite: 3]. Estimation gives us a quick mental picture of quantities when adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing in our everyday lives[cite: 3].

## Key Terms
- Term: Estimation
  Definition: Making a guess of nearly correct calculations, distances, weights, prices, or capacities of things without actual measurements or precise calculations[cite: 3].
- Term: Unit
  Definition: A standard quantity used in the measurement of physical quantities such as length, mass, time, capacity, and currency[cite: 3].
- Term: Distance (Length)
  Definition: A linear measure of length that uses multiples of ten in the metric system, with the metre as its basic unit[cite: 3].
- Term: Capacity
  Definition: The space available inside a container to hold something, closely related to volume which measures space occupied by an object[cite: 3].
- Term: Mass
  Definition: The quantity of matter contained in a body, with the kilogram (kg) as its base unit in the metric system[cite: 3].

## Core Concept
### Estimation of Dimensions and Distances
Estimation is making a rough guess of nearly correct values without precise measurement[cite: 3]. It helps us obtain a rough idea of answers during arithmetic operations[cite: 3]. Sometimes rounding off and approximations are used to make estimations[cite: 3].

When physical quantities are measured, they must be expressed in standard units[cite: 3]. Distance is a linear measure of length based on the metric system (multiples of ten), where the metre is the basic unit[cite: 3]:
- $10\text{ millimetres (mm)} = 1\text{ centimetre (cm)}$[cite: 3]
- $10\text{ centimetres (cm)} = 1\text{ decimetre (dm)}$[cite: 3]
- $10\text{ decimetres (dm)} = 1\text{ metre (m)}$[cite: 3]
- $10\text{ metres (m)} = 1\text{ decametre (dam)}$[cite: 3]
- $10\text{ decametres (dam)} = 1\text{ hectometre (hm)}$[cite: 3]
- $10\text{ hectometres (hm)} = 1\text{ kilometre (km)}$[cite: 3]

The most frequently used units of length are centimetres (cm), metres (m), and kilometres (km)[cite: 3].

### Capacity and Mass of Objects
- **Capacity:** Measures the space available inside a container to hold liquids or materials and is closely related to volume[cite: 3]. Cubic measures include $1000\text{ mm}^3 = 1\text{ cm}^3$, $1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ dm}^3$, and $1000\text{ dm}^3 = 1\text{ m}^3$[cite: 3]. The primary unit of capacity is the Litre (L), where $1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ litre} = 1\text{ dm}^3$, and $1000\text{ litres} = 1\text{ m}^3$[cite: 3]. Metric subdivisions include $10\text{ ml} = 1\text{ cl}$, $100\text{ cl} = 1\text{ l}$, and $1000\text{ ml} = 1\text{ l}$[cite: 3].
- **Mass:** The quantity of matter in a body, with the kilogram (kg) as the base unit in the metric system[cite: 3]. Metric mass relationships include $1\text{ decagramme (dg)} = 10\text{ g}$, $1\text{ hectogramme (hg)} = 100\text{ g}$, $1\text{ kilogramme (kg)} = 1000\text{ g}$, $1\text{ tonne (t)} = 1000\text{ kg}$, $100\text{ centigrammes (cg)} = 1\text{ g}$, $1000\text{ milligrammes (mg)} = 1\text{ g}$, and $200\text{ mg} = 1\text{ carat}$[cite: 3].

### Estimation of Time and Other Quantities
Unlike length and mass, time does not follow the base-ten metric system; it follows the **sexagesimal system (base 60)** inherited from the ancient Babylonian number system[cite: 3]:
- $60\text{ seconds} = 1\text{ minute}$[cite: 3]
- $60\text{ minutes} = 1\text{ hour}$[cite: 3]
- $24\text{ hours} = 1\text{ day}$[cite: 3]
- $7\text{ days} = 1\text{ week}$[cite: 3]
- $52\text{ weeks} = 1\text{ year}$[cite: 3]
- $365\text{ days} = 1\text{ year}$ ($366\text{ days} = 1\text{ leap year}$)[cite: 3]

- Label: Base Unit Standards
  Title: Distance vs Mass vs Capacity
  Body: The base unit of distance is the metre, mass is based on the kilogram (kg), and capacity uses the litre (or cubic metre)[cite: 3].

  Title: Quick Estimation vs Standard Conversion
  Body: Estimation provides a fast mental check without tools, while standard conversion yields exact mathematical equivalence using formulas[cite: 3].
- Label: Holding Space
  Title: Volume ($m^3$) vs Capacity (Litres)
  Body: Volume measures the actual space occupied by an object in cubic units, while capacity measures the interior space available to hold substances in litres[cite: 3].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using base-ten relationships to convert time units (e.g., thinking 1 hour = 100 minutes).
  Fact: Time uses the sexagesimal system (base 60), meaning 1 hour is 60 minutes and 1 minute is 60 seconds[cite: 3].
- Mistake: Confusing milligram and kilogram conversions.
  Fact: Because $1\text{ g} = 1000\text{ mg}$ and $1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g}$, $1\text{ kg} = 1,000,000\text{ mg}$[cite: 3].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Converting Linear Units to Metres
Context: A surveyor marks a school boundary distance as $12\text{ km}$[cite: 3].
Problem: Express $12\text{ km}$ in metres[cite: 3].
Fix: Since $1\text{ km} = 1000\text{ m}$, multiply $12$ by $1000$[cite: 3]:
$$12\text{ km} = 12 \times 1000\text{ m} = 12,000\text{ m}$$[cite: 3]

### Example B: Converting Capacity to Cubic Metres ($m^3$)
Context: A water tank holds $1450\text{ dm}^3$ of clean water[cite: 3].
Problem: Express $1450\text{ dm}^3$ in $m^3$[cite: 3].
Fix: Since $1000\text{ dm}^3 = 1\text{ m}^3$, divide $1450$ by $1000$[cite: 3]:
$$1450\text{ dm}^3 = \frac{1450}{1000}\text{ m}^3 = 1.45\text{ m}^3$$[cite: 3]

### Example C: Converting Time Units to Seconds
Context: A race official records a runner's finishing time as $5\text{ minutes } 45\text{ seconds}$[cite: 3].
Problem: Change this duration entirely to seconds[cite: 3].
Fix: Multiply the minutes by $60$ and add the remaining $45$ seconds[cite: 3]:
$$5\text{ mins } 45\text{ secs} = (5 \times 60\text{ secs}) + 45\text{ secs} = 300\text{ secs} + 45\text{ secs} = 345\text{ seconds}$$[cite: 3]
