## Hook
Have you ever heard the government announce that a new national budget will spend approximately ₦28 trillion on schools, roads, and hospitals, or seen a school librarian figure out how many new bookshelves to order for hundreds of donated books in less than ten seconds[cite: 3]? In the real world, banks, governments, market traders, and scientists deal with massive figures every day[cite: 3]. Instead of getting stuck counting every tiny kobo or single unit, they use powers of ten and practical approximation to make smart decisions quickly[cite: 3]!

## Key Terms
- Term: Powers of Ten
  Definition: Numbers formed by multiplying 10 by itself, such as $10^1 = 10$, $10^2 = 100$, and $10^3 = 1000$[cite: 3].
- Term: Rounding Up
  Definition: Adding 1 to the required digit place when the digit immediately following it is half of ten (5) or above[cite: 3].
- Term: Rounding Down
  Definition: Leaving the required digit unchanged and replacing all lower trailing digits with zeros when the following digit is less than 5[cite: 3].
- Term: Quantitative Reasoning
  Definition: Mathematical problem-solving that uses patterns, symbols, and logic rules to discover relationships between numbers[cite: 3].
- Term: Budget
  Definition: A financial plan estimating future income and expenses, prepared by governments, businesses, and families using approximation[cite: 3].

## Core Concept
### Rounding Off to Powers of Ten (10, 100, 1000)
Powers of ten are written with base 10 and exponents: $10^1 = 10$, $10^2 = 100$, and $10^3 = 1000$[cite: 3]. When rounding off whole numbers to any of these powers of ten:
- Identify the target place value (tens, hundreds, or thousands)[cite: 3].
- Look at the test digit immediately to its right (the digit before it)[cite: 3].
- If that test digit is **half or above half of 10** ($5, 6, 7, 8, 9$), round up by adding $1$ to the target digit, and change all lower digits to the right into zeros[cite: 3].
- If the test digit is **less than 5** ($0, 1, 2, 3, 4$), leave the target digit as it is, and drop all remaining lower digits to zero[cite: 3].

*Example:* To round off $253,718$ to the nearest thousand:
- The thousands digit is $3$[cite: 3].
- The digit immediately before it is $7$[cite: 3].
- Since $7$ is higher than $5$, it rounds up to $10$, so $1$ is added to $3$ to make $4$, and the lower digits become zeros: $253,718 = 254,000$ to the nearest $1000$[cite: 3].

### Applications of Approximation in Everyday Life
Approximation is an indispensable skill used across society[cite: 3]:
1. **Purchase of Household Commodities:** Shoppers in markets approximate prices before reaching the counter to ensure their budget covers their purchases[cite: 3].
2. **Measurement of Distances:** Two students can approximate the distances between their homes and the school to see who travels farther[cite: 3].
3. **Government Budgeting:** Governments use approximation as a primary tool when planning national and state budgets for public infrastructure[cite: 3].
4. **Scientific Research:** Scientists use approximations when working with extremely large astronomical distances or microscopic measurements[cite: 3].
5. **Financial Institutions:** Banks and insurance companies use approximation in day-to-day transactions and quick risk evaluations[cite: 3].

### Quantitative Reasoning with Approximation
Quantitative reasoning involves recognizing mathematical rules hidden inside coded operations or symbolic diagrams and applying those rules to solve new problems[cite: 3]. For example, if a logic code defines a combined operation like:
$$(a \ \underline{*}\ b) \cdot c = (a \div b) + c$$
then evaluating $(60 \ \underline{*}\ 12) \cdot 6$ requires computing $(60 \div 12) + 6 = 5 + 6 = 11$[cite: 3].

  Body: Direct arithmetic applies standard rounding rules to numbers, whereas quantitative aptitude requires decoding a hidden symbolic pattern before performing the calculation[cite: 3].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Dropping trailing zeros after rounding whole numbers (e.g., writing 254 instead of 254,000 when rounding 253,718 to the nearest thousand)[cite: 3].
  Fact: Trailing zeros must be written to preserve the place value of the thousands column[cite: 3].
- Mistake: Rounding down when the test digit is exactly 5.
  Fact: The digit 5 is exactly halfway to 10; by universal convention, 5 always rounds up by adding 1 to the target digit[cite: 3].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Sharing Funds in a Club
Context: A sum of ₦236,000 is divided equally among 54 members of a youth club[cite: 3].
Problem: Calculate how much each member receives, and approximate the share to (i) the nearest tenth, (ii) the nearest hundred, and (iii) the nearest thousand[cite: 3].
Fix: First, find the exact division: $236,000 \div 54 = ₦4,370.3703...$[cite: 3]
- (i) Nearest tenth: Look at the hundredths digit ($7 \ge 5$), so round up $3$ to $4$: $₦4,370.4$[cite: 3].
- (ii) Nearest hundred: Look at the tens digit ($7 \ge 5$), so round up $3$ to $4$: $₦4,400.00$[cite: 3].
- (iii) Nearest thousand: Look at the hundreds digit ($3 < 5$), so round down: $₦4,000.00$[cite: 3].

### Example B: Library Shelf Planning
Context: Amina is on her school library committee, which is planning to install new shelves for 580 newly donated books. Each shelf can hold about 28 books[cite: 3].
Problem: About how many additional shelves will the school need to order[cite: 3]?
Fix: Use practical approximation before ordering[cite: 3]:
- 580 is approximately 600[cite: 3].
- 28 is approximately 30[cite: 3].
- Divide the approximated values: $600 \div 30 = 20\text{ shelves}$[cite: 3].
The library committee needs about 20 additional shelves[cite: 3].

### Example C: Solving a Quantitative Reasoning Puzzle
Context: In a quantitative reasoning test, an operation rule is given as:
$$(x \ \underline{*}\ y) \cdot z = (x \div y) + z$$[cite: 3]
Problem: Use this rule to evaluate $(60 \ \underline{*}\ 12) \cdot 6$[cite: 3].
Fix: 
1. Work inside the parentheses first: $60 \div 12 = 5$[cite: 3].
2. Add the outer value $z = 6$: $5 + 6 = 11$[cite: 3].
The final evaluated answer is $11$[cite: 3].
