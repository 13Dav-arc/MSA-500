## Hook
When you speak, read, and write in English, every subtle sound and grammatical choice carries meaning and emotion[cite: 107]. Placing the tip of your tongue between your teeth allows you to pronounce words like *thick* and *those* accurately without confusing them with *tick* or *dose*[cite: 107]. At the same time, mastering past tenses enables you to recount past actions with exact timing, whether you are writing an official formal application to an administrative office, discerning an author's mood in an inspirational passage, or exploring the ancient supernatural realms of myths and legends[cite: 107].

## Key Terms
- Term: Dental Fricative
  Definition: A consonant sound produced when the tip of the tongue is placed lightly against or between the upper front teeth, forcing the airstream through a narrow friction-producing opening[cite: 107].
- Term: Past Tense
  Definition: A verb form used to describe actions, states of being, or habitual events that started or were completed at a time prior to the moment of speaking[cite: 107].
- Term: Author's Mood
  Definition: The emotional atmosphere, attitude, or tone conveyed by a writer toward their subject matter, detectable through word choice and viewpoint[cite: 107].
- Term: Formal Letter
  Definition: A business or official letter addressed to persons in their professional or administrative capacity, characterized by formal language, a specific layout with two addresses, and a stated subject heading[cite: 107].
- Term: Legend
  Definition: A traditional folk narrative regarded as historical fact by both narrator and audience, which recounts the heroic adventures of past figures or supernatural encounters after the creation of the world[cite: 107].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /θ/ and /ð/
Consonants **/θ/** and **/ð/** are dental fricatives produced by placing the tip of the tongue gently between or behind the front upper teeth[cite: 107]:
- **/θ/** is a **voiceless dental fricative** articulated without vocal cord vibration[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbol for /θ/: `th` as in *anthem*, *thick*, *both*, *bath*, *north*, *sheath*, *thorn*, *theme*, *thank*, *breath*, *wrath*, *method*, *ether*, *filthy*, *earthly*, and *myth*[cite: 107].
- **/ð/** is a **voiced dental fricative** articulated with noticeable vocal cord vibration as air escapes past the tongue tip[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbol for /ð/: `th` as in *then*, *that*, *this*, *those*, *thee*, *bathe*, *writhe*, *loathe*, *soothe*, *clothe*, *than*, *either*, *worthy*, *father*, *mother*, *further*, *brother*, and *rather*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Past Tenses
The past tense denotes actions, habits, or states of being that were initiated or completed in the past[cite: 107]. Regular verbs form their past tense by adding *-ed* to the base form (e.g., *worked*, *passed*), while irregular verbs change root vowels or adopt unique forms (e.g., *made*, *sang*, *kept*)[cite: 107].

**The Four Subdivisions of the Past Tense:**
1. **Simple Past Tense:** Indicates an action that started and finished in the past, or describes a past habit no longer practiced[cite: 107].
   - *I worked yesterday*[cite: 107].
   - *Olu passed the last stage of the examination last year*[cite: 107].
   - *He used to travel home when he was young*[cite: 107].
2. **Past Continuous Tense:** Expresses an action that was ongoing in the past, often interrupted by another past action[cite: 107].
   - Formed using *was/were* + *-ing* verb[cite: 107].
   - *I was teaching the students yesterday when the principal sent for me*[cite: 107].
   - *While the bus was moving, the woman jumped down*[cite: 107].
3. **Past Perfect Tense:** Used when two actions occurred in the past and one was completed before the other[cite: 107]. The earlier action takes the past perfect (*had* + past participle), while the subsequent action takes the simple past[cite: 107].
   - *The boy had died before help reached him*[cite: 107].
   - *By the time I got to the office, Olu had left for the meeting*[cite: 107].
   - *The suspect had escaped when the police raised the alarm*[cite: 107].
4. **Past Perfect Continuous Tense:** Indicates an action that had been underway for an extended duration before a specific point in the past[cite: 107].
   - Formed using *had been* + *-ing* verb[cite: 107].
   - *We had been praying since 1998 before God intervened in 2008*[cite: 107].
   - *The girl had been writing SSCE for five years before she passed it last year*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Format and Features of Formal Letters
Formal letters are letters written to offices, organizations, institutions, and administrative dignitaries[cite: 107]. They are also called **official** or **business letters**[cite: 107].

**Structural Layout of a Formal Letter[cite: 107]:**
1. **Writer's Address & Date:** Placed at the top right-hand side of the page with appropriate punctuation and the date written in full (e.g., *25th August, 2026.*)[cite: 107].
2. **Addressee's Designation & Address:** Placed at the left margin below the level of the date, stating the recipient's official title and organization (e.g., *The Education Secretary, Zenith Heights High School, Lagos.*)[cite: 107].
3. **Salutation:** Placed at the left margin: *Dear Sir,* or *Dear Madam,* (never informal greetings like *Hi* or *Dear John*)[cite: 107].
4. **Letter Heading (Title):** Written centrally in capital letters or title case with underlining, stating the subject matter concisely: *APPLICATION FOR ADMISSION INTO JS 1*[cite: 107].
5. **Body of the Letter:** Well-paragraphed formal prose without colloquial slang or contracted forms (use *do not* instead of *don't*)[cite: 107].
   - *Introduction:* State clearly the purpose of writing[cite: 107].
   - *Content Paragraphs:* Provide supporting facts, qualifications, or reasons[cite: 107].
   - *Conclusion:* A polite closing request or statement of appreciation[cite: 107].
6. **Subscript / Complimentary Close:** Written at the bottom right-hand or left-hand side as **Yours faithfully,** (with a capital *Y* and small *f*, followed by a comma)[cite: 107].
7. **Signature and Full Name:** The writer's signature, followed by the full official name clearly written underneath[cite: 107].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Oral Literature: Myths and Legends
Myths and legends belong to traditional oral literature—highly imaginative narratives passed down across generations[cite: 107]:
- **Myths:** Religious folktales that explain the origins of the universe, natural phenomena, and the creation of humanity and animals[cite: 107]. Both the narrator and audience historically regard them as sacred truth[cite: 107]. The central characters are typically gods, spirits, or divine supernatural deities operating in an early, primeval world[cite: 107].
- **Legends:** Traditional folk history dealing with real human experiences that occurred *after* the creation of the world was accomplished[cite: 107]. While often incorporating supernatural embellishments (such as ghosts, enchanted weapons, or monsters), legends are based on historical human heroes, heroines, kings, and warriors who performed extraordinary adventures (e.g., Queen Amina of Zaria, Oduduwa, Moremi, or King Arthur)[cite: 107].

  Title: Simple Past vs Past Perfect

  Body: A myth explains primeval cosmic creation involving gods and deities[cite: 107], while a legend recounts the historical adventures of human heroes after creation[cite: 107].

  Title: Formal vs Informal Letters
  Body: A formal letter requires two addresses and concludes with *Yours faithfully,* followed by a signature and full name[cite: 107], while an informal letter uses one address and ends with *Yours sincerely,* or a first name[cite: 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Substituting /t/ or /d/ for the dental fricatives /θ/ and /ð/ (e.g., pronouncing *think* as *tink*, or *this* as *dis*)[cite: 107].
  Fact: Place the tip of your tongue directly between your teeth so that air escapes with friction, rather than letting the tongue strike the roof of the mouth as a plosive[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Using the past perfect tense for a single standalone past action (e.g., saying *"I had eaten yesterday"* without an accompanying second past action)[cite: 107].
  Fact: The past perfect (*had eaten*) is specifically reserved to show that an action occurred before another past event; a single past event requires the simple past (*I ate yesterday*)[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Ending a formal letter addressed to an unknown official with *Yours sincerely* instead of *Yours faithfully*[cite: 107].
  Fact: When addressing an official as *Dear Sir* or *Dear Madam*, the mandatory complimentary close is *Yours faithfully,*[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Omitting the writer's signature in a formal letter before typing or writing the full name[cite: 107].
  Fact: A formal letter is invalid without the writer's physical signature placed directly above the full name[cite: 107].
