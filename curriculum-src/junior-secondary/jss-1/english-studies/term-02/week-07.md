## Hook
When you speak, write, or act, your mastery of vowel glides and descriptive timing shapes how your audience feels and reacts[cite: 107]. Gliding smoothly between vowels allows you to distinguish between *late* and *let*, or *cheer* and *chair*, preventing embarrassing miscommunications[cite: 107]. Meanwhile, combining robust verb tenses with expressive adverbials gives rhythm to your sentences, whether you are analyzing the electric mood of a crowded football stadium, delivering a persuasive debate speech, or untangling the dramatic conflicts of Maxwell Onyeka’s *Vengeance of the Spirit*[cite: 107].

## Key Terms
- Term: Diphthong
  Definition: An independent vowel sound produced by a continuous, smooth glide of the tongue from one vowel position to another within the same syllable[cite: 107].
- Term: Adverbial
  Definition: Any word, phrase, or clause that functions as an adverb by modifying a verb, an adjective, or another adverb, indicating time, manner, place, condition, or purpose[cite: 107].
- Term: Author's Mood
  Definition: The emotional atmosphere, feeling, or state of mind that a writer intentionally creates for the reader through vivid descriptions, word choices, and sentence rhythms[cite: 107].
- Term: Counter-Argument
  Definition: An opposing viewpoint or objection raised by an opponent in an argumentative essay, which the writer acknowledges and systematically refutes with evidence[cite: 107].
- Term: Theme
  Definition: The central idea, underlying message, or core philosophical concern explored by a playwright or author throughout a literary text[cite: 107].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Introduction to Diphthongs (/eɪ/, /əʊ/, /ɪə/, /eə/)
A **diphthong** is a double vowel sound articulated as the tongue glides smoothly from an initial vowel quality toward a second vowel quality within one syllable[cite: 107].
- **/eɪ/**: A closing diphthong where the glide begins at /e/ and moves toward /ɪ/ with spread lips[cite: 107]. Common spelling symbols include:
  - `a` (*face*, *mate*, *lame*), `ai` (*rain*, *main*, *gain*), `ay` (*pay*, *way*, *ray*), `eigh` (*eight*), `ey` (*they*), and `ea` (*break*)[cite: 107].
  - *Learner Pitfall:* Avoid pronouncing /eɪ/ as the short monophthong /e/ (e.g., confusing *late* with *let*, or *gate* with *get*)[cite: 107].
- **/əʊ/**: A closing diphthong where the glide begins at the central position /ə/ and moves toward the back rounded vowel /ʊ/ with lips becoming progressively rounded[cite: 107]. Common spelling symbols include:
  - `o` (*close*, *no*, *smoke*), `oe` (*hoe*, *roe*), `oa` (*moan*, *boast*, *oath*), `ou` (*soul*, *mould*, *boulder*), `ow` (*grow*, *flow*, *bestow*), and `ol` (*cold*, *gold*, *told*)[cite: 107].
- **/ɪə/**: A centring diphthong that glides from the close-mid front /ɪ/ toward the central vowel /ə/[cite: 107]. Word examples: *steer*, *tear*, *hear*, *cheer*, *mere*, *beard*, *spear*, *ear*[cite: 107].
- **/eə/**: A centring diphthong that glides from the front open-mid /e/ toward the central vowel /ə/[cite: 107]. Word examples: *chair*, *their*, *there*, *pear*, *heir*, *air*, *share*, *care*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Tenses and Adverbials (Continued)
Tenses specify the chronological occurrence of an action through changes in the verb, while adverbials add contextual precision regarding time, manner, place, condition, and purpose[cite: 107]:
- **Present Tenses & Adverbials:** Express routine habits, current states, or ongoing commentary[cite: 107].
  - *Habitual present with adverb of frequency:* *Paul and Silas pray everyday*[cite: 107].
  - *Habitual present with time adverbial:* *We do our assignments on weekends*[cite: 107].
- **Past Tenses & Adverbials:** Detail finished events and past states of affairs[cite: 107].
  - *Simple past with time adverbial:* *I travelled to London last week*[cite: 107].
  - *Past action with habitual time phrase:* *Akin washed his clothes on Fridays*[cite: 107].
- **Future Tenses & Adverbials:** Express prospective plans using *shall* (first person *I*, *we*) and *will* (second/third person *you*, *he*, *she*, *they*)[cite: 107].
  - *Simple future with time adverbial:* *I shall travel next year*; *We shall meet next week*[cite: 107].
  - *Future intention with condition:* *His father will pay his school fees if he passes*[cite: 107].
  - *Future action with purpose:* *He will wake up early in order to prepare for the test*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Argumentative Essay (Guided Composition)
An **argumentative essay** requires the candidate to present a controversial issue that has two distinct sides, arguing persuasively to convince the reader to agree with the writer's stance[cite: 107].
- **Core Requirements for Effective Argumentative Writing:**
  1. *Clear Understanding:* Possess a thorough grasp of the topic or proposition[cite: 107].
  2. *Jotted Points:* Brainstorm and jot down at least four solid supporting points with clear evidence[cite: 107].
  3. *Orderly Arrangement:* Structure points logically from strongest to weakest, refuting opposing claims[cite: 107].
  4. *Clarity of Expression:* Use concise, persuasive language without rambling emotional abuse[cite: 107].
  5. *Mechanical Accuracy:* Ensure the essay is completely free of grammatical, concord, and spelling errors[cite: 107].
- **Guided Debate Topic:** *"Male Children Are More Beneficial to Their Parents Than Female Children"* (or the reverse motion)[cite: 107].
- **Step-by-Step Writing Blueprint:**
  - *Title:* Boldly declared at the top (e.g., *WHY FEMALE CHILDREN ARE EQUALLY OR MORE BENEFICIAL TO PARENTS THAN MALE CHILDREN*)[cite: 107].
  - *Vocatives / Opening:* Address the Chairman, panel of judges, timekeeper, co-debaters, and audience[cite: 107].
  - *Body Paragraph 1 (Affirmation):* Define key terms and state your stance unambiguously[cite: 107].
  - *Body Paragraphs 2 & 3 (Evidence):* Present compelling examples of women excelling in leadership, caregiving, education, and elder-care[cite: 107].
  - *Body Paragraph 4 (Rebuttal):* Refute the counter-argument that only male children preserve the family name, explaining modern legal and socio-economic realities[cite: 107].
  - *Conclusion:* Reiterate your central stance and deliver an impactful final appeal[cite: 107].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Drama: Themes in Maxwell Onyeka's *Vengeance of the Spirit*
In drama, a **theme** is the central subject, core idea, or moral inquiry explored through character actions and dialogue[cite: 107]. In Maxwell Onyeka's recommended play *Vengeance of the Spirit*, several major themes intertwine[cite: 107]:
1. **Theme of Affection and Love:** Explores romantic loyalty and emotional attachment between young lovers, demonstrating how genuine affection often conflicts with rigid parental expectations[cite: 107].
2. **Theme of Wrong Decisions and Greed:** Portrays how parents who make materialistic marital choices for their children create catastrophe and generational heartbreak[cite: 107].
3. **Theme of Retribution and Supernatural Vengeance:** Emphasizes that unjust human actions, breaches of sacred oaths, and moral defiance inevitably awaken ancestral wrath and spiritual justice[cite: 107].
4. **Theme of Cultural Identity and Traditional Rites:** Highlights traditional African marriage customs, bride prices, respect for elders, and the sacred authority of deities in regulating community morality[cite: 107].

- Label: Sound Contrast
  Title: Diphthong /ɪə/ vs Diphthong /eə/
  Body: /ɪə/ starts with the close front vowel /ɪ/ as in *hear* and *cheer*, whereas /eə/ starts with the open-mid vowel /e/ as in *hair* and *chair*[cite: 107].
- Label: Expressive Intent
  Title: Persuasive Argument vs Dramatic Dialogue
  Body: Argumentative essays present logical points and refutations to persuade a reader[cite: 107], while dramatic dialogue portrays human conflict through spoken character exchanges on stage[cite: 107].

  Title: Theme vs Plot in Drama
  Body: The plot is the sequential chain of events and actions that happen on stage, whereas the theme is the deeper philosophical or moral idea revealed by those events[cite: 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Replacing the diphthong /eɪ/ with the short vowel /e/ (e.g., pronouncing *late* as *let*, or *wait* as *wet*)[cite: 107].
  Fact: The sound /eɪ/ is a glide from /e/ toward /ɪ/ and requires longer articulation with spread lips[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Pronouncing the centring diphthongs /ɪə/ and /eə/ identically (e.g., confusing *steer* with *stare*, or *hear* with *hair*)[cite: 107].
  Fact: /ɪə/ starts with the tongue raised close to /ɪ/ (*hear*), while /eə/ begins lower at /e/ (*hair*) before gliding to /ə/[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Resorting to abusive insults against opponents in an argumentative essay or debate[cite: 107].
  Fact: Argumentative writing demands objective evidence, logical deduction, and courteous persuasion rather than emotional hostility[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Retelling the plot summary when asked to analyze a theme in a drama text[cite: 107].
  Fact: Analyzing a theme requires explaining the underlying moral, social, or philosophical message of the play, not just recounting the story[cite: 107].
