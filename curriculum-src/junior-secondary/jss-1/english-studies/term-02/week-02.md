## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /s/ and /z/
The consonants **/s/** and **/z/** are alveolar fricatives[cite: 107]. They are articulated in almost the exact same way: the blade of the tongue touches the alveolar (upper tooth) ridge firmly for /s/ and gently for /z/[cite: 107].
- **/s/** is a **voiceless** alveolar fricative because the vocal cords do not vibrate during its articulation[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols for /s/: `s` (*sip*, *seal*, *bus*), `c` (*cease*, *price*, *ice*), and `sc` (*scene*)[cite: 107].
- **/z/** is a **voiced** alveolar fricative because the airstream causes the vocal cords to vibrate during its articulation[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols for /z/: `z` (*zip*, *zoo*, *zeal*, *buzz*, *prize*), `s` (*cause*, *houses*, *lies*, *eyes*), and `se` (*size*)[cite: 107].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Tenses: Present Tense Forms
Tenses express the time of an action in a sentence, where the verb plays the primary functional role[cite: 107]. There are three major forms of the present tense[cite: 107]:
1. **Present Simple Tense:**
   - Used for habitual, consistent actions that happen every time: *He comes here often*; *She dances everyday*[cite: 107].
   - Used for universal truths or permanent conditions: *I am a teacher*; *She is my wife*; *We are gentle people*[cite: 107].
   - Used for live sports commentaries reporting actions as they unfold: *Okocha passes the ball*; *He heads the ball*[cite: 107].
   - *Rule:* Singular subjects take singular verbs (*He runs*), while plural subjects (and pronouns *I* and *you*) take base plural verbs (*They run*; *I sing*)[cite: 107].
2. **Present Continuous Tense:**
   - Used for actions taking place right at the time of speaking[cite: 107].
   - Formed using the auxiliary verb *am/is/are* + present participle (*-ing* verb)[cite: 107]: *Mrs Adewole is teaching us English Language*; *I am eating my dinner*; *We are going to church*[cite: 107].
3. **Present Perfect Tense:**
   - Used for actions that have just been completed in the recent past[cite: 107]: *He has gone*; *She has just arrived*; *They have completed the building*[cite: 107].
   - Used for an action that began in the past and continues into the present: *I have lived here for ten years*; *She has taught English for five years*[cite: 107].
   - *Concord Rule:* Use **have** with *I*, *we*, *you*, and *they*; use **has** with *he*, *she*, and *it*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — More on Descriptive Composition
A **descriptive composition** gives a clear mental picture of a person, place, or object[cite: 107]. It requires the writer to describe things with such sensory accuracy that the reader can visualize, hear, and feel what is being described[cite: 107]. Examples include: *"My School Compound"*, *"My Favourite Teacher"*, and *"My Favourite Game"*[cite: 107].
- **Content:** Provide a detailed description of features, proportions, colors, and behavior, noting the significance or qualities you admire[cite: 107].
- **Organisation:**
  - *Introduction:* Introduce the scene, object, or person, giving general outward appearance and location[cite: 107].
  - *Body Paragraphs:* Move systematically (e.g., from head to toe for a person, or from entrance gate to inner classrooms for a compound)[cite: 107]. Do not itemize with bullet points; write in cohesive prose paragraphs[cite: 107].
  - *Conclusion:* Provide a brief summary, general impression, proverb, or personal remark that leaves an impact on the reader[cite: 107].
- **Expression:** Use precise adjectives (e.g., *towering*, *tranquil*, *sparkling*) and sensory verbs that evoke sights, sounds, and textures[cite: 107].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Prose: Core Literary Terms
Prose refers to non-poetic writing that follows conventional sentences and paragraphs[cite: 107]. Key literary terms in prose include:
- **Plot:** The organized sequence of events that forms the story of a novel or play[cite: 107].
- **Conflict:** The struggle between opposing forces that drives the plot forward; it may be **external** (a character against nature, society, or another person) or **internal** (two competing desires within a character)[cite: 107].
- **Climax:** The turning point and emotional peak of the plot where the tension reaches its highest level and action begins falling[cite: 107].
- **Dénouement:** The resolution or conclusion of the plot where loose ends are tied up and the outcome is settled[cite: 107].
- **Protagonist vs Antagonist:** The protagonist is the central main character, while the antagonist is the character or force that opposes the protagonist[cite: 107].
- **Character Types:**
  - *Flat Character:* Simple, two-dimensional character with few traits and predictable behavior[cite: 107].
  - *Round Character:* Complex, dynamic, multi-faceted character capable of surprising the reader[cite: 107].
  - *Static Character:* Remains unchanged throughout the story; a *dynamic character* undergoes significant growth or transformation[cite: 107].
- **Setting:** The specific physical place and historical time period in which the events occur[cite: 107].
- **Theme:** The central idea, moral lesson, or underlying message explored throughout the work[cite: 107].
- **Narrative Techniques:** *Suspense* (deliberately withholding information to build anticipation), *Flashback* (shifting back in time to reveal earlier occurrences), and *Foreshadowing* (giving subtle hints about future happenings)[cite: 107].

  Title: Present Simple vs Present Continuous
  Body: Present simple expresses habitual routines and permanent truths (*She dances everyday*), while present continuous expresses actions currently in progress at the moment of speaking (*She is dancing now*)[cite: 107].

  Title: Flat Character vs Round Character
  Body: A flat character has few traits, is predictable, and undergoes no change, whereas a round character is multifaceted, realistic, and undergoes growth throughout the narrative[cite: 107].
- Label: Compositional Modality
  Title: Narrative vs Descriptive Essay
  Body: Narrative essays focus on the chronological passage of time and action (*what happened first, next, and last*), while descriptive essays focus on spatial arrangement and sensory imagery (*how something looks, sounds, and feels*)[cite: 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Pronouncing the plural noun marker `-es` or final `s` as voiceless /s/ in words where it should be voiced /z/[cite: 107].
  Fact: In words like *houses*, *lies*, *eyes*, and *buzz*, the final sound is the voiced fricative /z/, not voiceless /s/[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Using the pronoun *have* with third-person singular subjects (e.g., saying *"He have gone"* or *"She have arrived"*)[cite: 107].
  Fact: Singular third-person pronouns (*he*, *she*, *it*) take **has**, while *I*, *we*, *you*, and *they* take **have**[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Confusing the climax of a prose plot with its dénouement[cite: 107].
  Fact: The climax is the highest point of dramatic tension, whereas the dénouement is the subsequent resolution and untying of the plot's conflicts[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Listing features in numbered bullet points within a descriptive essay.
  Fact: Descriptive essays must be written in continuous, well-developed prose paragraphs rather than bulleted lists[cite: 107].
