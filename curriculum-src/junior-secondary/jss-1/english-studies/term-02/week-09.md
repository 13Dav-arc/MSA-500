## Hook
When an orator mounts a podium to deliver an inspiring address, their success depends on the clear articulation of every consonant plosive, the rhythmic pauses indicated by punctuation, and the evocative imagery of their words[cite: 1]. Pronouncing the difference between /t/ and /d/ prevents a speaker from confusing *bent* with *bend* or *slapped* with *slammed*[cite: 1]. Punctuation transforms what would otherwise be a chaotic stream of words into clear, organized units of thought[cite: 1]. When combined with the public address techniques of speech writing and the emotional intensity of poetry, these foundational skills empower you to express yourself with authority, rhythm, and grace[cite: 1].

## Key Terms
- Term: Alveolar Plosive
  Definition: A consonant sound produced by pressing the tip of the tongue firmly against the alveolar ridge to block the airstream completely, which is then released with an explosive burst of sound[cite: 1].
- Term: Punctuation
  Definition: A system of established symbols and marks used in written text to separate sentences, clauses, and phrases, giving order, clarity, and fluent meaning to writing[cite: 1].
- Term: Oratory
  Definition: The art or practice of delivering formal, eloquent, and persuasive speeches before an assembled public audience[cite: 1].
- Term: Vocatives
  Definition: The formal, protocol-mandated opening words used to address specific dignitaries, panel members, and guests at the start of a speech or debate[cite: 1].
- Term: Stanza
  Definition: A grouped set of lines within a poem, separated from other groups by a blank line, functioning similarly to a paragraph in prose[cite: 1].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /t/ and /d/
The consonants **/t/** and **/d/** are alveolar plosive (stop) consonants[cite: 1]. During their articulation, the soft palate is raised to prevent air from escaping through the nasal cavity, and the tip of the tongue is pressed firmly against the alveolar (tooth) ridge to trap the breath behind it before releasing it explosively[cite: 1]:
- **/t/** is a **voiceless** alveolar plosive articulated without vocal cord vibration[cite: 1].
  - Common spelling symbols for /t/:
    - `t` as in *top*, *team*, *meat*, *set*, *tea*, *tell*, *lent*, *bit*, *bet*, *talk*, *technique*[cite: 1]
    - `tt` as in *betting*, *letter*, *kettle*, *putting*, *rotten*, *battalion*, *mattress*, *cigarette*[cite: 1]
    - `th` in select proper nouns and loanwords: *Thomas*, *Thames*, *Anthony*, *Thyme*, *Theresa*, *Thompson*, *Thailand*[cite: 1]
    - `d` in words like *width*, *breadth*, *apartheid*[cite: 1]
    - `-ed` past tense inflection following voiceless sounds (/p/, /k/, /f/, /s/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /θ/): *slapped*, *tasked*, *drenched*, *capped*, *raced*, *developed*, *bluffed*, *clinched*, *asked*, *looked*, *kicked*, *jumped*, *worked*, *laughed*, *cursed*, *hissed*[cite: 1]
- **/d/** is a **voiced** alveolar plosive articulated in the exact same mouth posture as /t/, but with active vocal cord vibration[cite: 1].
  - Common spelling symbols for /d/:
    - `d` as in *deep*, *hard*, *rider*, *code*, *dig*, *friend*, *dog*, *seed*, *dull*, *bad*, *amend*[cite: 1]
    - `dd` as in *pudding*, *Buddha*, *daddy*, *riddle*, *adding*, *muddle*, *wedding*, *saddle*[cite: 1]
    - `-ed` past tense inflection following vowel sounds or voiced consonants (/b/, /g/, /v/, /ð/, /z/, /ʒ/, /dʒ/, /m/, /n/, /ŋ/, /l/): *begged*, *judged*, *warned*, *feared*, *warmed*, *nabbed*, *called*, *loved*[cite: 1]

### Aspect 2: Structure — Introduction to Punctuation Marks and Capitalization
Punctuation prevents written language from collapsing into an unreadable string of words by establishing grammatical boundaries and tone[cite: 1]:
1. **Full Stop / Period (`.`):** The most emphatic mark; terminates a declarative sentence that is neither a question nor an exclamation[cite: 1]. It is also used in specific abbreviations (such as *Feb.* or *Sept.*) and in web and email addresses (*www.school.edu.ng*)[cite: 1].
2. **Question Mark (`?`):** Concludes direct interrogative queries[cite: 1]. Only one question mark should terminate a sentence[cite: 1].
   - *Example:* *Where are we?*[cite: 1]
3. **Exclamation Mark (`!`):** Concludes a sharp emotional exclamation, interjection, or urgent directive[cite: 1]. It should be used sparingly to preserve dramatic emphasis[cite: 1].
   - *Example:* *Run!*[cite: 1]; *What a beautiful day!*[cite: 1]
4. **Comma (`,`):** Separates items in a series, marks parenthetical insertions, and sets off introductory clauses or transitional adverbs[cite: 1].
5. **Apostrophe (`'`):** Indicates the omission of letters in contractions (*don't*, *it's*) and marks grammatical possession (*Tunde's book*, *the students' hall*)[cite: 1].
6. **Capital Letters:** Always begin a sentence and direct speech, open proper nouns (*Lagos*, *Mary Brown*), and introduce religious references (*God*, *Allah*), days of the week, months, and official titles (*President*, *Principal*)[cite: 1].

### Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The Art of Eloquent Oratory
**Reading Passage:**

>

A nervous speaker who mumbles into written notes, mispronounces basic words, or ignores punctuation marks will rapidly lose the attention of the hall[cite: 1]. Conversely, an orator who maintains confident eye contact, pauses deliberately at full stops, and modulates pitch to emphasize key points commands rapt silence[cite: 1]. When oral technique unites with compelling content, the spoken address becomes an unforgettable performance that lingers in the minds of the listeners long after the podium is vacated[cite: 1].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Speech Writing (Introduction, Vocatives, and Delivery)
**Speech writing** is the art of drafting a written composition intended to be delivered orally before an audience on occasions such as prize-giving days, valedictory services, or society launches[cite: 1].
- **Key Characteristics:**
  - *Spoken Register:* It demands the skill of writing almost as one speaks, using clear, simple sentences and audience-inclusive pronouns (*we*, *our*, *you and I*)[cite: 1].
  - *Oral Conventions:* Controlled contractions (*don't*, *didn't*, *doesn't*) are acceptable, but street slang and crude colloquialisms are strictly prohibited[cite: 1].
- **Structural Blueprint:**
  1. **Title / Purpose Heading:** Written at the top (e.g., *A SPEECH DELIVERED BY THE PRESIDENT OF THE RED CROSS SOCIETY ON THE LAUNCH OF THE SCHOOL BRANCH*)[cite: 1].
  2. **Opening Vocatives:** The formal protocol acknowledging the audience in order of official rank[cite: 1]:
     > *"Honourable Commissioner for Education;*[cite: 1]  
     > *Respected Principal;*[cite: 1]  
     > *Chairman and Members of the Parents-Teachers Association;*[cite: 1]  
     > *Dedicated Teachers;*[cite: 1]  
     > *Fellow Students;*[cite: 1]  
     > *Ladies and Gentlemen."*[cite: 1]
  3. **Introduction:** Expresses gratitude for the opportunity to speak and states the purpose of the occasion[cite: 1].
  4. **Body Paragraphs:** Explains the mission, background, achievements, or appeals using vivid points and clear connective adverbs[cite: 1].
  5. **Conclusion:** Summarizes the message, delivers an appeal or charge, and concludes with an expression of thanks (e.g., *"Thank you all for listening"*).

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Types of Poetry and Oiwona Andrew's "Nightfall"
A **poem** is a piece of creative writing arranged in verse, lines, and stanzas, while **poetry** refers to the body or collection of poems[cite: 1]. Poetry expresses deep human emotions, philosophy, and sensory experiences using condensed, metaphoric language[cite: 1].

**Major Types of Poetry:**
1. **Epic:** A lengthy, serious narrative poem recounting the grand heroic exploits of a legendary figure or national hero[cite: 1].
2. **Lyric:** A short, song-like poem that expresses the personal thoughts, feelings, and emotions of the poet[cite: 1].
3. **Ode:** A dignified, serious lyric poem written to a formal structure, usually composed to praise an individual, an object, or an abstract concept[cite: 1].
4. **Ballad:** A narrative folk poem composed in short stanzas with a repeated refrain, often telling a tale of romance or legend and designed to be sung[cite: 1].
5. **Dirge:** A short, mournful song or poem expressing grief, typically chanted at a funeral[cite: 1].
6. **Elegy:** A reflective, melancholy poem lamenting the death of a specific individual or contemplating human mortality[cite: 1].
7. **Sonnet:** A strict lyric poem consisting of exactly **fourteen lines** with an established rhyme scheme[cite: 1].

**Study of Oiwona Andrew's "Nightfall":**
In his evocative poem *"Nightfall"*, Oiwona Andrew explores the transition of nature from the vitality of daylight into the silence, mystery, and potential perils of the night[cite: 1]. Through rich personification, visual imagery, and solemn rhythm, the poem depicts darkness not merely as an absence of light, but as an encroaching force that brings physical rest to weary laborers while awakening anxieties in the human mind[cite: 1].

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Distinctions
- Label: Articulatory Voicing
  Title: Consonant /t/ vs Consonant /d/
  Body: Both are alveolar plosives formed by the tongue tip against the tooth ridge, but /t/ is voiceless with no vocal cord vibration (*bent*, *slapped*), while /d/ is voiced with prominent vocal cord vibration (*bend*, *judged*)[cite: 1].
- Label: Past Tense Inflection
  Title: Voiceless "-ed" (/t/) vs Voiced "-ed" (/d/)
  Body: Past tense "-ed" is pronounced as /t/ when following voiceless sounds (*laughed*, *jumped*, *drenched*), but is pronounced as /d/ when following voiced sounds (*warned*, *begged*, *feared*)[cite: 1].
- Label: Mournful Poetry
  Title: Dirge vs Elegy
  Body: A dirge is a short, solemn song or chant performed directly at a funeral service, while an elegy is a longer, meditative poem reflecting formally upon death and mortality[cite: 1].
- Label: Rhetorical Scope
  Title: Speech Writing vs Argumentative Essay
  Body: Speech writing uses direct vocatives, spoken conversational register, and inclusive pronouns for oral delivery before an audience[cite: 1], whereas an argumentative essay focuses on establishing claims, evidence, and logical refutations in a debate format[cite: 1].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Pronouncing the past tense suffix "-ed" as a full extra syllable "-ed" after sounds other than /t/ or /d/[cite: 1].
  Fact: The suffix "-ed" only adds a separate syllable (/ɪd/) after base verbs ending in /t/ or /d/ (*wanted*, *needed*)[cite: 1]; after voiceless sounds it is pronounced /t/ (*slapped*, *looked*) and after voiced sounds it is pronounced /d/ (*warned*, *loved*)[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Overusing exclamation marks repeatedly at the end of informal sentences (e.g., *Come here!!!!!*)[cite: 1].
  Fact: A single exclamation mark is sufficient; overuse weakens dramatic impact and looks sloppy[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Omitting the formal vocatives when delivering or writing a formal speech[cite: 1].
  Fact: A formal speech must always open with the designated protocol addressing the dignitaries, teachers, and guests present[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Confusing a fourteen-line sonnet with an epic poem[cite: 1].
  Fact: A sonnet is strictly fourteen lines long[cite: 1], whereas an epic is a very long, multi-book narrative poem[cite: 1].
