## Hook
Whether following an illustrated route map to locate a new school building, giving step-by-step instructions on cooking your favorite local meal, or painting vivid poetic imagery with comparisons, language functions as an essential map for thinking and action[cite: 107]. Pronouncing closing and centring diphthongs accurately prevents confusion between words like *boil* and *bowl*, while selecting exact determiners tells your reader precisely which objects you are talking about[cite: 107]. Combining clear speech with sequential expository composition and figurative tools like similes and metaphors allows you to instruct, inform, and inspire any audience[cite: 107].

## Key Terms
- Term: Closing Diphthong
  Definition: A double vowel sound produced when the tongue glides smoothly from an open or central vowel position toward a closer vowel position like /ɪ/ or /ʊ/[cite: 107].
- Term: Determiner
  Definition: A word placed before a noun or noun phrase to specify, identify, or quantify it without strictly serving as a descriptive adjective[cite: 107].
- Term: Graphic Representation
  Definition: A visual diagram, sketch, symbol, or map used to represent physical terrain, relationships, processes, or numerical information clearly[cite: 107].
- Term: Expository Essay
  Definition: A structured composition written primarily in the simple present tense that explains a process, gives instructions, or clarifies how a system operates step-by-step[cite: 107].
- Term: Simile
  Definition: A figure of speech that directly compares two distinct things having similar qualities by using connective words such as "like" or "as"[cite: 107].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Diphthongs /aɪ/, /aʊ/, /ɔɪ/, and /ʊə/
Diphthongs are dynamic vowel glides produced as the tongue shifts smoothly from one articulatory posture to another within the same syllable[cite: 107]:
- **/aɪ/**: A closing diphthong gliding from the open vowel /a/ toward the close-mid front vowel /ɪ/[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols: `i` (*write*, *climb*, *bite*), `y` (*by*, *fry*), `ie` (*lie*, *tie*, *cries*), `igh` (*right*, *flight*), `uy` (*buy*, *guy*), `ui` (*guide*, *guile*), `ei`/`ey` (*either*, *height*, *eye*), and `ai` (*aisle*)[cite: 107].
- **/aʊ/**: A closing diphthong starting between the central and back region and gliding toward /ʊ/, while the lips change from neutral to rounded[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols: `ow` (*cow*, *clown*, *crown*, *bow*, *owl*), `ou` (*round*, *bounce*, *voucher*, *doubt*, *devout*), and `ough` (*drought*, *plough*, *bough*)[cite: 107].
- **/ɔɪ/**: A closing diphthong that glides from the back open-mid rounded vowel /ɔ/ toward /ɪ/[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols: `oi` (*avoid*, *boil*, *voice*, *joint*, *anoint*), `oy` (*boy*, *cloy*, *alloy*), and `uoy` (*buoy*)[cite: 107].
- **/ʊə/**: A centring diphthong that glides from the close-mid rounded vowel /ʊ/ toward the central unrounded vowel /ə/[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols: `oor` (*poor*, *moor*, *boor*), `our` (*tourist*, *dour*), `ure` (*sure*, *cure*, *pure*, *lure*), `ua` (*continual*), and `eur` (*liqueur*)[cite: 107].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Determiners: Types and Functions
A **determiner** is a word placed before a noun or pronoun to identify, point out, or quantify it[cite: 107]. Unlike adjectives, determiners do not describe physical attributes like color or texture; they define grammatical reference[cite: 107].

**Major Classes of Determiners:**
1. **Articles:**
   - *Definite Article ("the"):* Identifies a specific person or object already mentioned, unique entities (*the Bible*, *the Prime Minister*), or an entire class (*the big cats*)[cite: 107].
   - *Indefinite Article ("a" / "an"):* Refers to non-specific singular countable nouns or means "one"[cite: 107].
     - Use **a** before words beginning with consonant sounds (e.g., *a box*, *a road*, *a unit* because *unit* begins with the consonant sound /j/)[cite: 107].
     - Use **an** before words beginning with vowel sounds (e.g., *an apple*, *an hour*, *an honest man*, *an heir* where the letter *h* is silent)[cite: 107].
   - *Zero Article:* The deliberate absence of an article in general expressions (e.g., *We went by train*; *He is in hospital*; *She is at work*)[cite: 107].
2. **Demonstrative Determiners:** Point out specific nouns in space or time: *this*, *that*, *these*, *those* (*this cake*, *those flowers*, *that house*, *these students*)[cite: 107].
3. **Possessive Determiners:** Show ownership or connection: *my*, *our*, *your*, *his*, *her*, *its*, *their* (*my book*, *his car*, *their new bikes*, *its leg*)[cite: 107].
4. **Indefinite (General) Determiners:** Quantify nouns in an indefinite manner without naming exact amounts: *all*, *any*, *both*, *each*, *either*, *every*, *few*, *little*, *less*, *many*, *much*, *more*, *neither*, *no*, *several*, *some*, *enough*[cite: 107].
5. **Number Determiners:** Express quantities or positions[cite: 107]:
   - *Cardinal Numbers:* Express exact counting (*one*, *two*, *seven*, *sixty*, *five hundred*)[cite: 107].
   - *Ordinal Numbers:* Express sequence or ranking (*first*, *second*, *third*, *twenty-fifth*)[cite: 107].

### Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — Reading to Interpret Diagrams, Maps, and Sketches
**Reading Passage:**
> Reading comprehension extends beyond deciphering conventional printed sentences; it encompasses the critical ability to interpret graphical illustrations, directional maps, engineering sketches, and structural diagrams[cite: 107]. In our increasingly technical world, navigating through a bustling metropolitan transport interchange, understanding the floor plan of an architectural complex, or assembling a piece of mechanical laboratory apparatus relies entirely on visual literacy[cite: 107]. Diagrams condense vast volumes of intricate geographic and operational data into clear, spatial representations that can be grasped at a glance[cite: 107].
>
> When examining a geographic map or architectural sketch, a skilled reader must first consult the legend or key[cite: 107]. The key interprets the specialized cartographic symbols: a cluster of wavy blue lines signifies a river, a bold dashed line marks an administrative boundary, while triangular icons often represent towering mountain peaks[cite: 107]. Furthermore, paying close attention to the scale of the map enables the reader to calculate real-world physical distances accurately, while the cardinal compass rose indicates true orientation[cite: 107]. Misreading these graphical signposts can cause travelers to lose their bearings entirely.
>
> In secondary school and technical training, interpreting flowchart diagrams and scientific process sketches is equally indispensable[cite: 107]. A well-designed scientific flowchart illustrates how raw inputs undergo sequential transformations before yielding a finished product[cite: 107]. Directional arrows indicate the continuous flow of energy or materials, while rectangular blocks outline specific operational phases[cite: 107]. By learning to synthesize visual data alongside written prose, a student develops the analytical precision required to solve complex real-world problems efficiently[cite: 107].

**Guided Comprehension Questions & Model Responses:**
1. **Question:** Why does reading comprehension include the interpretation of diagrams, maps, and sketches[cite: 107]?
   - *Model Answer:* Comprehension includes visual materials because modern life requires the ability to navigate physical environments, understand architectural plans, and assemble equipment using visual literacy[cite: 107].
2. **Question:** What is the vital function of a legend or key on a geographic map[cite: 107]?
   - *Model Answer:* The legend or key explains the meanings of specialized symbols, colors, and markings used across the map[cite: 107].
3. **Question:** What two map features enable a reader to calculate physical distances and determine direction[cite: 107]?
   - *Model Answer:* The scale of the map allows the calculation of real-world distances, while the compass rose indicates directional orientation[cite: 107].
4. **Question:** In a scientific flowchart, what do directional arrows and rectangular blocks represent[cite: 107]?
   - *Model Answer:* Directional arrows represent the movement or flow of materials and energy, while rectangular blocks denote specific operational phases or steps[cite: 107].
5. **Question:** Identify the determiner in the phrase *"<u>those</u> green grapes"* and state its grammatical class[cite: 107].
   - *Model Answer:* *"Those"* is a demonstrative determiner pointing out specific plural nouns[cite: 107].

**Target Vocabulary in Context:**
- **Deciphering** (*Verb*): Interpreting, decoding, or making sense of written text, symbols, or obscure markings[cite: 107].
- **Cartographic** (*Adjective*): Relating to the scientific art, practice, or technique of drawing and interpreting maps[cite: 107].
- **Legend** (*Noun*): An explanatory table or list of symbols and keys accompanying an illustration, map, or chart[cite: 107].
- **Orientation** (*Noun*): The positioning or alignment of a map or object in relation to cardinal points of the compass[cite: 107].
- **Synthesize** (*Verb*): To combine diverse ideas, data, and visual information into a coherent, unified whole[cite: 107].
- **Indispensable** (*Adjective*): Absolutely essential, necessary, and impossible to manage without[cite: 107].
- **Intricate** (*Adjective*): Very complicated, detailed, or made of numerous interconnected elements[cite: 107].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay (Explaining a Process)
An **expository essay** explains a process, gives instructions, or clarifies how something works or operates[cite: 107]. While it shares descriptive features, its main purpose is to inform, instruct, and explain mechanisms or recipes systematically[cite: 107].
- **Core Features of Expository Writing:**
  - Written primarily in the **simple present tense** (*get*, *boil*, *mix*, *serve*)[cite: 107].
  - Demands high clarity, precision, and sequential logic[cite: 107].
  - Uses transitional time connectives (*first*, *subsequently*, *next*, *meanwhile*, *finally*)[cite: 107].
  - Common topics: *How to Prepare My Favourite Meal*, *How Bean Balls Are Produced from Beans*, *A Traditional Marriage Ceremony*, *The Way to the Staff Quarters from the School Gate*[cite: 107].
- **Standard Structural Blueprint:**
  1. **Title:** Stated clearly in bold or capital letters (e.g., *HOW TO PREPARE WHITE RICE AND TOMATO STEW*)[cite: 107].
  2. **Introduction:** Introduce the subject, define its significance, and name the requirements (e.g., stating why white rice and stew is a nutritious, popular dish)[cite: 107].
  3. **Body Paragraphs (Step-by-Step Explanation):**
     - *Step One:* Assembly and preparation of utensils and raw ingredients (rice, fresh tomatoes, peppers, onions, cooking oil, seasonings)[cite: 107].
     - *Step Two:* Parboiling and washing the rice to reduce starch, followed by boiling until tender[cite: 107].
     - *Step Three:* Blending, boiling, and frying the tomato and pepper base with seasoned meat or fish[cite: 107].
     - *Step Four:* Simmering the stew with appropriate spices until the oil rises cleanly to the top[cite: 107].
  4. **Conclusion:** Summarize how the meal should be served (hot, garnished with vegetables) and state a concluding remark on its dietary value[cite: 107].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Figures of Speech: Simile and Metaphor
Figures of speech are imaginative linguistic devices used by poets, playwrights, and novelists to create vivid mental images, elevate emotional tone, and deepen meaning[cite: 107]:
- **Simile:** A figure of speech that compares two fundamentally different things that share a notable characteristic, using explicit connective words such as **"like"** or **"as"**[cite: 107].
  - *Alaba's shirt is as white as snow*[cite: 107].
  - *He swims like a fish*[cite: 107].
  - *On our entering the premises, it was as quiet as a graveyard*[cite: 107].
  - *The old warrior fights like a lion*[cite: 107].
- **Metaphor:** A direct comparison between two distinct things without the use of "like" or "as", asserting that one thing *is* another in order to transfer qualities between them[cite: 107].
  - *Ade was a lion in the fight* (he possessed the fierce courage of a lion)[cite: 107].
  - *My elder sister is the pillar of our family* (she single-handedly sustains and carries the burdens of the household)[cite: 107].
  - *Jemima is the apple of her father's eyes* (she is cherished above all others)[cite: 107].
  - *The striker is the shining light in the school football team* (he serves as the prime source of inspiration and guidance)[cite: 107].

  Title: Cardinal Numbers vs Ordinal Numbers
  Body: Cardinal numbers denote exact numerical quantity or counting (*one*, *two*, *seven*), while ordinal numbers denote sequential ranking or position (*first*, *second*, *twenty-fifth*)[cite: 107].
- Label: Figurative Comparison
  Title: Simile vs Metaphor
  Body: A simile states that one object is *like* or *as* another using connective words (*swims like a fish*), whereas a metaphor makes a direct equation without connectives (*Ade was a lion in the fight*)[cite: 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using the indefinite article "an" before words starting with the letter *u* that are pronounced with an initial consonant glide /j/[cite: 107].
  Fact: The choice between *a* and *an* depends on sound, not spelling; words like *unit*, *university*, and *union* begin with the consonant sound /j/ and take **a** (*a unit*, not *an unit*)[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Using the indefinite article "a" before words starting with a silent letter *h*[cite: 107].
  Fact: In words like *hour*, *heir*, and *honest*, the initial *h* is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound and requires **an** (*an hour*, *an honest man*)[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Writing an expository recipe or process in the past tense like a story[cite: 107].
  Fact: Expository writing explains timeless or general procedures and must be written in the **simple present tense** (*wash the rice*, *boil the water*)[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Calling a direct metaphor a simile simply because two things are compared[cite: 107].
  Fact: A comparison is only a simile if it explicitly uses connective markers such as *like* or *as*; without those words, it is a metaphor[cite: 107].
