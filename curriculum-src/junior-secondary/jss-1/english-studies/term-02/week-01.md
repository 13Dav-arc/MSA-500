## Key Terms
- Term: Labio-Dental Fricative
  Definition: A consonant sound produced by bringing the upper front teeth into light contact with the lower lip, causing audible friction as the airstream passes through[cite: 107].
- Term: Adverb
  Definition: A word or group of words that modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb by providing details about manner, time, place, frequency, degree, or duration[cite: 107].
- Term: Diligence

- Term: Genre
  Definition: A recognized category, class, or variety of literature characterized by a distinctive style, form, or purpose, specifically Prose, Drama, and Poetry[cite: 107].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /f/ and /v/
Consonants **/f/** and **/v/** are labio-dental fricative sounds articulated by bringing the front upper teeth into partial contact with the lower lip, creating a continuous frictional noise as the airstream escapes[cite: 107]:
- **/f/** is a **voiceless** consonant sound because the glottis is wide open and the vocal cords do not vibrate during production[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbols for /f/: `f` (*feet*, *fun*, *float*), `ph` (*Physics*, *phase*), `gh` (*laugh*, *cough*), and `ff` (*staff*)[cite: 107].
- **/v/** is a **voiced** consonant sound because the vocal cords vibrate noticeably as the airstream escapes between the lower lip and upper front teeth[cite: 107].
  - Common spelling symbol for /v/: `v` (*vane*, *vast*, *voice*, *weave*, *starve*), with the notable exception of `ph` in *Stephen*[cite: 107].

| Position | Voiceless /f/ | Voiced /v/ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Initial** | *fine*, *fast*, *fan*, *few*, *fry*, *flow*[cite: 107] | *vine*, *vast*, *van*, *view*, *verse*, *veal*[cite: 107] |
| **Medial** | *wafer*, *refuse*, *effort*, *defend* | *waiver*, *lever*, *every*, *divide*[cite: 107] |
| **Final** | *safe*, *staff*, *serf*, *belief*, *loaf*, *grief*[cite: 107] | *save*, *starve*, *serve*, *believe*, *naive*, *weave*[cite: 107] |

### Aspect 2: Structure — Adverbs: Forms and Functions
The primary function of an **adverb** is to give more information about (modify) a verb by limiting or describing the action[cite: 107]. Adverbs can also modify other adverbs (*extremely* suddenly), adjectives (*gravely* ill), prepositions (*just* after dinner), and conjunctions (*exactly* what he said)[cite: 107]. When a group of words performs the function of an adverb, it is called an **adverbial phrase** or **adverbial**[cite: 107].

**Types of Adverbs:**
1. **Adverbs of Manner:** Indicate *how* or the circumstances in which something happens, frequently formed by adding *-ly* to an adjective (e.g., She was *neatly* dressed; They spoke *eloquently*; We waited *patiently*)[cite: 107].
2. **Adverbs of Time:** Indicate *when* something happens (e.g., I'll see you *soon*; It was her birthday *today*; *Afterwards*, we had afternoon tea)[cite: 107].
3. **Adverbs of Frequency:** Indicate *how frequently* something occurs (e.g., It *always* snowed; We *rarely* meet; He *never* drinks alcohol; We play tennis *regularly*)[cite: 107].
4. **Adverbs of Duration:** Indicate *how long* something lasts (e.g., She is living with her grandmother *temporarily*; He has been suspended *indefinitely*; They stopped *briefly*)[cite: 107].
5. **Adverbs of Place:** Indicate *where* something takes place (e.g., He has gone *overseas*; They live *nearby*; She went *inside*; We waded *ashore*)[cite: 107].
6. **Adverbs of Degree & Emphasis:** Indicate the *extent* or intensity of an action (e.g., I enjoyed the film *immensely*; She was *slightly* hurt; We *totally* disagree; I was *utterly* devastated)[cite: 107].
7. **Interrogative Adverbs:** *Wh-* words used to ask questions (e.g., *When* did you last see him? *Where* was the money hidden? *How* are you feeling? *Why* was he there?)[cite: 107].

### Aspect 4: Composition — More on Narrative Composition (Paragraph Development)
A **narrative composition** is an essay in which the writer tells a story or gives an orderly account of events that have happened[cite: 107]. Whether the story is true or imagined, it must be plausible (believable and true to life)[cite: 107].
- **Verb Tense Rule:** The primary tense used in narrative writing is the **simple past tense**, supported occasionally by the past continuous or past perfect tense, with careful attention to tense sequence in reported speech[cite: 107].
- **Four Pillars of a Good Narrative Essay:**
  1. **Content:** Present a detailed, engaging sequence of events addressing *what* happened, *who* was involved, *when* and *where* it occurred, *how* it unfolded, and the *consequences*[cite: 107].
  2. **Organization:** Begin with an introductory paragraph establishing the time, place, scene, and key characters; develop the episode chronologically across well-linked body paragraphs; and end with a brief, memorable conclusion[cite: 107].
  3. **Expression:** Choose vivid, appropriate vocabulary and adverbs that clearly convey the action to the reader[cite: 107].
  4. **Conclusion:** Round off the essay in line with the topic using a reflection on what was gained, a proverb, or how you felt about the outcome[cite: 107].
- **Common Narrative Topics:** *My First Day at School*; *A Day I Will Never Forget in My Life*; *My First Journey to Lagos*; *How I Spent My Last Holiday*[cite: 107].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Review on the Genres of Literature
The **genres of literature** are the major varieties or categories of literary composition: **Prose**, **Drama**, and **Poetry**[cite: 107]:
1. **Prose:** A free-flowing, extended narrative written in ordinary grammatical structure (sentences, paragraphs, and chapters)[cite: 107]. It is divided into **fiction** (novels, short stories) and **non-fiction** (biographies, essays)[cite: 107]. Someone who writes a prose work or novel is called a **novelist** or **author** (e.g., *The Costly Mistake* by Chinelo Ifezulike)[cite: 107].
2. **Drama:** A story written in **dialogue form** to be performed on stage by actors before an audience, projecting human conflict through speech and action[cite: 107]. Its major sub-genres include **tragedy** and **comedy** (such as farce, satire, and comedy of manners)[cite: 107]. Someone who writes a drama is called a **playwright** (e.g., *Vengeance of the Spirit* by Maxwell Onyeka)[cite: 107].
3. **Poetry:** An imaginative rendition that gives powerful expression to experiences, ideas, and feelings in condensed, rhythmic, and metaphoric language written in verse (lines and stanzas)[cite: 107]. Sub-genres include epic, lyric, sonnet, and ode (e.g., the poem *"Nightfall"* by Oiwona Andrew)[cite: 107].

## Comparative Matrix
Heading: Comparing Core Linguistic and Literary Contrasts
- Label: Phonetic Voicing
  Title: Consonant /f/ vs Consonant /v/
  Body: Both are labio-dental fricatives produced with the upper teeth and lower lip, but /f/ is voiceless (no vocal cord vibration, as in *safe* and *fan*) whereas /v/ is voiced (vocal cords vibrate, as in *save* and *van*)[cite: 107].
- Label: Temporal vs Spatial Modification
  Title: Adverbs of Duration vs Adverbs of Frequency
  Body: Adverbs of duration indicate how long an action continues (*temporarily*, *indefinitely*, *overnight*), whereas adverbs of frequency indicate how often an action repeats (*always*, *regularly*, *seldom*)[cite: 107].
- Label: Literary Presentation
  Title: Prose vs Drama vs Poetry
  Body: Prose flows in continuous paragraphs and chapters (*The Costly Mistake*), Drama unfolds through stage dialogue and action (*Vengeance of the Spirit*), and Poetry uses condensed verse and metaphoric rhythm (*"Nightfall"*)[cite: 107].
- Label: Prose Sub-Genres
  Title: Fiction vs Non-Fiction
  Body: Fiction consists of imaginative narratives invented by a novelist (such as novels and short stories), while non-fiction recounts factual real-life people and events (such as biographies and essays)[cite: 107].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Confusing the spelling and pronunciation of final /f/ and /v/ in noun-verb pairs like *belief/believe*, *safe/save*, and *grief/grieve*[cite: 107].
  Fact: Nouns like *belief* and *grief* end with the voiceless sound /f/, whereas verbs like *believe* and *save* end with the voiced sound /v/[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Assuming that every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb of manner[cite: 107].
  Fact: While many adverbs of manner end in *-ly* (*neatly*, *quickly*), several *-ly* words are adverbs of time (*lately*), frequency (*regularly*, *rarely*), duration (*temporarily*, *briefly*), or degree (*immensely*, *partly*)[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Writing a narrative composition in the simple present tense or switching tenses randomly mid-paragraph[cite: 107].
  Fact: Narrative essays recount events that have already happened and must be anchored consistently in the past tense[cite: 107].
- Mistake: Referring to the author of a stage play as a "novelist" or spelling playwright as "playwrite"[cite: 107].
  Fact: A novelist writes prose novels, whereas a writer of drama is a **playwright**[cite: 107].
