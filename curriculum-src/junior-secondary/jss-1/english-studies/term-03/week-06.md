## Hook
When speaking in public, drafting a feature article for your school magazine, or reciting a moving poem about maternal love, your mastery of linguistic stress and structural emphasis shapes your impact on the audience[cite: 2]. Shifting stress from the first syllable to the second can turn a person, like a **DOC-tor**, into an action, like to **pro-TECT**[cite: 2]. Using the passive voice allows you to highlight critical events—such as noting that a holy journey *has been completed* or a prize *was awarded*—without cluttering the sentence with unnecessary details[cite: 2]. Blending grammatical awareness with vivid poetic devices like apostrophe, personification, and synecdoche equips you to write and speak with clarity and emotional resonance[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Stress Pattern
  Definition: The established, customary placement of vocal prominence or primary stress on a particular syllable across different grammatical word classes[cite: 2].
- Term: Agentless Passive
  Definition: A passive construction in which the doer or performer of the action is intentionally omitted because the agent is unknown, obvious, or unimportant[cite: 2].
- Term: Pilgrimage
  Definition: A sacred spiritual journey undertaken to a holy shrine or religious site, such as the Islamic Hajj to Mecca, to fulfill spiritual obligations[cite: 2].
- Term: Magazine Feature Article
  Definition: An informative, expository piece of continuous writing composed for publication in a school or community periodical to analyze social issues systematically[cite: 2].
- Term: Apostrophe
  Definition: A figure of speech in which a speaker or poet directly addresses an absent person, an inanimate object, or an abstract idea as if it were present, alive, and capable of responding[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Stress Patterns in Two-Syllable Words
In English, **stress pattern** refers to the conventional position of primary stress in multi-syllabic words[cite: 2]. In disyllabic (two-syllable) words, stress distribution often distinguishes grammatical word classes[cite: 2]:
1. **Disyllabic Nouns (First Syllable Stressed):**
   - The majority of two-syllable nouns take primary stress on their **first syllable**[cite: 2].
   - Examples: **STU-dent**, **PIC-ture**, **MO-ther**, **TEA-cher**, **FA-ther**, **BUt-cher**, **TAI-lor**, **VEN-dor**, **TRA-der**, **DOC-tor**, **WOR-ker**, **DRI-ver**, **BUIL-der**, **FAR-mer**, **LAW-yer**[cite: 2].
2. **Disyllabic Verbs (Second Syllable Stressed):**
   - In two-syllable verbs, primary stress commonly shifts to the **second syllable**[cite: 2].
   - Examples: de-**PEND**, a-**CHIEVE**, pro-**TECT**, ex-**PLAIN**, in-**SULT**, ex-**PORT**, ap-**PROACH**[cite: 2].
3. **Weak Syllables:**
   - Any syllable that contains a reduced vowel sound (such as schwa /ə/) or weak /ɪ/ is generally unstressed and spoken quickly with reduced breath force[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Verbs: Passive Voice Constructions (With and Without By-Agent)
A sentence is in the **passive voice** when the grammatical subject is acted upon by the verb rather than performing the action[cite: 2].
- In many passive constructions, the doer is indicated using a prepositional phrase introduced by **by**[cite: 2]:
  - *The injured man was helped by a passer-by*[cite: 2].
  - *The man was being questioned by the police*[cite: 2].
  - *The patient was operated on by a team of five surgeons*[cite: 2].
  - *My brother was given extra tuition by his teacher*[cite: 2].
- **Agentless Passives (Passive Without "By"):**
  When the identity of the performer is unknown, obvious from context, general, or unimportant, the by-agent phrase is omitted[cite: 2]:
  - *The window was broken* (the culprit is unknown)[cite: 2].
  - *The safe was blown open with dynamite* (focuses on the criminal outcome rather than the identity of the thieves)[cite: 2].
  - *The sorting is done by machine* (instrumental focus)[cite: 2].
  - *Roger has been given his promotion* (the appointing committee is obvious)[cite: 2].
  - *An elderly man was run over while crossing the road*[cite: 2].
  - *I was showered with presents on my eighteenth birthday*[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Writing for Publication (School Magazine Article)
Writing an expository article for publication in your school magazine requires presenting verified facts, detailed characteristics, and methods of solving social issues in an objective, orderly manner[cite: 2].
- **Key Formatting Rules for Magazine Articles:**
  - Do **NOT** write personal postal addresses, formal salutations (such as *Dear Sir*), or complimentary closes (such as *Yours faithfully*)[cite: 2].
  - The composition must carry an eye-catching, informative title followed by a byline (e.g., *By: [Your Name], JSS 1B*)[cite: 2].
  - The essay must proceed in coherent, well-linked prose paragraphs following a logical progression[cite: 2].
- **Model Topic: "The Evil of Unemployment in My Country"[cite: 2]:**
  - **Title:** Written boldly at the top (e.g., *THE MENACE OF UNEMPLOYMENT: CAUSES, CONSEQUENCES, AND REMEDIES*)[cite: 2].
  - **Introduction:** Defines unemployment and exposes its prevalence among energetic educated youths across urban and rural communities[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 1 (Security Challenges):** Discusses how idleness and financial frustration push jobless youths into violent crimes, armed robbery, pipeline vandalism, and thuggery[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 2 (Social Vices):** Details the surge in substance addiction, prostitution, and hired assassination fueled by economic hopelessness[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 3 (Domestic & Health Crises):** Analyzes the breakdown of marriages, disease affliction, chronic malnutrition, and depression leading to suicide[cite: 2].
  - **Conclusion:** Proposes practical remedies, including vocational technical training, agricultural funding, and industrial revival[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Figures of Speech in Akeem Ajibade's "Let My Mother Sing"
Poets deploy figures of speech to express complex ideas and deep emotions vividly[cite: 2]. In Akeem Ajibade’s poem *"Let My Mother Sing"*, the poet celebrates the sacrificial nature and enduring dignity of motherhood[cite: 2].

**Major Poetic Devices and Figures of Speech in the Recommended Text:**
1. **Simile:** Directly comparing two different things using connective words like *"like"* or *"as"* (e.g., *Her gentle voice is like morning dew*)[cite: 2].
2. **Metaphor:** An implied comparison that treats two distinct things as one without using "like" or "as" (e.g., *A mother is the radiant sun of the household*)[cite: 2].
3. **Personification:** Attributing human life, emotions, or actions to inanimate objects or non-human entities (e.g., *The restless wind wept through the night*)[cite: 2].
4. **Apostrophe:** Addressing an absent person, a deceased relative, or an inanimate object directly as if listening (e.g., *O Mother, hear your children's grateful chorus!*)[cite: 2].
5. **Symbolism:** An image or concrete object that represents a deeper, abstract meaning beyond its literal sense (e.g., a *crown* symbolizing royal authority; a *nursing bowl* symbolizing maternal nurture)[cite: 2].
6. **Repetition:** The deliberate recurring use of words, phrases, or full lines to create musical rhythm and emphasize central emotions (e.g., *Let my mother sing, let her sweet voice rise*)[cite: 2].
7. **Synecdoche:** A figure of speech in which a part of something represents the whole, or the whole represents a part (e.g., *"All hands on deck"* where *hands* represents the entire crew of workers)[cite: 2].

- Label: Direct Address
  Title: Personification vs Apostrophe
  Body: Personification gives human attributes to non-human things (*The flowers danced*)[cite: 2], while apostrophe directly addresses an absent person or inanimate object as if they were present to hear (*O death, where is thy sting?*)[cite: 2].
- Label: Figurative Representation
  Title: Metaphor vs Synecdoche
  Body: A metaphor equates two fundamentally different things (*Life is a battlefield*)[cite: 2], while synecdoche uses an actual physical part of an entity to represent the entire whole (*All hands on deck*)[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Stressing the second syllable of two-syllable professional nouns (e.g., pronouncing *DOC-tor* as *doc-TOR*, or *TEA-cher* as *tea-CHER*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: In standard English, two-syllable nouns denoting professions and objects are stressed on the first syllable (**DOC-tor**, **TEA-cher**, **TAI-lor**)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Adding an unnecessary "by someone" phrase to every passive construction[cite: 2].
  Fact: When the performer is unknown or unimportant, omit the agent to create a concise, natural agentless passive (*The car was stolen*, not *The car was stolen by someone*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Including personal letters headers (addresses and salutations like *Dear Sir*) when writing an article for a school magazine[cite: 2].
  Fact: Magazine articles require an eye-catching title and byline, opening directly with the introductory paragraph without letter formatting[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Confusing apostrophe (the figure of speech) with apostrophe (the punctuation mark)[cite: 1, 2].
  Fact: The punctuation mark `'` indicates omission or possession[cite: 1], whereas the figure of speech *apostrophe* is a poetic direct address to an absent entity[cite: 2].

- **Explanation:** The physical part (*hands*) represents the entire body of workers or sailors summoned to work together[cite: 2].
