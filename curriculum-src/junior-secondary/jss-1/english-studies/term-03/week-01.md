## Hook
When you listen to a skilled storyteller, read a stirring poem, or report an incident clearly to a school authority, your voice depends on clean pronunciation and active, purposeful language[cite: 2]. Articulating the difference between /l/ and /r/ ensures that listeners never mistake a "clash" for a "crash" or confusing "flew" with "brew"[cite: 2]. Understanding how active verbs place the doer of an action at the center of the sentence gives energy and directness to your writing, whether you are analyzing the vital civic duties of the police, organizing a step-by-step expository explanation of a local game, or exploring how poets capture deep human emotions through verse[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Lateral Consonant
  Definition: A consonant sound articulated when the tongue tip contacts the alveolar ridge while air escapes freely around one or both sides of the tongue, specifically represented by /l/[cite: 2].
- Term: Active Voice
  Definition: A grammatical construction in which the grammatical subject of the clause is the actual performer or doer of the action expressed by the verb[cite: 2].
- Term: Prosecution
  Definition: The institution and conducting of legal proceedings against an individual accused of committing a criminal offense in a court of law[cite: 2].
- Term: Expository Essay
  Definition: A form of continuous writing that explains, informs, or instructs the reader on how something works, how something is done, or how something is made[cite: 2].
- Term: Anthology
  Definition: A published collection of poems or other literary pieces authored by one or multiple writers[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /l/ and /r/
Consonants **/l/** and **/r/** are voiced sonorants (liquids) produced without friction when articulated normally, but they require distinctly different tongue movements[cite: 2]:
- **/l/** is a **lateral** voiced consonant sound[cite: 2]. The tip of the tongue makes firm contact with the alveolar ridge, blocking the center of the vocal tract while air flows freely over the sides of the tongue[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples: *leak*, *line*, *lead*, *lemon*, *fill*, *middle*, *shall*, *silly*[cite: 2].
  - **Silent "l":** The letter "l" is completely silent in many common English words when preceded by certain vowels: *talk*, *walk*, *yolk*, *could*, *should*, *folk*, *palm*, *half*, *calm*[cite: 2].
- **/r/** is a **post-alveolar continuant** voiced sound[cite: 2]. The tip of the tongue curls upward very near to the alveolar ridge without actually touching it, allowing the voiced airstream to flow through over the center of the tongue without lateral escape[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples: *rate*, *rock*, *rag*, *rank*, *robe*, *race*, *rake*, *rug*, *range*, *ferry*, *rib*, *parade*, *barrel*[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Verbs: The Active Voice
In English grammar, **voice** indicates the relationship between the action expressed by the verb and the participants identified by the sentence arguments[cite: 2]. A verb is in the **active voice** when the subject of the sentence performs the action[cite: 2].
- The action flows directly from the **subject** (the doer or performer) across the verb to the **object** (the receiver of the action)[cite: 2].
- In an active sentence, the **logical subject** (the person or thing performing the action) is identical to the **grammatical subject** (the noun or pronoun appearing before the verb)[cite: 2].
- **Core Active Sentence Pattern:** $\text{Subject} + \text{Active Verb} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
  - *Paul wrote a letter* (Paul is the doer of the writing)[cite: 2].
  - *The dog chased the postman* (The dog is performing the chase)[cite: 2].
  - *Obi cleaned the blackboard* (Obi performed the cleaning)[cite: 2].
  - *The driver drove the car carelessly*[cite: 2].
  - *Bola sang the song melodiously*[cite: 2].
  - *Ade killed a goat*[cite: 2].
  - *Aisha ate the food*[cite: 2].
  - *The police must arrest that thief*[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Introduction and Brainstorming
An **expository essay** is a composition in which the writer explains how something works, how something is done, or how something is made[cite: 2]. It addresses the fundamental *"how"* of concepts, mechanisms, games, or processes, striving to inform and instruct the reader through clear, systematic facts[cite: 2].
- **Brainstorming Before Writing:**
  - Before writing, an author must understand the subject thoroughly and engage in deep thinking (brainstorming) to identify every necessary step[cite: 2].
  - List all raw materials, equipment, participants, rules, or requirements in rough notes.
  - Re-arrange the itemized points into a strict, logical sequence where no prerequisite step is skipped[cite: 2].
- **Elements of Expository Writing:**
  1. **Title:** Clearly stated at the top (e.g., *HOW TO PLAY THE TRADITIONAL GAME OF AYO*, or *HOW TO PREPARE A LOCAL MEAL*)[cite: 2].
  2. **Introduction:** Defines the game, process, or concept, stating its cultural or practical origin, the number of participants required, and the essential equipment[cite: 2].
  3. **Body Paragraphs:** Systematically explains the rules and chronological stages of the process using simple present tense verbs and precise transition markers (*first*, *secondly*, *subsequently*, *meanwhile*)[cite: 2].
  4. **Conclusion:** Summarizes the objective, how a winner is determined or how the finished product is enjoyed, and its benefits[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Definitions, Elements, and Types
A **poem** is a piece of creative, imaginative writing arranged in verse and stanzas[cite: 2]. **Poetry** refers to the art, study, or entire collection of poems, while a published compilation of poems by one or more authors is known as an **anthology**[cite: 2]. A person who writes poems is a **poet**[cite: 2].

**Core Elements of Poetic Language:**
- **Imagery:** Mental sensory pictures created in poetry through figures of speech (such as similes, metaphors, and personification)[cite: 2].
- **Rhythm:** The musical cadence or beat of poetry created by the deliberate alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables[cite: 2].
- **Sound:** The acoustic effects produced by vowels and consonants in vocal utterance, including rhyme, alliteration, and onomatopoeia[cite: 2].

**Major Types of Poems:**
1. **Epic:** A very long narrative poem detailing the extraordinary adventures and achievements of heroic legendary figures[cite: 2].
2. **Ode:** A dignified, lyrical poem composed in praise of or addressed to a specific person, event, or object[cite: 2].
3. **Dirge:** A short, mournful song or poem expressing grief and lamentation for the dead, usually chanted at funerals[cite: 2].
4. **Sonnet:** A lyric poem consisting of exactly **fourteen lines** that follow a strict, fixed rhyme scheme[cite: 2].
5. **Ballad:** A narrative folk poem composed in short stanzas with a recurring refrain, telling a dramatic story often meant to be sung[cite: 2].
6. **Elegy:** A melancholy, contemplative poem reflecting sadly upon someone who has died or pondering human mortality[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Confusing /l/ and /r/ in speech (e.g., pronouncing *pray* as *play*, or *bleed* as *breed*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: For /l/, make firm tongue-tip contact with the upper gum ridge[cite: 2]; for /r/, keep the tongue tip raised close to the palate without making physical contact[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Pronouncing the letter "l" in words like *calm*, *palm*, *talk*, and *walk*[cite: 2].
  Fact: In these words, the letter "l" is completely silent; pronounce them as /kɑːm/, /pɑːm/, /tɔːk/, and /wɔːk/[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Mistaking an active sentence for a passive sentence when an action is forceful[cite: 2].
  Fact: Voice does not depend on the violence of the action; if the grammatical subject performs the verb (*The dog chased the postman*), the sentence is active[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Writing an expository essay as a past-tense personal narrative story[cite: 2].
  Fact: An expository essay explains a general process or instructions and must be written primarily in the simple present tense[cite: 2].

- **Subject:** *The driver* (the person performing the action)[cite: 2].

- **Object:** *the car* (the entity receiving the driving action)[cite: 2].
- **Voice:** The verb is in the **Active Voice** because the grammatical subject is the doer of the action[cite: 2].
