## Hook
When reading an English text aloud, our eyes often encounter letters that our voices must never speak, from the silent *b* in *climb* to the quiet *k* in *knowledge*[cite: 2]. Mastering silent letters ensures you sound polished and educated, while controlling the passive voice across multiple tenses lets you report events with precise timing and focus, whether the subject is scoring a goal or an essay is being written[cite: 2]. When you combine that grammatical agility with sequential paragraph linkers to analyze issues like unemployment and explore poetry confronting social prejudice, you develop the comprehensive command needed for mature expression[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Silent Letter
  Definition: An alphabetic letter that appears in the written spelling of a word but is completely omitted during oral pronunciation, such as *b* in *comb* or *k* in *know*[cite: 2].
- Term: Tense-Voice Concord
  Definition: The systematic grammatical rule ensuring that when an active sentence transforms into the passive voice, the auxiliary verb *to be* matches the exact tense of the original active verb[cite: 2].
- Term: Delinquency
  Definition: Minor criminal, antisocial, or disruptive conduct committed by young individuals or students that violates school regulations and community norms[cite: 2].
- Term: Paragraph Linkers
  Definition: Transitional words and cohesive connective phrases (such as *however*, *meanwhile*, *in addition*, *apart from these*) that guide readers smoothly from one argument to the next[cite: 2].
- Term: Social Commentary Verse
  Definition: Poetry written with the explicit thematic purpose of exposing, criticizing, and correcting social vices, injustices, or prejudices within human society, such as racial discrimination[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Nasal Consonants and Silent Letters (mb, mn, kn, gn)
English spelling often retains historical letters that have ceased to be pronounced in modern speech[cite: 2]. Understanding the distribution of silent letters prevents pronunciation errors:
- **Silent "b" in final *-mb* clusters:** When the letter *b* follows *m* at the end of a word or root syllable, the end sound is /m/; the *b* is completely silent[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples ending in /m/: *climb*, *comb*, *dumb*, *plumb*, *tomb*, *bomb*, *numb*, *lamb*, *crumb*[cite: 2].
- **Silent "n" in final *-mn* clusters:** When *n* follows *m* at the end of a word, the sound /m/ is articulated while *n* remains silent[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples ending in /m/: *damn*, *hymn*, *condemn*, *solemn*, *column*[cite: 2].
- **Silent "k" in initial *kn-* clusters:** When *k* precedes *n* at the beginning of a word, the *k* is completely silent and only /n/ is pronounced[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples starting with /n/: *know*, *knowledge*, *knot*, *knew*, *knit*, *knee*, *knife*[cite: 2].
- **Silent "g" in initial *gn-* clusters:** When *g* is placed before *n* at word beginnings, the *g* is silent while /n/ is pronounced[cite: 2].
  - Pronounced examples starting with /n/: *gnash*, *gnaw*, *gnat*, *gnome*[cite: 2].
- **Regular Nasals:** In standard positions without silent pairings, /m/ and /n/ are voiced nasal sonorants (e.g., *manner*, *banner*, *nanny*, *funnel*, *cranny*, *grammar*, *commission*)[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Active and Passive Voice Across Tenses
When converting a sentence from active to passive voice, the auxiliary verb *to be* must adopt the exact tense and aspect of the original active verb, followed immediately by the **past participle** of the main verb[cite: 2]:

1. **Present Simple Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{Base Verb} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{is/are} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I kick a ball*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *A ball is kicked by me*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *He writes two letters*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *Two letters are written by him*[cite: 2].
2. **Past Simple Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{Past Verb} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{was/were} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I kicked the ball*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *The ball was kicked by me*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *The boy wrote a letter*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *A letter was written by the boy*[cite: 2].
3. **Present Continuous Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{am/is/are} + \text{-ing Verb} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{am/is/are} + \text{being} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I am kicking a ball*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *A ball is being kicked by me*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *He is writing two letters*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *Two letters are being written by him*[cite: 2].
4. **Present Perfect Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{have/has} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{have/has} + \text{been} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I have kicked the ball*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *The ball has been kicked by me*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *He has written two letters*[cite: 2] $\rightarrow$ *Passive:* *Two letters have been written by him*[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Logical Sequencing and Paragraph Linkers
An **expository essay** explains how things work, analyzes societal phenomena, or instructs readers through a systematic breakdown of facts[cite: 2]. To ensure coherence, paragraphs must be arranged in a logical sequence, unified by **paragraph linkers** (transitional devices)[cite: 2]:
- **Brainstorming and Outlining:**
  - Understand the topic thoroughly and brainstorm key issues into an itemized list[cite: 2].
  - Group related ideas together and re-arrange them in logical order, ensuring that each distinct point occupies its own well-developed paragraph[cite: 2].
- **Essential Paragraph Linkers:**
  - *Addition:* *Also*, *In addition to these*, *Apart from these*, *Furthermore*[cite: 2].
  - *Contrast:* *However*, *On the other hand*, *Nevertheless*, *But*[cite: 2].
  - *Time & Concurrence:* *Meanwhile*, *Subsequently*, *At the same time*[cite: 2].
- **Model Topic Outline: "The Evil of Unemployment in My Country"[cite: 2]:**
  - **Title:** Centered at the top in bold or capital letters[cite: 2].
  - **Introduction:** Defines unemployment (the inability of qualified, energetic citizens to secure gainful work) and states its national prevalence[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 1 (Crime & Insecurity):** Analyzes how idleness drives frustrated youths into armed robbery, thuggery, and hired assassination (*Paragraph linker: "First and foremost..."*)[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 2 (Social Vices):** Discusses the rise of prostitution, substance abuse, and moral degradation among jobless citizens (*Paragraph linker: "In addition to these security challenges..."*)[cite: 2].
  - **Body Paragraph 3 (Health & Family Breakdown):** Details the psychological affliction of diseases, malnutrition, broken marriages, and premature death/suicide (*Paragraph linker: "Apart from these social vices, unemployment inflicts severe domestic devastation..."*)[cite: 2].
  - **Conclusion:** Recommends viable solutions, such as vocational education, agricultural empowerment, and industrial expansion, summarizing the overall message[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Pronouncing the letter "b" in words ending in *-mb* (e.g., pronouncing *plumb* as /plʌmb/ or *climb* as /klaɪmb/)[cite: 2].
  Fact: When "b" follows "m" at the end of a word, it is silent; pronounce them cleanly as /plʌm/ and /klaɪm/[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Omitting the continuous auxiliary "being" when converting continuous tenses into the passive voice[cite: 2].
  Fact: Present continuous active (*is writing*) must become *is being written* in the passive; omitting *being* erroneously converts the tense to simple present (*is written*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Failing to use transitional linkers between body paragraphs in an expository essay[cite: 2].
  Fact: Paragraphs should open with linking phrases like *Furthermore*, *In addition to these*, or *However* to preserve logical flow[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Assuming that silent "n" in *-mn* applies at the beginning of words[cite: 2].
  Fact: The letter "n" is only silent when preceded by "m" at the end of a word (*hymn*, *condemn*)[cite: 2]; initial "n" is always pronounced (*name*, *note*)[cite: 2].
