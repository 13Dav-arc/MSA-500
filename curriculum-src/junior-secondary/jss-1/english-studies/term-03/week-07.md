## Hook
When conversing with friends, seeking confirmation, or presenting instructions, the rhythm of your words and the precision of your grammatical tags dictate whether your message is understood or questioned[cite: 2]. Pronouncing three-syllable words with their correct primary stress ensures you are never misunderstood when expressing an opinion in a **dis-CUS-sion** or seeking an **af-ter-NOON** appointment[cite: 2]. Using question tags allows you to verify facts politely and maintain conversational flow[cite: 2]. When coupled with civic reflections on obedience, step-by-step food process descriptions, and the figurative devices of poetry, your communicative toolkit becomes sharp, persuasive, and engaging[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Polysyllabic Stress
  Definition: The placement of primary vocal prominence or breath emphasis on a designated syllable in words containing three or more syllables[cite: 2].
- Term: Question Tag
  Definition: A brief interrogative clause tagged onto the end of a declarative statement to seek confirmation, agreement, or verification from the listener[cite: 2].
- Term: Civic Obedience
  Definition: The willing compliance with lawful regulations, ethical principles, and constituted authority necessary to preserve peace, order, and progress in society[cite: 2].
- Term: Guided Composition
  Definition: An essay developed systematically using guiding questions, topic outlines, and structured prompts arranged into coherent paragraphs[cite: 2].
- Term: Symbolism
  Definition: A literary device where an object, person, or situation represents a deeper, abstract meaning beyond its literal interpretation[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Stress in Three-Syllable Words
In English, words of three syllables distribute stress across different positions depending on their morphological structure and historical roots[cite: 2]. Unlike two-syllable words where stress predominantly falls on the first or second syllable, three-syllable words can bear primary stress on the **first**, **second**, or **third** syllable[cite: 2]:

1. **Stress on the First Syllable (Antepenultimate Stress):**
   - In many English three-syllable nouns, adjectives, and adverbs, the primary stress falls on the **first syllable**[cite: 2].
   - Examples: **DE-li-cate**, **FOR-tu-nate**, **PRE-sent-ly**, **CHA-rac-ter**, **BA-che-lor**[cite: 2].
2. **Stress on the Second Syllable (Penultimate Stress):**
   - In words featuring specific prefixes, suffixes, or Latinate roots, the primary stress falls on the **second syllable**[cite: 2].
   - Examples: **dis-CUS-sion**, **pro-JEC-tion**, **oc-CA-sion**, **stra-TE-gic**, **co-LO-ni-al**[cite: 2].
3. **Stress on the Third Syllable (Ultimate Stress):**
   - Certain loanwords, compound constructions, and prefixed words bear their strongest prominence on the **final (third) syllable**[cite: 2].
   - Examples: **dis-a-GREE**, **dis-be-LIEF**, **af-ter-NOON**, **re-fu-GEE**, **un-der-STAND**[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Question Tags: Polarity and Concord Rules
A **question tag** is a short question added to the end of a statement[cite: 2]. It converts a declarative sentence into an inquiry to confirm information or invite agreement[cite: 2].

**Fundamental Rules Governing Question Tags:**
1. **Rule of Opposing Polarity:**
   - A **positive statement** takes a **negative tag**[cite: 2]:
     - *You are leaving now, **aren't you?***[cite: 2]
     - *Sola took the book, **didn't she?***[cite: 2]
     - *She is here, **isn't she?***[cite: 2]
   - A **negative statement** takes a **positive tag**[cite: 2]:
     - *You are not leaving now, **are you?***[cite: 2]
     - *The boys are not leaving yet, **are they?***[cite: 2]
2. **Pronoun and Auxiliary Concord:**
   - The auxiliary verb in the statement is repeated in the tag (or replaced by *do/does/did* for simple tense verbs without auxiliaries)[cite: 2].
   - The subject pronoun of the statement is repeated in the tag[cite: 2].
3. **Special Use of the Pronoun "It":**
   - Use the neuter pronoun **"it"** in the tag when the subject refers to a little baby, an animal, an inanimate object, or an abstract noun[cite: 2]:
     - *The dog has eaten its food, **hasn't it?***[cite: 2]
     - *My baby is sleeping, **isn't it?***[cite: 2]
     - *A good idea is vital to progress, **isn't it?***[cite: 2]
     - *The rain is heavy today, **isn't it?***[cite: 2]
4. **Answering Tag Questions:**
   - Answers reflect factual reality rather than the grammatical phrasing of the tag[cite: 2]:
     - *You are leaving now, aren't you?* $\rightarrow$ **Yes, I am** (if leaving) or **No, I am not** (if staying)[cite: 2].
     - *The dog has eaten its food, hasn't it?* $\rightarrow$ **Yes, it has** or **No, it hasn't**[cite: 2].
     - *You bring them every day, don't you?* $\rightarrow$ **Yes, I do** or **No, I don't**[cite: 2].

### Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The Value of Obedience
**Reading Passage:**
> The paramount importance of obedience in the life of any individual, institution, or civilized society cannot be overstated[cite: 2]. Obedience is the moral adhesive that binds diverse human beings into harmonious communities governed by shared legal statutes and ethical norms[cite: 2]. Without collective obedience to constituted authority, societal structures rapidly disintegrate into violent chaos, unchecked banditry, and crippling disorder[cite: 2]. From the nuclear family unit to the classroom, and from corporate establishments to sovereign nations, disciplined compliance with established rules guarantees mutual protection and sustainable advancement[cite: 2].
>
> In an educational setting, a student who embraces prompt obedience toward school regulations and teachers creates an optimal atmosphere for intellectual growth[cite: 2]. Punctual arrival at morning assemblies, dedicated submission of assignments, and respectful decorum during lectures are not oppressive burdens designed to restrict youthful freedom[cite: 2]. Rather, they are character-building habits that shield learners from destructive peer distractions and academic failure[cite: 2]. Conversely, defiant students who celebrate disobedience find that their stubbornness alienates mentors, incurs severe disciplinary sanctions, and frequently culminates in derailed ambitions[cite: 2].
>
> In the wider world, obedience to civic responsibilities—such as obeying traffic signals, paying statutory taxes, and protecting public utilities—distinguishes prosperous societies from failing states[cite: 2]. Citizens who abide by traffic guidelines preserve lives, while merchants who obey commercial standards ensure fair economic transactions[cite: 2]. History consistently demonstrates that order yields tranquility, while rebellion breeds misery; therefore, every youth must recognize that disciplined obedience is not weakness, but the ultimate foundation of personal greatness and national honor[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Guided Composition ("The Food I Like Best")
A **guided expository composition** utilizes a series of structured prompting questions to assist the writer in developing a systematic, paragraph-by-paragraph account of a process, personal preference, or object[cite: 2].
- **Writing Constraint:** Do **NOT** number your points or write answers as fragmented list items; organize your responses into cohesive, well-developed prose paragraphs[cite: 2].
- **Guiding Prompts and Essay Structure for "The Food I Like Best"[cite: 2]:**
  1. **Title:** Boldly stated at the top (e.g., *THE FOOD I LIKE BEST: POUNDED YAM AND EGUSI SOUP*)[cite: 2].
  2. **Introduction (Identification & Source):** Which type of food do you enjoy most? Describe it generally, stating its agricultural origins (e.g., white yams, melon seeds) and cultural significance[cite: 2].
  3. **Body Paragraph 1 (Preparation Process):** How is the meal prepared? Detail the peeling, boiling, and pounding of the yam in a wooden mortar, alongside the blending and simmering of melon seeds with palm oil, leafy vegetables, and seasoned meats[cite: 2].
  4. **Body Paragraph 2 (Personal Affinity & Frequency):** Why do you prefer this meal over other foods, when did you first develop a taste for it, and how frequently do you consume it[cite: 2]?
  5. **Body Paragraph 3 (Nutritional Benefits):** What dietary value do you gain from it (e.g., carbohydrates for energy, proteins for tissue building, vitamins for immune health)[cite: 2]?
  6. **Conclusion (Recommendation):** Would you recommend this dish to friends or visitors to Nigeria? Summarize its appeal and conclude with a warm, inviting remark[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Poetic Devices in Recommended Poems
Poets employ figurative devices to express human experiences with striking imagery and musical cadence[cite: 2]. In recommended selections such as Akeem Ajibade's *"Let My Mother Sing"* and Sanyaolu's poem on racism, several major literary devices feature prominently[cite: 2]:
1. **Symbolism:** The use of concrete symbols to represent abstract concepts beyond their literal definitions (e.g., a *crown* symbolizing sovereign kingship; *chains* symbolizing racial oppression; *nursing arms* symbolizing maternal protection)[cite: 2].
2. **Apostrophe:** A figure of speech in which the speaker turns aside to directly address an absent person, a dead ancestor, or an inanimate object as though present and listening (e.g., *"O Mother, let your songs heal our weary souls!"*)[cite: 2].
3. **Synecdoche:** Using a part of something to stand for the whole entity, or the whole to represent a part (e.g., *"All hands on deck"*, where *hands* represents the entire workforce of persons)[cite: 2].
4. **Repetition:** The deliberate re-occurrence of specific words, phrases, or full lines to create musical emphasis, intensify emotional mood, and reinforce thematic resonance (e.g., *"He that will come, will come"*)[cite: 2].
5. **Personification:** Investing inanimate objects, animals, or abstract forces with human emotions and capabilities (e.g., *"The whispering night embraced the sleepy village"*)[cite: 1, 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Stressing the second syllable of words like "character" or "delicate" (e.g., pronouncing *cha-RAC-ter* instead of **CHA-rac-ter**)[cite: 2].
  Fact: In standard English, words like *character*, *delicate*, *fortunate*, and *bachelor* have primary stress on the first syllable (**CHA-rac-ter**, **DE-li-cate**)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Pairing a positive statement with a positive question tag (e.g., saying *"You are coming, are you?"* when seeking ordinary confirmation)[cite: 2].
  Fact: Standard question tags must follow opposing polarity; a positive statement takes a negative tag (*You are coming, aren't you?*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Using the pronoun "he" or "she" for unknown animals or abstract nouns in tags (e.g., saying *"The rain is falling, isn't he?"*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: Abstract nouns, natural phenomena, and animals take the neuter pronoun **it** (*The rain is heavy today, isn't it?*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Numbering points in a guided composition instead of writing continuous prose paragraphs[cite: 2].
  Fact: Guiding questions should be synthesized into cohesive, well-developed paragraphs joined by logical linkers[cite: 2].
