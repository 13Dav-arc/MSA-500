## Hook
When speaking or reading English, placing emphasis on the wrong syllable can completely alter a word's grammatical identity, turning a noun like an *IM-port* into a verb like to *im-PORT*[cite: 2]. Similarly, choosing the passive voice allows a speaker to report past incidents with precision, highlighting what was done rather than who did it, such as noting that a forgotten bag of money *had been returned*[cite: 2]. Whether exploring the inspiring honesty of a Lagos taxi driver, arranging an expository essay in logical paragraphs, or chanting oral verses passed down across generations, controlling these skills empowers you to communicate with clarity, authority, and moral purpose[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Syllabic Stress
  Definition: The extra breath force, loudness, pitch prominence, or muscular effort placed on a specific syllable within a multi-syllable word to make it stand out[cite: 2].
- Term: Past Perfect Passive
  Definition: A grammatical passive construction formed with *had been* followed by the past participle of the main verb, showing that an action had been performed on the subject before another past event[cite: 2].
- Term: Contentment
  Definition: The mental state of being peacefully satisfied with what one has, shielding an individual from greedy desires and dishonest practices[cite: 2].
- Term: Brainstorming
  Definition: The creative pre-writing stage where a writer reflects deeply on a topic, generating and recording a comprehensive list of ideas before organizing them logically[cite: 2].
- Term: Oral Poetry
  Definition: Poetry composed, performed, and transmitted by word of mouth across generations through chanting, singing, or recitation rather than written printed texts[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Introduction to Syllabic Stress
A **syllable** is a unit of spoken sound pronounced with a single chest pulse or breath effort[cite: 2]. In multi-syllabic words, syllables are not uttered with equal loudness; the syllable that receives extra breath force and pitch height is the **stressed syllable**[cite: 2]:
- **One-Syllable Words:** Words consisting of only one syllable (e.g., *come*, *sit*, *pen*) cannot bear independent word stress because there is no competing syllable to contrast with; word stress operates on words of two or more syllables[cite: 2].
- **Word-Class Stress Shift in Disyllabic Words:** Many two-syllable words in English have identical spelling but belong to different parts of speech[cite: 2]. As a general rule:
  - **Nouns** place stress on the **first syllable**[cite: 2].
  - **Verbs** place stress on the **second syllable**[cite: 2].

| Word | Noun Pronunciation (1st Syllable Stressed) | Verb Pronunciation (2nd Syllable Stressed) | Contextual Usage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Import**[cite: 2] | **IM-port**[cite: 2] | **im-PORT**[cite: 2] | Rice is an expensive **IM-port**; we must **im-PORT** less[cite: 2]. |
| **Export**[cite: 2] | **EX-port**[cite: 2] | **ex-PORT**[cite: 2] | Cocoa is a major **EX-port**; farmers **ex-PORT** cocoa[cite: 2]. |
| **Conduct**[cite: 2] | **CON-duct**[cite: 2] | **con-DUCT**[cite: 2] | His moral **CON-duct** was good; she will **con-DUCT** tests[cite: 2]. |
| **Escort**[cite: 2] | **ES-cort**[cite: 2] | **es-CORT**[cite: 2] | The police provided an **ES-cort** to **es-CORT** the cash[cite: 2]. |

- **Other Two-Syllable Words:**
  - *First Syllable Stressed:* **EN-ter**, **O-pen**, **PA-per**, **MO-ney**, **BO-rrow**[cite: 2].
  - *Second Syllable Stressed:* a-**LIVE**, di-**VINE**, a-**PPLY**, be-**WARE**, en-**JOY**[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Structure — Passive Transformations: Past Continuous and Past Perfect Tenses
When transforming active sentences in past aspectual forms into the passive voice, the auxiliary verb *to be* must carry the exact past aspect, followed by the past participle of the action verb[cite: 2]:

1. **Past Continuous Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{was/were} + \text{-ing Verb} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{was/were} + \text{being} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I was writing my note*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *My note was being written by me*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *The butcher was killing a goat*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *A goat was being killed by the butcher*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *The man was questioning the driver*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *The driver was being questioned by the man*[cite: 2].
2. **Past Perfect Tense:**
   - Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{had} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{Object}$[cite: 2]
   - Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{had been} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$[cite: 2]
   - *Active:* *I had kicked the ball before he arrived*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *The ball had been kicked by me before he arrived*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *The farmer had killed a snake before daybreak*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *A snake had been killed by the farmer before daybreak*[cite: 2].
   - *Active:* *The police had arrested the thief before noon*[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* *The thief had been arrested by the police before noon*[cite: 2].

### Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The Taxi Driver
**Reading Passage:**
> Mr. Okonkwo was once a humble commercial taxi driver navigating the congested highways and labyrinthine streets of Lagos[cite: 2]. Though he worked tirelessly from the break of dawn until late at night, he was a very poor man who struggled constantly to pay the modest rent on his one-room apartment and provide adequate nourishment for his young family[cite: 2]. Despite his biting financial deprivation, Mr. Okonkwo possessed an unshakeable moral compass; he steadfastly refused to overcharge stranded commuters during torrential downpours or engage in the dubious practices common among unscrupulous transporters[cite: 2].
>
> One sweltering Friday afternoon, after dropping off an agitated passenger at the international airport terminal, Mr. Okonkwo parked under an almond tree to clean his vehicle[cite: 2]. While dusting the rear passenger seat, his hand struck a bulky leather briefcase that had slipped beneath the driver's seat[cite: 2]. Upon unzipping the compartment to search for identity documents, his eyes widened in utter disbelief: neatly stacked bundles of crisp, high-denomination foreign currencies totaled several millions of naira[cite: 2]. A voice of desperation whispered that this sudden fortune could eradicate his poverty forever, buy him a fleet of modern commercial buses, and secure his family's comfort[cite: 2].
>
> However, Mr. Okonkwo promptly rejected the temptation to retain ill-gotten wealth[cite: 2]. Reminding himself of his father's sacred counsel that an untainted name is superior to stolen gold, he drove straight to the airport police station and surrendered the intact briefcase[cite: 2]. The owner, a foreign investor who was weeping in agony over the catastrophic loss, was astonished by the driver's extraordinary uprightness[cite: 2]. The grateful merchant rewarded Mr. Okonkwo with a brand-new vehicle and an executive employment contract as his company's logistics manager, proving beyond all doubt that it truly pays to be contented and honest[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Structuring Logical Sequences and Brainstorming
An **expository essay** explains how things are done, reveals facts about a process, or clarifies the causes and effects of social phenomena[cite: 2]. Its structure requires methodical planning[cite: 2]:
- **Brainstorming Stage:**
  - Before writing, think deeply about the topic to uncover all relevant angles and consequences[cite: 2].
  - Jot down every idea in rough notes without editing immediately[cite: 2].
  - Group related ideas together, eliminating irrelevant points, and arrange the finalized concepts into a coherent outline[cite: 2].
- **Paragraph Development and Logical Sequencing:**
  - *Unity:* Every paragraph must develop one central controlling idea introduced by a clear topic sentence[cite: 2].
  - *Orderly Progression:* Move logically from causes to consequences and solutions[cite: 2].
  - *Transitional Linkers:* Smoothly bridge ideas using connective adverbs: *firstly*, *furthermore*, *in addition to these*, *however*, *meanwhile*, *consequently*, *apart from these*[cite: 2].
- **Standard Four-Part Blueprint:**
  1. **Title:** Written boldly at the top (e.g., *THE EVIL OF UNEMPLOYMENT IN MY COUNTRY*)[cite: 2].
  2. **Introduction:** Defines the core subject, sets the national context, and highlights why the issue demands urgent analysis[cite: 2].
  3. **Body Paragraphs:** Develops the brainstormed points systematically (one major consequence per paragraph)[cite: 2].
  4. **Conclusion:** Summarizes the main arguments and proposes constructive recommendations[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Oral Poetry vs. Written Poetry
Poetry represents one of the oldest forms of human artistic expression, existing in two distinct traditions[cite: 2]:
1. **Oral Poetry:**
   - Composed and transmitted by word of mouth from generation to generation before or alongside written literacy[cite: 2].
   - Performed dynamically before a community audience, often accompanied by musical instruments, clapping, drumming, and dance[cite: 2].
   - Relies heavily on mnemonic devices: strong rhythmic beats, repetitive refrains, parallel sentence structures, and evocative sound effects[cite: 2].
   - *Examples:* Traditional praise chants (*oriki*), occupational hunting chants, cradle songs (lullabies), heroic war chants, and burial incantations[cite: 2].
2. **Written Poetry:**
   - Composed through written text by an individual poet who arranges words deliberately into lines and stanzas on the page[cite: 2].
   - Relies on visual typography, line breaks, punctuation pauses, and intricate literary devices designed for private reading and reflective recitation[cite: 2].
   - *Examples:* Sonnets, formal odes, elegies, and modern social protest verse[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Stressing single-syllable words in English sentences (e.g., attempting to place word-class stress on *come* or *sit*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: A one-syllable word cannot have word stress within itself because stress is a relative prominence between two or more syllables in a word[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Stressing the second syllable of two-syllable nouns (e.g., pronouncing *IM-port* or *EX-port* as *im-PORT* or *ex-PORT* when using them as nouns)[cite: 2].
  Fact: When used as nouns, these words must be stressed on the first syllable (**IM-port**, **EX-port**)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Confusing *being* with *been* in passive verb forms[cite: 2].
  Fact: Continuous tenses take **being** (*was being written*)[cite: 2], while perfect tenses take **been** (*had been written*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Cramming multiple brainstormed points into a single, unfocused body paragraph in an expository essay[cite: 2].
  Fact: Each distinct brainstormed point must occupy its own separate paragraph, linked logically with transitional connectives[cite: 2].
