## Hook
When navigating an unfamiliar town using a written guide, following a craft manual, or listening to a recited poem, clear articulation and grammatical awareness make the difference between confusion and understanding[cite: 2]. Pronouncing minimal pairs like *bleed* and *breed* or *flesh* and *fresh* with distinct tongue positions ensures that listeners catch your exact words without hesitation[cite: 2]. Shifting between the active and passive voice allows you to focus either on who performed an action or on the result itself, whether you are following travel directions to ASCON Topo, explaining the traditional craft of tie-and-dye (*adire*), or appreciating the auditory musicality of rhythm and rhyme in verse[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Frictionless Continuant
  Definition: A consonant sound produced when an active articulator approaches a passive articulator without making full contact or creating audible friction, specifically exemplified by /r/[cite: 2].
- Term: Passive Voice
  Definition: A grammatical construction where the grammatical subject of the sentence receives or suffers the action expressed by the verb rather than performing it[cite: 2].
- Term: Directional Landmark
  Definition: A prominent, easily recognizable geographic feature, junction, or building used as a navigational reference point when following written travel instructions[cite: 2].
- Term: Process Exposition
  Definition: An expository text that explains the chronological, step-by-step procedure required to manufacture a product, prepare a dish, or play a game[cite: 2].
- Term: Rhythm
  Definition: The patterned, musical flow of language in verse created by the regular alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Minimal Pair Contrast of /l/ and /r/
Consonants **/l/** and **/r/** are voiced liquid sonorants that learners frequently confuse, despite their distinct articulatory mechanisms[cite: 2]:
- **/l/** (Alveolar Lateral): The tongue tip presses against the alveolar ridge, while the sides of the tongue drop to allow the voiced airstream to escape laterally[cite: 2].
- **/r/** (Post-Alveolar Continuant): The tongue tip curls slightly toward the rear of the alveolar ridge without making contact, allowing the airstream to pass centrally without friction[cite: 2].

| Word with /l/ | Word with /r/ | Contextual Sentence Contrast |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *lack*[cite: 2] | *rack*[cite: 2] | They *lack* coats on the wooden *rack*[cite: 2]. |
| *loom*[cite: 2] | *room*[cite: 2] | The weaver set up a *loom* in the spacious *room*[cite: 2]. |
| *bleed*[cite: 2] | *breed*[cite: 2] | Scratches *bleed*, but shepherds *breed* sheep[cite: 2]. |
| *blew*[cite: 2] | *brew*[cite: 2] | The wind *blew* as she prepared the herbal *brew*[cite: 2]. |
| *play*[cite: 2] | *pray*[cite: 2] | Children *play* outside while worshippers *pray* quietly[cite: 2]. |
| *flesh*[cite: 2] | *fresh*[cite: 2] | The vendor sold *fresh* fruits and tender *flesh*[cite: 2]. |
| *flew*[cite: 2] | *brew*[cite: 2] | The birds *flew* away while tea continued to *brew*[cite: 2]. |

### Aspect 2: Structure — Active vs. Passive Voice Transformations
In English syntax, the grammatical relationship between the participants and the verb can be structured in two primary voices[cite: 2]:
- **Active Voice:** The **logical subject** (the entity that actually carries out the action) matches the **grammatical subject** (the position before the verb)[cite: 2]. The action flows from the subject to the object[cite: 2].
  - *The dog chased the postman*[cite: 2].
  - *Obi cleaned the blackboard*[cite: 2].
  - *I wash the clothes*[cite: 2].
  - *Adana cooks the meals*[cite: 2].
- **Passive Voice:** The object of the active sentence is promoted to become the grammatical subject of the verb, while the original subject becomes the agent preceded by the preposition **by** (or is omitted entirely)[cite: 2]. The auxiliary verb *to be* is introduced in the original tense alongside the past participle of the main verb[cite: 2].
  - *The postman was chased by the dog*[cite: 2].
  - *The blackboard was cleaned by Obi*[cite: 2].
  - *The clothes are washed by me*[cite: 2].
  - *The meals are cooked by Adana*[cite: 2].

**Pronoun Transformation Rules:**
When subject pronouns shift to the object position behind *by*, they take their objective case forms[cite: 2]:
- *I* $\rightarrow$ *by me*[cite: 2]
- *We* $\rightarrow$ *by us*[cite: 2]
- *You* $\rightarrow$ *by you*
- *He* $\rightarrow$ *by him*[cite: 2]
- *She* $\rightarrow$ *by her*
- *They* $\rightarrow$ *by them*[cite: 2]

### Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Process Description (Local Crafts and Games)
An **expository process essay** explains how something is made or done through an orderly sequence of actions[cite: 2]. It lays out the procedure so clearly that a person who has never attempted the task can follow and complete it successfully[cite: 2].
- **Popular Expository Process Topics:**
  - *How to Make Tie and Dye (Campala/Adire)*[cite: 2]
  - *How to Play the Traditional Game of Ayo*[cite: 2]
  - *How to Prepare My Local Food*[cite: 2]
- **Process Essay Outline: How to Make Tie and Dye (*Campala*)[cite: 2]:**
  1. **Title:** Written boldly at the top (e.g., *HOW TO MAKE TIE AND DYE (CAMPALA) FABRIC*)[cite: 2].
  2. **Introduction:** Define tie-and-dye as a traditional textile resist-dyeing craft, stating its cultural significance and enumerating the required materials (plain white cotton cloth, synthetic dyes, caustic soda, sodium hydrosulphite, rubber bands or raffia thread, plastic basins, boiling water, and rubber gloves)[cite: 2].
  3. **Body Paragraph 1 (Preparation and Tying):** Wash the fabric to remove industrial starch, fold or pleat the cloth into desired geometric patterns, and bind the folds tightly with raffia or rubber bands to prevent dye penetration in wrapped sections[cite: 2].
  4. **Body Paragraph 2 (Dye Bath Formulation):** Dissolve the powdered dyestuff in boiling water inside a sturdy plastic basin, adding specified quantities of caustic soda to fix the dye and hydrosulphite to activate color pigments safely[cite: 2].
  5. **Body Paragraph 3 (Dyeing and Oxidation):** Immerse the tied fabric into the hot dye solution using wooden tongs; leave it submerged for fifteen to twenty minutes before removing it to allow oxidation and color setting in the open air[cite: 2].
  6. **Conclusion:** Cut open the binding threads, rinse the fabric in clean cold water until the water runs clear, dry the cloth in shade, and press with an iron to reveal the intricate patterns[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetic Language: Rhythm, Sound, and Imagery
Poets use language differently from prose writers; they deploy condensed figurative language and musical sound patterns to evoke sensory emotions and convey deep truths[cite: 2]:
1. **Imagery:** The creation of vibrant sensory pictures in the mind of the reader through figurative devices, such as visual pictures, auditory sounds, and tactile feelings[cite: 2].
2. **Rhythm:** The musical cadence or beat of a poem produced by the deliberate alternation of stressed (strong) and unstressed (weak) syllables within lines of verse[cite: 2].
3. **Sound:** The acoustic effects created through conscious arrangements of vowels and consonants in speech[cite: 2]:
   - **Rhyme:** The repetition of identical or similar vowel and end-consonant sounds at the ends of poetic lines (e.g., *flew / brew*, *lead / bleed*)[cite: 2].
   - **Alliteration:** The repetition of identical initial consonant sounds in neighboring words (e.g., *the wild wind went weeping*).
   - **Onomatopoeia:** The use of words that imitate the physical sound of the action they describe (e.g., *buzz*, *splash*, *clatter*)[cite: 1].

- Label: Case Shift
  Title: Subject Pronouns vs Agent Pronouns
  Body: Subject pronouns in active sentences (*I*, *we*, *they*) must shift to their objective case forms behind *by* in passive sentences (*by me*, *by us*, *by them*)[cite: 2].
- Label: Poetic Mechanics
  Title: Poetic Rhythm vs Poetic Rhyme
  Body: Rhythm is the musical beat produced by alternating stressed and unstressed syllables throughout a line[cite: 2], whereas rhyme is the acoustic matching of terminal syllables between lines[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Substituting /r/ for /l/ or /l/ for /r/ (e.g., saying *"pray the game"* instead of *"play the game"*, or *"flesh air"* instead of *"fresh air"*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: Make sure the tongue tip firmly strikes the gum ridge for /l/, and ensure it does not touch the ridge when uttering /r/[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Forgetting to insert the appropriate form of the auxiliary verb *to be* when converting active sentences into the passive voice[cite: 2].
  Fact: A passive verb requires a tense-appropriate form of *be* (*is*, *are*, *was*, *were*, *been*, *being*) paired with the past participle of the main verb[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Leaving pronoun subjects unchanged in passive constructions (e.g., writing *"The food was eaten by he"* instead of *"by him"*)[cite: 2].
  Fact: Pronouns functioning as the object of the preposition *by* must change to their objective forms (*me*, *us*, *him*, *her*, *them*)[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Itemizing steps in an expository essay using bulleted fragments instead of continuous prose paragraphs.
  Fact: An expository essay must explain sequential steps in complete, well-developed paragraphs joined by transitional adverbs[cite: 2].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Phonetic Identification (Speech Work)
Context: Examine the words: *loom*, *room*, *breed*, *bleed*, *calm*, *ferry*[cite: 2].
Problem: Identify which words feature the alveolar lateral /l/, which feature the continuant /r/, and which contain a silent "l"[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Features /l/:** *loom*, *bleed* (tongue tip makes contact; air escapes laterally)[cite: 2].
- **Features /r/:** *room*, *breed*, *ferry* (tongue curls near alveolar ridge without touching)[cite: 2].
- **Silent "l":** *calm* (the letter "l" is completely unpronounced)[cite: 2].

### Example B: Grammatical Application (Structure)
Context: Change the following two active sentences into the passive voice: (1) *The cat ate the fish*; (2) *Emeka dusts the chairs*[cite: 2].
Problem: Promote the direct object, select the appropriate form of *be*, and place the original subject in a by-agent phrase[cite: 2].
Fix:
1. *Active:* The cat (*subject*) ate (*past verb*) the fish (*object*)[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* **The fish was eaten by the cat** (past singular *was* + past participle *eaten*)[cite: 2].
2. *Active:* Emeka (*subject*) dusts (*present verb*) the chairs (*plural object*)[cite: 2].
   - *Passive:* **The chairs are dusted by Emeka** (present plural *are* + past participle *dusted*)[cite: 2].

### Example C: Writing & Literary Application (Composition / Literature)
Context: A candidate is writing an expository essay on preparing tie-and-dye fabric and identifying poetic devices in a verse[cite: 2].
Problem: State two safety precautions when working with chemical dyes, and define the poetic element called *rhythm*[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Safety Precautions:** (1) Wear thick rubber gloves to shield skin from caustic soda burns; (2) Mix dyes in well-ventilated areas to avoid inhaling chemical fumes[cite: 2].
- **Poetic Rhythm:** Rhythm is the regular, musical cadence of verse produced by the systematic alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables[cite: 2].
