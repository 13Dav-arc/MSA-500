## Hook
If you tell a farmer that you saw a "coat" chewing grass in his field, he might wonder why a piece of clothing has an appetite—unless he realizes you meant a "goat"! The difference between voiceless /k/ and voiced /g/ hinges on a single microscopic muscular action: the vibration of your vocal cords. In written grammar, ensuring that singular subjects pair smoothly with singular verbs—the golden law of **Concord**—is what elevates your writing from clumsy sentences to scholarly excellence. As we arrive at the culmination of our first term, synthesizing our skills in speech, grammar, varied writing styles, and literary analysis equips you to express your thoughts with undeniable authority and distinction.

## Key Terms
- Term: Velar Plosive
  Definition: A consonant stop sound produced by raising the back of the tongue to form an airtight seal against the soft palate (velum), followed by an explosive burst of released air (such as /k/ and /g/).
- Term: Subject-Verb Concord
  Definition: The fundamental grammatical agreement in number (singular or plural) and person between the subject of a sentence and its finite verb.
- Term: Sentence Pattern
  Definition: The structural arrangement and grammatical sequence of essential functional sentence components (Subject, Verb, Object, Complement, Adverbial).
- Term: Onomatopoeia
  Definition: A literary sound device wherein a word phonetically imitates, resembles, or suggests the natural sound that it describes (e.g., *buzz*, *hiss*, *clatter*, *splash*).
- Term: Alliteration
  Definition: The repetition of identical consonant sounds at the beginning of adjacent or closely connected words within a poetic line or sentence (e.g., *"Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers"*).

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Speech Work — Velar Plosives /k/ and /g/
Both /k/ and /g/ are velar plosives articulated by pressing the back of the tongue against the soft palate (velum).
- **/k/ (Voiceless Velar Plosive):**
  - Articulated with the back of the tongue raised against the soft palate to block the airstream completely. The vocal cords do **not** vibrate (voiceless). In initial stressed positions, it is aspirated with an audible puff of air.
  - Common spelling symbols: `c` (*cat*, *coat*, *cup*, *clean*), `k` (*king*, *keep*, *kettle*), `ck` (*back*, *lock*, *sick*), `q` or `qu` (*queen* /kwiːn/, *equal*), and `ch` in Greek-origin words (*school*, *ache*, *chemist*, *character*).
  - (Note: `k` is silent before `n` in initial positions: *knife*, *knee*, *knot*, *know*, *knight*).
- **/g/ (Voiced Velar Plosive):**
  - Articulated identically against the soft palate, but the vocal cords **vibrate** energetically (voiced). It is never aspirated.
  - Common spelling symbols: `g` (*go*, *get*, *goat*, *big*, *bag*), `gg` (*egg*, *struggle*, *dagger*), and `gu` (*guest*, *guide*, *guard*, *guilt*).
  - (Note: `g` is silent before `n` or `m` in words like *gnat*, *gnaw*, *sign*, *reign*, *phlegm*, *foreign*).

| Word Position | Voiceless Plosive /k/ | Voiced Plosive /g/ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Initial** | *coat*, *cap*, *class*, *cold*, *curl* | *goat*, *gap*, *glass*, *gold*, *girl* |
| **Medial** | *baker*, *locker*, *anchor*, *market* | *bagger*, *logger*, *anger*, *target* |
| **Final** | *back*, *lock*, *pick*, *dock*, *leak* | *bag*, *log*, *pig*, *dog*, *league* |

### Aspect 2: Structure — Basic Sentence Patterns and Subject-Verb Concord
1. **Five Core Sentence Structural Patterns:**
   - **SV (Subject + Verb):** *"The birds [S] sang [V]."* / *"The sun [S] shines [V]."*
   - **SVO (Subject + Verb + Direct Object):** *"The girl [S] kicked [V] the football [O]."*
   - **SVC (Subject + Verb + Complement):** *"Amina [S] is [V] a doctor [C]."* / *"The soup [S] tastes [V] delicious [C]."*
   - **SVOO (Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object):** *"The teacher [S] gave [V] the student [IO] a prize [DO]."*
   - **SVOC (Subject + Verb + Direct Object + Object Complement):** *"The class [S] elected [V] Chidi [O] prefect [C]."*
2. **Cardinal Rules of Subject-Verb Concord (Agreement):**
   - **Rule 1 (Number Agreement):** A singular subject requires a singular verb; a plural subject requires a plural verb (*"The student **writes**"*; *"The students **write**"*).
   - **Rule 2 (Compound Subjects with 'And'):** Two or more subjects joined by *and* generally take a plural verb (*"Chidi and Ngozi **are** diligent"*). (Exception: When two nouns express a single unified concept, they take a singular verb: *"Bread and butter **is** my favorite breakfast"*).
   - **Rule 3 (The Proximity Rule with 'Either...Or' / 'Neither...Nor'):** When subjects are joined by *either...or* or *neither...nor*, the verb agrees in person and number with the closer subject (*"Neither the teacher nor the students **were** present"*; *"Neither the students nor the teacher **was** present"*).
   - **Rule 4 (Parenthetical Interventions):** Words introduced by *as well as*, *together with*, *along with*, *in addition to*, or *accompanied by* do not affect the subject's number (*"The principal, together with his staff, **is** attending the meeting"*).
   - **Rule 5 (Indefinite Pronouns):** Pronouns such as *everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, neither, either, each* are grammatically singular and require singular verbs (*"Each of the boys **has** a notebook"*).
   - **Rule 6 (Plural in Form, Singular in Meaning):** Nouns denoting academic subjects or diseases ending in "-s" (*Mathematics, Physics, Civics, Measles, News*) take singular verbs (*"Mathematics **is** my best subject"*; *"The news **was** encouraging"*).

>

Society does not solely require brilliant doctors, inventive engineers, and astute accountants; it desperately hungers for upright citizens whose professional brilliance is anchored in profound ethical integrity.

### Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Literary Synthesis: Genres and Sound Devices
To culminate our first-term literary appreciation, we synthesize the three overarching genres and examine powerful sound devices:
1. **Synthesis of the Three Genres:**
   - *Prose:* Narrative text in continuous paragraphs (Fiction: novels, short stories; Non-Fiction: biographies, essays).
   - *Drama:* Enacted theatrical performance in dialogue, acts, and scenes (Tragedy: catastrophe and sorrow; Comedy: humor and joyful reconciliation).
   - *Poetry:* Concentrated rhythmic verse in lines and stanzas (Couplets, Quatrains, Rhyme Scheme, Sensory Imagery).
2. **Core Literary Sound Devices:**
   - **Alliteration:** Repetition of identical initial consonant sounds in closely linked words (e.g., *"The **s**oft **s**ea **s**ighed **s**ilently"*).
   - **Assonance:** Repetition of identical or similar vowel sounds within nearby words (e.g., *"Fl**ee**t f**ee**t sw**ee**p by sl**ee**ping gr**ee**ns"*).
   - **Onomatopoeia:** Words that imitate the acoustic sound of what they name (e.g., *"The dry leaves **crunched** underfoot"*; *"The bees **buzzed** around the hive"*).
   - **Irony:** A contrast between expectation and reality, or saying the opposite of what is actually meant (e.g., *"The pilot had a morbid fear of heights"*).

  Body: Singular subjects require singular verbs with '-s' inflection in the present tense (e.g., 'he writes'); plural subjects require base verbs without '-s' (e.g., 'they write').
- Label: Alliteration vs Assonance
  Title: Consonant vs Vowel Sound Repetition
  Body: Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds (e.g., 'proud princes'); assonance repeats internal vowel sounds (e.g., 'deep green sea').
- Label: Simile vs Metaphor vs Personification
  Title: Nature of Figurative Identification
  Body: Simile uses 'like/as' to compare; metaphor identifies directly without 'like/as'; personification assigns human qualities to non-human entities.

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Violating concord with plural-looking nouns that are singular in meaning, such as writing *"Mathematics are difficult"* or *"The news are bad"*.
  Fact: Academic subjects like *Mathematics* and mass nouns like *news* are singular and take singular verbs (*"Mathematics **is**..."*; *"The news **was**..."*).
- Mistake: Violating the proximity rule with *either...or*, writing *"Neither the boys nor the teacher were present"*.
  Fact: The verb must agree with the closer subject; since *the teacher* is singular and closest to the verb, write *"was present"*.
- Mistake: Pronouncing the initial "k" in words like *knife*, *knee*, and *know*.
  Fact: In standard English, initial "k" before "n" is completely silent.

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Velar Plosive Discrimination (Speech Work)
Context: A student reads the sentence: *"The farmer put the lock on the pen of the big pig."*
Problem: Transcribe the final consonant sounds of the words *lock*, *big*, and *pig*.
Fix:
- *lock* ends in the voiceless velar plosive /k/ (/lɒk/).
- *big* ends in the voiced velar plosive /g/ (/bɪg/).
- *pig* ends in the voiced velar plosive /g/ (/pɪg/).

### Example B: Concord Application and Correction (Structure)
Context: Examine the following three sentences containing grammatical concord errors:
Sentence 1: *"Every student in the boarding house have a personal locker."*
Sentence 2: *"Bread and butter are his favorite breakfast every morning."*
Sentence 3: *"Neither the captain nor his players was ready for the kickoff."*
Problem: Correct each error and provide the governing concord rule.
Fix:
- Sentence 1: *"have"* -> Change to *"has"*. (Rule: Indefinite pronoun *Every* is singular).
- Sentence 2: *"are"* -> Change to *"is"*. (Rule: *Bread and butter* forms a single combined food unit).
- Sentence 3: *"was"* -> Change to *"were"*. (Rule: Proximity rule; the plural subject *players* is closest to the verb).

### Example C: Literary Device Identification (Literature-in-English)
Context: Read this poetic line: *"The buzzing bees built bright blossoms on the bank."*
Problem: Identify two distinct literary devices operating in this line.
Fix:
- Device 1: **Onomatopoeia** in the word *"buzzing"*, which mimics the physical vibrating sound of bees.
- Device 2: **Alliteration** in the repetition of the initial /b/ consonant sound (*buzzing bees built bright blossoms bank*).
