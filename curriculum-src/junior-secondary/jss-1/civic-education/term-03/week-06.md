## Hook
Imagine boarding an international commercial airliner bound for London or New York. The pilot walks into the cockpit, looks at the instrument panel, and decides: "Today, I will not follow the official flight manual. I will fly upside down, ignore all air traffic control towers, turn off the radar, and land wherever I feel like!" Would you remain in your seat for another second? You would sprint toward the emergency exit screaming for help! An airplane cannot fly safely without an authoritative flight operating manual; in the exact same way, a country of over 220 million citizens cannot exist without a supreme operating rulebook. That supreme rulebook is called the **Constitution**! What is a constitution, where does it come from, and how has Nigeria’s constitutional journey shaped our freedom today? Let us step into the supreme library of constitutional law!

## Key Terms
- Term: Constitution
- Definition: The fundamental, supreme body of written and unwritten laws, principles, conventions, and customs according to which a sovereign state, government, or organization is organized and governed.
- Term: Written Constitution
- Definition: A constitution whose fundamental provisions, rules, and principles are formally codified and contained within a single, authoritative legal document (e.g., Nigeria and the United States).
- Term: Unwritten Constitution
- Definition: A constitution whose principles are not codified in a single document, but derived from multiple historical statutes, judicial precedents, conventions, and ancient customs (e.g., the United Kingdom).
- Term: Rigid Constitution
- Definition: A constitution that requires a cumbersome, specialized, and difficult legal process (such as a two-thirds majority in parliament and ratification by state legislatures) to amend or alter.
- Term: Flexible Constitution
- Definition: A constitution that can be amended or altered easily through the ordinary, standard lawmaking procedure of parliament without special hurdles.
- Term: Federal Constitution
- Definition: A constitutional framework that legally divides governmental powers between a central federal government and autonomous regional or state governments (e.g., Nigeria).
- Term: Unitary Constitution
- Definition: A constitutional system where all supreme governmental powers and sovereignty are concentrated in a single central national government (e.g., Britain and France).
- Term: Judicial Precedent
- Definition: A past decision or ruling by a superior court of record that establishes a binding legal principle for judges to follow in similar future cases.
- Term: Conventions
- Definition: Unwritten, long-standing political customs, practices, and traditions that are universally observed as binding, though not enforceable in courts of law.
- Term: Constitutionalism
- Definition: The democratic doctrine that government power must be strictly limited by law and that leaders must govern strictly within constitutional boundaries.

## Core Concept
### What is a Constitution?
The Constitution is the supreme law of the land. Section 1(1) of the 1999 Constitution of Nigeria states: *"This Constitution is supreme and its provisions shall have binding force on all authorities and persons throughout the Federal Republic of Nigeria."* It defines the powers of the President, the National Assembly, and the Judiciary; outlines how citizens acquire citizenship; and guarantees fundamental human rights.

### Sources of a Constitution
Where do the laws in a constitution originate?
1. **Customs and Traditions:** Ancient cultural norms and societal values accepted by the people over centuries.
2. **Statutory Legislation (Acts of Parliament):** Laws enacted by legislative assemblies over time that become part of the constitutional structure.
3. **Judicial Precedents (*Stare Decisis*):** Landmark judgments delivered by superior courts (such as the Supreme Court) that interpret constitutional ambiguities.
4. **Constitutional Conventions:** Unwritten political traditions and ethical understandings observed by statesmen (e.g., the ceremonial role of the British monarch).
5. **International Treaties and Conventions:** Agreements, protocols, and international covenants ratified by the country (e.g., the Universal Declaration of Human Rights).
6. **Historical Constitutional Documents:** Past charters, declarations of rights, and colonial constitutional orders.

---

### Major Classifications of Constitutions:
- **Written vs. Unwritten:**
  - *Written:* Highly precise, easily accessible to citizens, difficult to manipulate arbitrarily, but can be rigid.
  - *Unwritten:* Highly adaptable to emergencies, but vague and difficult for ordinary citizens to read in a single book.
- **Rigid vs. Flexible:**
  - *Rigid:* Nigeria has a rigid constitution. Under **Section 9 of the 1999 Constitution**, amending the constitution requires:
    1. A two-thirds (2/3) majority vote in both the Senate and the House of Representatives.
    2. Approval by a resolution passed by the Houses of Assembly of at least **two-thirds (24 out of 36) of the States**!
  - *Flexible:* The British Parliament can change any constitutional law by a simple 51% majority vote in an afternoon.
- **Federal vs. Unitary:**
  - *Federal:* Power shared between federal, state, and local governments (Exclusive, Concurrent, and Residual legislative lists).
  - *Unitary:* A single central parliament in the capital makes all decisions for the whole country.

## Comparative Matrix
| Dimension | Written Constitution (e.g., Nigeria) | Unwritten Constitution (e.g., United Kingdom) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical Form** | Single, codified, bound organic document | Scattered across various statutes, charters, and customs |
| **Amendment Process** | Usually rigid; requires special super-majorities | Flexible; altered through ordinary simple majority |
| **Accessibility** | Easily bought, read, and consulted by any citizen | Complex; requires legal expertise to synthesize |
| **Protection of Rights**| Explicitly codified in a dedicated Bill of Rights | Derived from common law precedents and statutes |
| **Judicial Scrutiny** | Courts can nullify acts contrary to constitution | Parliament is sovereign; courts cannot strike down acts |

## Common Mistakes
- **Confusing "Rigid" with "Unchangeable":** A rigid constitution does not mean it can *never* be changed; it simply means amendment requires a difficult, rigorous super-majority procedure (Section 9) to protect stability.
- **Thinking the 1960 Independence Constitution Made Nigeria a Republic:** Nigeria remained a constitutional monarchy under Queen Elizabeth II from 1960 until the **1963 Republican Constitution**, which officially severed ties with the British Crown!
- **Believing Nigeria Has Always Practiced a Presidential System:** From 1960 to 1966, Nigeria practiced the British **Parliamentary System** with a Prime Minister (Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa) as head of government. The American **Presidential System** was first introduced in 1979.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Calculating the Super-Majority for Constitutional Amendment
**Scenario:** A proposed constitutional amendment bill seeking to grant financial autonomy to local governments is being voted on in Nigeria.
- **Step 1 (Federal Level):** Must receive the affirmative vote of at least **two-thirds** of the 109 Senators (at least 73 Senators) and **two-thirds** of the 360 Representatives (at least 240 Reps).
- **Step 2 (State Level):** The bill must then be transmitted to all 36 State Houses of Assembly. It must be approved by resolutions of at least **two-thirds of the states** (at least 24 out of 36 State Assemblies).
- **Civic Lesson:** This rigorous process ensures that no single political faction can rewrite the nation's supreme rules easily!

### Example 2: The Elective Principle under the Clifford Constitution (1922)
**Scenario:** In 1922, Governor Sir Hugh Clifford introduced a new constitution that allowed Nigerians to vote for the first time in history.
- **Limitation:** Only adult males residing in Lagos and Calabar earning at least £100 annually could vote. They elected only 4 members (3 for Lagos, 1 for Calabar) out of 46 members of the Legislative Council!
- **Significance:** Despite the small franchise, this sparked the birth of Nigerian political parties, led by Herbert Macaulay's NNDP!

## Guardian Sync
- **Inspect the Constitution:** If you have a copy of the 1999 Constitution, show it to your child. Read Section 1 together: "This Constitution is supreme..."
- **Discuss Constitutional Progress:** Talk about how Nigeria evolved from colonial rule where foreign governors made all laws to an independent republic where Nigerian citizens elect their own lawmakers.
- **Current Affairs Connection:** When state or federal lawmakers debate constitutional amendments on television, discuss the issues with your child—showing that our constitution is a living, evolving document.
