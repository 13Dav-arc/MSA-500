## Hook
Consider this fascinating family story: Mr. and Mrs. Okonkwo are Nigerian citizens working as medical doctors in London, United Kingdom. While living in London, they give birth to a baby girl named Chiamaka. Chiamaka has never set foot on Nigerian soil, yet the moment she was born, she became an authentic, legal citizen of the Federal Republic of Nigeria! How could a baby born five thousand kilometers away in England be a Nigerian citizen from the very second she drew her first breath? At the same time, an Italian engineer who has lived peacefully in Kano for twenty-five years, speaks fluent Hausa, and loves eating Tuwo Shinkafa, is not automatically a citizen unless he submits a formal application to the President! Why does the law treat these two situations so differently? The answer lies in the **Types of Citizenship** established under the 1999 Constitution of Nigeria! Let us unlock this constitutional mystery!

## Key Terms
- Term: Citizenship by Birth
- Definition: Citizenship acquired automatically at birth by an individual born in Nigeria, either of whose parents or grandparents belongs or belonged to a community indigenous to Nigeria.
- Term: Citizenship by Descent
- Definition: Citizenship acquired by an individual born outside Nigeria whose father, mother, or grandparents were Nigerian citizens at the time of the child's birth.
- Term: Citizenship by Registration
- Definition: Citizenship granted by the President of Nigeria to a foreign woman married to a Nigerian citizen, or to a person of full age born outside Nigeria whose grandparents were Nigerians.
- Term: Citizenship by Naturalization
- Definition: Citizenship acquired through official statutory application by a foreign national who has resided in Nigeria continuously for at least 15 years, is of good character, and has assimilated into local culture.
- Term: Honorary Citizenship
- Definition: A prestigious, symbolic legal status of citizenship conferred by the President and Federal Government on an eminent foreign personality for extraordinary services to Nigeria or humanity.
- Term: Jus Sanguinis
- Definition: A legal principle (Latin for "right of blood") by which citizenship is determined by the nationality of a person's parents rather than by their place of birth.
- Term: Jus Soli
- Definition: A legal principle (Latin for "right of the soil") by which citizenship is acquired automatically by being born within the territorial borders of a country.
- Term: Renunciation
- Definition: The formal, voluntary surrender or relinquishment of one's citizenship of a country, typically done when acquiring the citizenship of another state.
- Term: Indigenous Community
- Definition: An ethnic nationality or native community originally native to and existing within the geographical boundaries of Nigeria before independence (October 1, 1960).
- Term: Dual Citizenship
- Definition: The legal status of being simultaneously recognized as a citizen of two sovereign countries under the laws of both nations.

### Detailed Examination of the Five Types:

#### 1. Citizenship by Birth (Section 25)
This is the most common form of citizenship in Nigeria. A person is a citizen by birth if:
- They were born in Nigeria before October 1, 1960, and either of their parents or grandparents belonged to an indigenous Nigerian community.
- They were born in Nigeria after October 1, 1960, and either of their parents or any of their grandparents is a citizen of Nigeria.
- *Crucial Rule:* Simply being born on Nigerian soil to two foreign tourists or temporary aliens does *not* automatically make that child a Nigerian citizen. Nigeria follows primarily *Jus Sanguinis* (blood lineage) rather than unconditional *Jus Soli* (soil birth).

#### 2. Citizenship by Descent
This applies to individuals born outside the territory of Nigeria:
- If a child is born in London, New York, Johannesburg, or Beijing, but their father, mother, or any grandparent was a Nigerian citizen by birth, that child is automatically a Nigerian citizen **by descent**.
- Such individuals only need to present their birth certificates and their parents' Nigerian passports at a Nigerian Embassy to receive their green Nigerian passport.

#### 3. Citizenship by Registration (Section 26)
This route is specifically designed for foreign spouses and persons of Nigerian heritage:
- **Foreign Women Married to Nigerian Citizens:** A French, Ghanaian, or Japanese woman who marries a Nigerian male citizen can apply to the President to be registered as a Nigerian citizen.
- **Foreign-born Adults of Nigerian Heritage:** A person of full age and capacity born outside Nigeria whose grandparents were Nigerian citizens.
- *Requirements:* The applicant must satisfy the President of good character, show clear intention to reside permanently in Nigeria, and take the constitutional **Oath of Allegiance**.

#### 4. Citizenship by Naturalization (Section 27)
This route allows qualified foreign nationals (aliens) living in Nigeria to voluntarily become citizens:
- *Conditions for Naturalization:*
  1. The applicant must be of full age (at least 18 years old) and sound mind.
  2. The applicant must be a person of good character, certified by reputable citizens in their local community.
  3. The applicant must have lived in Nigeria continuously for at least **15 years** (or 12 consecutive months plus 15 aggregate years).
  4. The applicant must show genuine assimilation into the customs, language, and culture of the local community where they reside.
  5. The applicant must be acceptable to the local community in which they live.
  6. The applicant must be capable of contributing positively to the economic and social advancement of Nigeria.
  7. The applicant must officially renounce their previous foreign citizenship (unless the President permits an exception) and take the Oath of Allegiance.

#### 5. Honorary Citizenship
This is a distinguished, symbolic status conferred by the President and the Federal Executive Council upon an eminent foreigner (such as a foreign head of state, Nobel laureate, or global philanthropist) who has rendered outstanding, heroic, or extraordinary service to Nigeria.

## Common Mistakes
- **Assuming Any Baby Born in a Nigerian Hospital is Automatically Nigerian:** Unlike the United States (which practices unconditional *Jus Soli*), Nigeria requires that at least one parent or grandparent belongs to a Nigerian community or is a citizen. A child born in Lagos to two French tourists is a French citizen, not a Nigerian citizen!
- **Thinking a Foreign Husband of a Nigerian Woman Automatically Gets Citizenship by Registration:** The 1999 Constitution (Section 26) specifically mentions a foreign *woman* married to a Nigerian citizen. A foreign *man* married to a Nigerian woman must apply through the **naturalization** process.
- **Believing Naturalized Citizens Cannot Lose Their Citizenship:** If a naturalized citizen engages in treason, aids an enemy nation during war, or is sentenced to prison for more than three years within seven years of naturalization, the President can revoke their citizenship under Section 30 of the Constitution.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Determining Citizenship by Descent
**Scenario:** David was born in Toronto, Canada. His father was born in Ibadan, Oyo State, and his mother was born in Owerri, Imo State. David holds a Canadian passport and has lived in Toronto his entire life.
- **Question:** Is David legally a Nigerian citizen?
- **Analysis:** Yes! Under Section 25 of the 1999 Constitution, because both of David's parents are Nigerian citizens by birth, David is automatically a Nigerian citizen **by descent**, regardless of his place of birth. He can legitimately hold both Canadian and Nigerian passports (dual citizenship).

### Example 2: Evaluating a Naturalization Application
**Scenario:** Mr. Lee, a Chinese civil engineer, moved to Abuja in 2008. By 2024 (16 years later), he speaks fluent Hausa, runs a registered construction company employing 200 Nigerians, pays taxes regularly, and has no criminal record.
- **Question:** Is Mr. Lee eligible to apply for Nigerian citizenship?
- **Analysis:** Yes! Mr. Lee has satisfied all statutory criteria: he has lived in Nigeria for over 15 years, is of verified good character, has assimilated into the local community, contributes to the economy, and can formally petition the President for citizenship by naturalization.
