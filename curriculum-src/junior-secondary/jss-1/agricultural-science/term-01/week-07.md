## Hook
Have you ever wondered why a farmer in Kano or Sokoto grows endless golden fields of millet and groundnuts, but would completely fail if they tried to plant a commercial cocoa plantation in their backyard? Or why farmers in Ondo, Cross River, and Edo harvest giant bunches of oil palm nuts, latex from rubber trees, and baskets of cocoa pods, but you almost never see them cultivating vast plains of wheat? Does soil alone determine what grows where? The answer lies in **ecological distribution**! Nigeria is an extraordinary geographical masterpiece. From the salt-spray mangrove swamps of the Atlantic coast in the Niger Delta to the arid sand dunes of the Sahel near the Sahara desert, Nigeria is divided into distinct **ecological and vegetation zones**. Each zone has its own unique combination of annual rainfall, sunshine duration, humidity, and soil type that dictates exactly which crops can thrive. In this lesson, you will journey across Nigeria's five major agricultural ecology zones and discover the scientific connection between climate, geography, and crop production!

## Key Terms
- Term: Ecology
- Definition: The branch of biological science that studies the relationships and interactions between living organisms (crops, animals) and their physical environment (climate, soil, water).
- Term: Ecological Distribution
- Definition: The geographical spread and localization of specific crop species across different climatic and vegetative zones where environmental conditions support their optimal growth.
- Term: Mangrove Swamp Forest
- Definition: The coastal wetland vegetation zone in southern Nigeria characterized by brackish/saline waters, daily tidal flooding, mangrove trees, and extremely heavy rainfall (>2,500mm annually).
- Term: Tropical Rainforest
- Definition: The dense forest zone in southern Nigeria with tall canopied evergreen trees, high humidity, rich organic soils, and prolonged rainfall (1,500–2,500mm annually).
- Term: Guinea Savanna
- Definition: The central "Middle Belt" grassland zone characterized by tall grasses, scattered deciduous trees, and moderate rainfall (1,000–1,500mm), serving as Nigeria's food basket for grains and tubers.
- Term: Sudan Savanna
- Definition: The northern semi-arid grassland zone characterized by short grasses, thorny acacia and baobab trees, low rainfall (600–1,000mm), and long dry seasons.
- Term: Sahel Savanna
- Definition: The extreme northern arid border zone characterized by sparse thorny scrub vegetation, minimal rainfall (<500mm), high temperatures, and sandy soils.
- Term: Agro-Ecological Zone
- Definition: A land resource mapping unit defined by climate, landform, soils, and vegetation that determines agricultural production potential.

## Core Concept
### Climate Drivers of Crop Distribution in Nigeria
[IMAGE: alt="Educational diagram and authentic visual representation of Ecological Distribution of Crops in Nigeria" caption="Ecological Distribution of Crops in Nigeria: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration"]

The distribution of crops across Nigeria is governed primarily by two climatic gradients that shift from south to north:
1. **Annual Rainfall:** Decreases sharply from over **3,000 mm** along the southern Atlantic coast to less than **500 mm** in the extreme north.
2. **Length of the Rainy Season:** Decreases from **9 to 11 months** in the south (with a bimodal rainfall pattern and a short August break) to **3 to 4 months** in the Sahel north (unimodal rainfall).

### Detailed Analysis of the Agro-Ecological Zones

#### 1. Mangrove Swamp and Freshwater Wetland Zone
- **Geographical Spread:** Coastal belt of the Niger Delta (Bayelsa, Rivers, Delta, Akwa Ibom, coastal Lagos, and Cross River).
- **Climatic Profile:** Rainfall exceeds **2,500 to 3,500 mm** annually; humidity is perpetually high (>85%); daily tidal inundation.
- **Predominant Agricultural Activities:**
  - Swamp rice cultivation (tidal and deepwater varieties).
  - Commercial fisheries, fish farming (aquaculture), shrimp, and crab harvesting.
  - Mangrove timber harvesting for firewood, piling poles, and charcoal.
  - Raffia palm tapping for palm wine and piassava fiber.

#### 2. Tropical Rainforest Zone
- **Geographical Spread:** Southern states (Ondo, Ogun, Osun, Oyo, Edo, Delta, Imo, Abia, Anambra, Enugu, Ebonyi, Cross River).
- **Climatic Profile:** High annual rainfall (**1,500 to 2,500 mm**) with a bimodal distribution; long growing season (8–10 months); deep, loamy soils rich in organic matter.
- **Characteristic Crops:**
  - **Permanent Tree Crops (Cash Crops):** Cocoa, Oil Palm, Para Rubber, Kolanut, Coffee, Citrus.
  - **Arable Roots and Tubers:** Cassava, White Yam, Water Yam, Cocoyam, Sweet Potato.
  - **Fruits and Vegetables:** Plantain, Banana, Pineapple, Pawpaw, Fluted Pumpkin (*Ugwu*), Bitter leaf.

#### 3. Guinea Savanna Zone (Southern & Northern Guinea)
- **Geographical Spread:** The broad "Middle Belt" and central Nigeria (Benue, Kogi, Kwara, Niger, Plateau, Taraba, Kaduna, Adamawa, FCT Abuja).
- **Climatic Profile:** Moderate rainfall (**1,000 to 1,500 mm**); 5 to 7 months rainy season; distinct dry season; open woodland with tall grasses (1.5 to 3 meters high) and fire-resistant trees.
- **Agricultural Title:** Known as **"The Food Basket of Nigeria."**
- **Characteristic Crops:**
  - **Tuber Crops:** Massive commercial production of Yam (Benue is the leading yam producer), Cassava, Sweet potato.
  - **Cereals:** Maize, Rice (especially in Niger, Benue, and Kaduna river basins), Sorghum (Guinea corn).
  - **Legumes and Oilseeds:** Soybean (Benue is leading producer), Cowpea, Groundnut, Sesame (Benniseed).
  - **Vegetables and Fruits:** Mangoes, Citrus, Tomatoes, Peppers.

#### 4. Sudan Savanna Zone
- **Geographical Spread:** Northern states (Kano, Katsina, Zamfara, Sokoto, Kebbi, Jigawa, Bauchi, Gombe, southern Borno and Yobe).
- **Climatic Profile:** Low rainfall (**600 to 1,000 mm**); short rainy season (3–4 months); long dry season (7–8 months) with intense Harmattan winds; short feather grasses, thorny acacia, baobab, and shea butter trees.
- **Characteristic Crops:**
  - **Drought-Tolerant Cereals:** Sorghum (*Guinea corn*), Pearl Millet, Wheat (under dry-season irrigation schemes like Kano River Project).
  - **Legumes and Oilseeds:** Groundnuts (historically famous Kano Groundnut Pyramids), Cowpeas, Sesame.
  - **Fiber and Cash Crops:** Cotton, Onions, Garlic, Chili peppers.
  - **Livestock Husbandry:** Prime livestock breeding center for cattle (Zebu, White Fulani), sheep, and goats due to abundant open grazing and absence of tsetse flies.

#### 5. Sahel Savanna Zone
- **Geographical Spread:** Extreme northern fringe of Nigeria bordering Niger Republic (northern Borno, northern Yobe, northern Sokoto).
- **Climatic Profile:** Very low rainfall (**less than 500 mm**); rainy season lasts barely 2 to 3 months; high evaporation rates; sandy, desert-like soils.
- **Characteristic Crops:**
  - Early-maturing Pearl Millet.
  - Gum Arabic (*Acacia senegal*).
  - Date Palm (*Phoenix dactylifera*).
  - Nomadic livestock grazing (camels, desert goats, sheep).

## Common Mistakes
- **Mistake 1:** Believing you can grow cocoa profitably in the Sudan or Sahel savanna. Cocoa trees require high rainfall (1,500–2,000mm) and high humidity, which do not exist in the dry northern savannas.
- **Mistake 2:** Assuming cattle can thrive equally across the southern rainforest without health hazards. The southern forest zone historically harbored dense populations of the **tsetse fly**, which transmits **trypanosomiasis** (sleeping sickness / nagana) to humped Zebu cattle.
- **Mistake 3:** Thinking Guinea Savanna only grows grasses. The Guinea Savanna is Nigeria's greatest food basket, producing the majority of commercial yams, maize, and soybeans.
- **Mistake 4:** Forgetting that irrigation can modify ecological limitations. In northern states like Kano and Kebbi, river irrigation dams (e.g., Tiga Dam) enable farmers to grow dry-season crops like wheat, rice, and tomatoes despite low natural rainfall.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Planning Agricultural Relocation Across Ecological Zones
*Question:* A commercial livestock and crop company operating in Ondo State (Rainforest Zone) plans to expand into large-scale commercial cattle breeding and commercial wheat production. Would you advise them to purchase land in Ondo State or relocate to a Sudan Savanna state like Kano or Jigawa? Provide scientific justifications.
*Step-by-step Solution:*
1. **Analyze Crop/Livestock Requirements:**
   - **Humped Zebu Cattle:** Require dry, low-humidity conditions, abundant open grassland grazing, and freedom from the tsetse fly (vector of trypanosomiasis).
   - **Wheat:** A temperate cereal crop that requires cool, dry nights, low humidity, and controlled irrigation during growth.
2. **Evaluate Ondo State (Rainforest):** High rainfall (>1,800mm), dense forest cover, high humidity (causing fungal rot in wheat), and tsetse fly presence make commercial cattle and wheat unviable.
3. **Evaluate Kano/Jigawa (Sudan Savanna):** Low humidity, expansive open pastures, absence of tsetse flies, cool harmattan nights, and established irrigation schemes (Tiga Dam, Hadejia-Jama'are) provide optimal conditions.
4. **Recommendation:** The company should relocate its wheat and cattle operations to the **Sudan Savanna** (Kano/Jigawa) to align with natural ecological requirements.

### Example 2: Analyzing Rainfall Gradient and Crop Adaptation
*Question:* Why do farmers in northern Borno State plant pearl millet instead of cocoa or plantain?
*Step-by-step Solution:*
1. **Examine the Ecological Zone:** Northern Borno lies in the **Sahel / Sudan Savanna** fringe, receiving under 500 mm of rain over a 60–80 day window.
2. **Examine Crop Physiology:**
   - Cocoa and plantain have large, thin leaves with high transpiration rates and shallow root systems that require continuous moisture (>1,500 mm) and high humidity. In Borno, they would dehydrate and die within weeks.
   - Pearl millet has narrow, waxy leaves, a deep root system, and a rapid 60-day maturation cycle specifically adapted to survive severe water deficits.
3. **Conclusion:** Farmers cultivate pearl millet because its physiological drought resistance matches the arid climate of the Sahel.
