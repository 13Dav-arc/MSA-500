## Hook
Walk down any busy street in your neighborhood, town, or city in Nigeria—whether in Aba, Ibadan, Kano, or Port Harcourt. You will see hundreds of vibrant businesses: the tailor stitching traditional attire in a small shop, the corner provision store selling bread and milk, the pharmacy dispensing medication, and the local law chambers with two partners' names boldly painted on the brass signboard. Have you ever wondered who owns these businesses? Who provided the startup money to rent the shop and buy the goods? If the business goes bankrupt and owes ₦5,000,000 to suppliers, who is legally forced to pay the debt? And if the owner suddenly falls ill or passes away, what happens to the shop? The answers to these crucial questions depend entirely on the legal structure chosen by the founders, known as the **Form of Business Ownership**! In this lesson, you will explore the two most widespread foundational forms of business organization: the **Sole Proprietorship (One-Man Business)** and the **Partnership**, uncover the legal concept of unlimited liability, and learn how business partnerships are forged!

## Key Terms
- Term: Business Ownership
- Definition: The legal and commercial structure that defines how an enterprise is owned, financed, governed, and held accountable for liabilities, and how its profits are distributed.
- Term: Sole Proprietorship (One-Man Business)
- Definition: An unincorporated commercial business owned, financed, managed, and controlled by a single individual who enjoys all profits and personally bears all losses and risks.
- Term: Partnership
- Definition: A legal relationship and commercial association between two or more persons (usually 2 to 20 individuals) who agree to pool capital, labor, and expertise to operate a business for profit.
- Term: Unlimited Liability
- Definition: A legal condition where the business owner(s) are personally liable for all business debts; if the firm fails, the owner's personal private assets (house, car, land) can be seized by creditors to settle unpaid debts.
- Term: Partnership Deed (Agreement)
- Definition: A formal, written legal document that outlines the terms, conditions, capital contributions, profit-sharing ratios, duties, and dissolution rules agreed upon by partners.
- Term: Active (Managing) Partner
- Definition: A partner who takes an active, daily operational role in the management and routine administration of the partnership business.
- Term: Dormant (Sleeping) Partner
- Definition: A partner who contributes financial capital and shares in profits and losses, but takes zero active part in the daily management of the firm.
- Term: Nominal (Quasi) Partner
- Definition: An individual who allows their famous name or reputation to be used by a partnership without contributing capital or taking part in management, but who shares liability to third parties.
- Term: Continuity of Existence
- Definition: The legal ability of a business enterprise to survive and continue operating indefinitely despite the death, bankruptcy, insanity, or retirement of its owners.
- Term: Trade Credit
- Definition: A short-term commercial financing arrangement where suppliers deliver goods to a merchant for resale with payment deferred until a future agreed date.

### 2. The Sole Proprietorship (One-Man Business)
The sole proprietorship is the oldest, simplest, and most common form of business enterprise in Nigeria and worldwide (e.g., local barbershops, grocery stores, vulcanizing stands, private tutoring services):

#### A. Key Characteristics
- **Single Ownership:** Established and owned by exactly one individual.
- **Easy Formation:** Requires little legal formality to start; simple registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) if operating under a business name.
- **Source of Capital:** Relies primarily on personal savings, gifts or loans from family and friends, and trade credit from wholesalers.
- **Unlimited Liability:** The business and the owner are legally one and the same (**no separate legal entity**). If the business incurs debts, creditors have the legal right to seize the owner's personal home, land, and furniture to recover their money!
- **Lack of Continuity:** The survival of the business is tied directly to the owner's lifespan and health. If the sole proprietor dies or becomes permanently incapacitated, the business frequently collapses.

#### B. Advantages & Disadvantages
- **Advantages:**
  - **Quick Decision-Making:** The owner acts instantly without consulting committees or partners.
  - **100% of Profits:** All net financial surplus belongs entirely to the sole proprietor.
  - **Close Customer Relations:** Direct, personal contact builds deep customer loyalty and tailored service.
  - **Complete Business Secrecy:** Financial records and trade recipes remain strictly confidential.
- **Disadvantages:**
  - **Unlimited Liability:** Extreme personal financial risk.
  - **Limited Capital:** Capital is restricted to what one person can save or borrow.
  - **Heavy Workload & Stress:** The owner must handle all roles—buyer, salesman, accountant, cleaner.
  - **Lack of Managerial Specialization:** One person cannot be an expert in accounting, marketing, and legal affairs.

### 3. The Partnership
When an enterprise outgrows the financial or managerial capacity of a single individual, two or more people join forces to form a **Partnership**:

#### A. Key Characteristics
- **Membership Size:** Minimum of **2 persons**; maximum of **20 persons** (extended up to 50 for specialized professional partnerships like law firms and accounting practices).
- **Formation via Partnership Deed:** While oral agreements are legally valid, prudent partners draft a written **Partnership Deed** (Articles of Partnership) to prevent future disputes.
- **Capital Contribution:** Partners pool their personal wealth, skills, and connections to raise substantially larger capital than a sole proprietor.
- **Mutual Agency:** Every partner acts as an agent of the firm; the commercial actions and contracts signed by one partner legally bind all other partners!

#### B. Types of Partners
1. **Active (Managing) Partner:** Contributes capital, participates daily in operations, and receives a salary in addition to profit share. Has unlimited liability.
2. **Dormant (Sleeping) Partner:** Contributes financial capital and shares profits/losses, but takes **no active part** in daily management. Retains full liability.
3. **Nominal (Quasi) Partner:** Contributes **no capital** and takes no part in management; merely lends their famous name, prestige, or goodwill to help the firm attract clients. However, they are legally liable to third parties who trusted the firm because of their name!
4. **General Partner:** Has unlimited liability for all debts of the firm.
5. **Limited Partner:** A partner whose liability is strictly limited to the amount of capital they invested. (In Nigeria, a limited partnership must have at least one general partner with unlimited liability).

#### C. Advantages & Disadvantages of Partnership
- **Advantages:**
  - **Greater Capital:** Combining wealth allows partners to buy larger inventories and modern equipment.
  - **Specialization of Labor:** One partner manages accounting, another handles sales, and a third directs technical operations.
  - **Shared Risk & Losses:** Financial losses are divided among partners rather than crushing one person.
  - **Better Credit Standing:** Commercial banks lend money more readily to partnerships than sole proprietors.
- **Disadvantages:**
  - **Unlimited Liability:** General partners risk losing personal property.
  - **Disagreements & Friction:** Quarrels, jealousy, and policy clashes can destroy the business.
  - **Action of One Binds All:** A reckless contract signed by an incompetent partner legally binds everyone.
  - **Instability & Dissolution:** The death, bankruptcy, or withdrawal of a key partner automatically dissolves the partnership unless the deed provides otherwise.

## Common Mistakes
- **Mistake 1:** Believing that "unlimited liability" means the owner has unlimited money.
  *Correction:* Unlimited liability means the owner has **unlimited personal financial risk**; creditors can legally seize the owner's private personal house, land, and car to pay unpaid business debts.
- **Mistake 2:** Assuming that a sleeping (dormant) partner has zero liability for business debts.
  *Correction:* A sleeping partner does not participate in daily management, but they still carry full **unlimited liability** for all debts incurred by the business!
- **Mistake 3:** Thinking partners always share profits equally.
  *Correction:* Profits are shared according to the **profit-sharing ratio** written in the Partnership Deed (e.g., 60:40 or based on capital contributed). Equal sharing occurs only if no written agreement exists.
- **Mistake 4:** Assuming a sole proprietorship cannot hire any employees.
  *Correction:* A sole proprietorship has only **one owner**, but that single owner can employ 50 or 100 workers, clerks, and assistants!

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Calculating Profit Distribution in a Partnership
*Scenario:* Aisha and Kemi establish a retail textile partnership in Kaduna named *"Radiant Fabrics"*. 
- Aisha contributes ₦3,000,000 to the business capital.
- Kemi contributes ₦1,000,000.
- Their Partnership Deed specifies that net profits and losses shall be shared in the ratio of their capital contributions ($3:1$).
- In their first financial year, the partnership earns a net profit of ₦1,600,000.

*Task:* Calculate the exact profit share received by Aisha and Kemi.

*Solution:*
1. $\text{Total Ratio Parts} = 3 + 1 = 4 \text{ parts}$.
2. **Aisha's Share:**
   $$\text{Aisha's Profit} = \frac{3}{4} \times ₦1,600,000 = ₦1,200,000.$$
3. **Kemi's Share:**
   $$\text{Kemi's Profit} = \frac{1}{4} \times ₦1,600,000 = ₦400,000.$$
*Check:* $₦1,200,000 + ₦400,000 = ₦1,600,000$. Aisha receives ₦1,200,000 and Kemi receives ₦400,000.

### Example 2: Analyzing the Danger of Unlimited Liability
*Scenario:* Danladi operates a small wholesale electronics shop as a sole proprietor in Kano. He purchases ₦8,000,000 worth of televisions on credit from an importer. A sudden electrical fire destroys his shop, which was completely uninsured. 
- After selling the scorched scrap metal, Danladi has only ₦1,000,000 in cash left in the business bank account.
- He still owes ₦7,000,000 to the importer.
- Danladi owns a private personal bungalow valued at ₦12,000,000 and a personal car.

*Task:* Explain how the legal doctrine of **Unlimited Liability** applies to Danladi.

*Legal Analysis:*
- Because Danladi operates as a **Sole Proprietor**, the law sees no legal separation between his business and his private personal life.
- Danladi possesses **Unlimited Liability**. The ₦1,000,000 in the business account will be paid to the importer, leaving an unpaid debt of ₦7,000,000.
- The court will issue a writ of execution empowering the creditor to seize and auction Danladi's **personal bungalow and private car** to recover the remaining ₦7,000,000!
