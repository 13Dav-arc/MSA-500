## Hook
Imagine that an ambitious young merchant in Kano opens an electronics shop. He buys 20 refrigerators on credit from a wholesale distributor, promising to pay ₦4,000,000 in two months. Next, he rents a shop in the market for ₦300,000, hires two sales assistants whom he pays ₦80,000 in monthly wages, and pays ₦15,000 for electricity to run the shop lights. At the end of the month, his cash box has ₦1,200,000 from customer sales. Does that ₦1,200,000 belong to him as pure profit? Absolutely not! If he foolishly celebrates and spends all that cash on personal parties, the wholesale distributor will drag him to court, the landlord will lock his shop doors, and the electricity company will disconnect his wires! To know the real financial truth, every merchant must master the double-entry treatment of **Liabilities** (what you owe), **Expenses** (what you spend to run the business), and **Revenue** (what you earn), and learn how to **Balance** accounts at month-end. In this lesson, you will master the full spectrum of double entry and become an expert in balancing accounts!

## Key Terms
- Term: Liability
- Definition: A financial obligation or debt owed by a business to external third parties (such as trade creditors, commercial banks, or suppliers) resulting from past transactions.
- Term: Creditor
- Definition: A person or commercial supplier to whom a business owes money for goods or services supplied on credit.
- Term: Debtor
- Definition: A customer or organization that owes money to a business for goods sold or services rendered on credit.
- Term: Expense
- Definition: The operational cost of resources consumed or services utilized in running daily business operations to generate revenue (e.g., rent, wages, electricity).
- Term: Revenue (Income)
- Definition: The gross monetary inflow earned by an enterprise through the sale of goods, provision of services, fees, or commissions.
- Term: Purchases Account
- Definition: The dedicated ledger account used strictly to record the cost of goods bought specifically for resale (trading merchandise).
- Term: Sales Account
- Definition: The dedicated ledger account used strictly to record the gross revenue generated from selling trading inventory.
- Term: Balancing an Account
- Definition: The accounting process of equalizing the debit and credit sides of a ledger account at the end of an accounting period by calculating the net difference.
- Term: Balance Carried Down (c/d)
- Definition: The closing balancing figure placed on the smaller side of an account to bring both column totals into mathematical equilibrium.
- Term: Balance Brought Down (b/d)
- Definition: The opening balance brought forward to the opposite (normal) side of an account at the start of the next accounting period.

## Core Concept
### 1. The Double Entry Treatment of Liabilities
[IMAGE: alt="Educational diagram and authentic visual representation of Double Entry Bookkeeping II" caption="Double Entry Bookkeeping II: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration"]

A **Liability** is money owed by the business to an outside party. Common examples include trade creditors, bank loans, and unpaid accrued bills.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   DOUBLE ENTRY RULES: LIABILITIES                      │
├────────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┤
│ INCREASE IN A LIABILITY            │ DECREASE IN A LIABILITY           │
│ When the business borrows money or │ When the business pays off or     │
│ buys goods on credit:              │ settles part of its debt:         │
│ • CREDIT the Liability / Creditor  │ • DEBIT the Liability / Creditor  │
│   Account (Gives credit benefit)   │   Account (Receives debt relief)  │
└────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### A. Credit Purchases of Trading Goods:
When a business buys merchandise for resale on credit from a supplier (e.g., Alhaji Bello):
1. **DEBIT Purchases Account:** Represents the acquisition of trading stock (merchandise asset).
2. **CREDIT Alhaji Bello (Creditor) Account:** Represents the supplier who gave the credit value.

*Example:* On January 10, bought goods on credit from Amadu Electronics for ₦100,000.
- **Debit:** Purchases Account ₦100,000.
- **Credit:** Amadu Electronics Account ₦100,000.

#### B. Credit Sales of Trading Goods:
When a business sells merchandise on credit to a customer (e.g., B. Solape):
1. **DEBIT B. Solape (Debtor) Account:** Represents the customer who received the goods and now owes money.
2. **CREDIT Sales Account:** Represents the revenue generated by the business.

*Example:* On January 24, sold goods on credit to B. Solape for ₦25,000.
- **Debit:** B. Solape Account ₦25,000.
- **Credit:** Sales Account ₦25,000.

### 2. Double Entry Treatment of Expenses
**Expenses** are the operational costs required to keep the business running. Common examples include shop rent, staff wages, electricity tariffs, telephone bills, motor vehicle fuel, and advertising.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     DOUBLE ENTRY RULES: EXPENSES                       │
│ Every expense consumed represents a operational value received:        │
│                                                                        │
│ • DEBIT the Specific Expense Account (Rent, Wages, Electricity)        │
│ • CREDIT the Cash Account (if paid in cash) or                         │
│   CREDIT the Bank Account (if paid by cheque / electronic transfer)    │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

*Example:* On June 12, paid shop rent of ₦60,000 by cheque, and paid cleaner's wages of ₦20,000 in cash.
1. **Rent Transaction:**
   - **Debit:** Rent Account ₦60,000.
   - **Credit:** Bank Account ₦60,000.
2. **Wages Transaction:**
   - **Debit:** Wages Account ₦20,000.
   - **Credit:** Cash Account ₦20,000.

---

### 3. Double Entry Treatment of Revenue
**Revenue** is the money earned from commercial activities:
- When cash is received from direct sales: **DEBIT Cash Account**, **CREDIT Sales Account**.
- When commission is received by cheque: **DEBIT Bank Account**, **CREDIT Commission Received Account**.
- When rent is received from subletting a warehouse: **DEBIT Cash/Bank Account**, **CREDIT Rent Received Account**.

---

### 4. Summary of the Universal Double Entry Rules (DEAD CLIC)
Accountants use the popular mnemonic **DEAD CLIC** to memorize double entries instantly:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                              D E A D                                   │
│    DEBIT:   Expenses (E)  |  Assets (A)  |  Drawings (D)               │
│             (These accounts increase with a DEBIT entry)               │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                              C L I C                                   │
│    CREDIT:  Liabilities (L) | Income/Revenue (I) | Capital (C)         │
│             (These accounts increase with a CREDIT entry)              │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 5. Step-by-Step Guide to Balancing Ledger Accounts
At the end of every week, month, or financial year, accounts must be balanced to determine their closing position:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     5 STEPS TO BALANCE A LEDGER ACCOUNT                │
│                                                                        │
│ Step 1: Add up the Debit side and Credit side on scrap paper.          │
│                                                                        │
│ Step 2: Determine which side is GREATER and which is SMALLER.          │
│                                                                        │
│ Step 3: Subtract the smaller side from the greater side.               │
│                                                                        │
│ Step 4: Insert the difference on the SMALLER side as "Balance c/d"     │
│         (carried down), dated on the last day of the month.            │
│                                                                        │
│ Step 5: Sum both sides on the same horizontal line, seal with double   │
│         underlines, and bring down the balance to the OPPOSITE side as │
│         "Balance b/d" (brought down) dated the 1st of the next month.  │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Comparative Matrix
| Account Category | What It Represents | Impact of a DEBIT Entry | Impact of a CREDIT Entry | Normal Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Asset** | What the business owns | **INCREASE** in asset | Decrease in asset | **Debit Balance** |
| **Liability** | What the business owes | Decrease in liability (debt repaid) | **INCREASE** in liability (debt borrowed) | **Credit Balance** |
| **Capital** | Owner's investment | Decrease in capital (drawings) | **INCREASE** in owner's capital | **Credit Balance** |
| **Expense** | Operational cost running firm | **INCREASE** in operating expenses | Reduction in expenses | **Debit Balance** |
| **Revenue / Income** | Earnings from sales/fees | Reduction in income | **INCREASE** in revenue earned | **Credit Balance** |

## Common Mistakes
1. **Debiting Cash When Paying an Expense:** When paying rent or wages, cash is *leaving* the business. Therefore, Cash must be **Credited**, and the Expense account must be **Debited**.
2. **Opening a "Goods Account" Instead of Purchases and Sales:** In modern accounting, there is no generic "Goods Account." Goods bought for resale are recorded in the **Purchases Account**; goods sold are recorded in the **Sales Account**.
3. **Placing Balance b/d on the Wrong Side:** Balance b/d must *always* be placed on the opposite side of Balance c/d, beneath the double underline, representing the side that had the greater sum!
4. **Failing to Seal Balancing Lines with Double Underlines:** Accounting conventions require a single line above the totals and a double underline beneath to indicate that the account is closed for that period.

## Worked Examples

### Scenario: Recording and Balancing Accounts
*Problem:* Record the following transactions for K. Danladi Trading Store in the appropriate T-accounts, and balance the Cash Account on June 30, 2026:
- June 01: Started business with ₦300,000 cash.
- June 05: Bought goods for resale on credit from B. Balogun for ₦120,000.
- June 12: Cash sales of goods ₦80,000.
- June 18: Paid shop electricity bill in cash ₦15,000.
- June 25: Paid staff salary in cash ₦45,000.

*Solution:*
1. **June 01:** Debit Cash ₦300,000; Credit Capital ₦300,000.
2. **June 05:** Debit Purchases ₦120,000; Credit B. Balogun (Creditor) ₦120,000.
3. **June 12:** Debit Cash ₦80,000; Credit Sales ₦80,000.
4. **June 18:** Debit Electricity Expense ₦15,000; Credit Cash ₦15,000.
5. **June 25:** Debit Salaries Expense ₦45,000; Credit Cash ₦45,000.

*Balancing the Cash Account on June 30:*
- Total Debit side = ₦300,000 (Capital) + ₦80,000 (Sales) = **₦380,000**.
- Total Credit side = ₦15,000 (Electricity) + ₦45,000 (Salaries) = **₦60,000**.
- Difference = ₦380,000 - ₦60,000 = **₦320,000**.
- Enter **Balance c/d ₦320,000** on Credit side (June 30).
- Total both sides to ₦380,000 and draw double underlines.
- Bring down **Balance b/d ₦320,000** on Debit side dated July 01.
