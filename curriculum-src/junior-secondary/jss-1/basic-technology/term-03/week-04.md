## Hook
When a carpenter attempts to saw a tenon joint or plane a rough board, timber cannot simply be held in place with one bare hand while operating a sharp tool with the other[cite: 2]. The forward push of a plane or the rapid stroke of a saw demands that timber be anchored firmly using specialized holding devices like vices, bench hooks, and sash clamps[cite: 2]. Once secured, shaping timber down to exact dimensions requires choosing between specialized bench saws for straight grain cutting, intricate curve-cutting saws, and precision bench planes designed to produce flat, smooth surfaces[cite: 2]. Mastering holding devices alongside cutting and paring tools ensures that timber is shaped safely, cleanly, and with dimensional accuracy[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Holding Devices
  Definition: Workshop tools and fixtures mounted on the workbench or clamped to workpieces to hold timber securely during cutting, planing, chiseling, and assembly[cite: 2].
- Term: Bench Vice (Fitter's Vice)
  Definition: A heavy clamping device equipped with broad jaws and a torsion bar used to hold timber firmly on the workbench during operations such as filing, bending, cutting, and assembly[cite: 2].
- Term: Sash Clamp
  Definition: A large, elongated bar clamp used for holding and drawing wooden boards and frames tightly together during gluing and assembly[cite: 2].
- Term: Bench Saws
  Definition: Straight-bladed hand saws used for linear cutting along or across the grain of wood (such as rip saws, cross-cut saws, panel saws, and tenon saws)[cite: 2].
- Term: Bench Planes
  Definition: Woodworking paring and smoothing tools equipped with a cutting iron, used to remove tool marks, dress timber surfaces, and produce flat surfaces and straight edges[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Workbench Holding Devices and Fittings
Woodworkers perform their constructions on sturdy workbenches fitted with specialized work-holding fixtures[cite: 2]:
1. **The Bench Vice (Fitter's Vice):**
   - Mounted to the side of the workbench to clamp and hold jobs during operations such as filing, bending, tapping, cutting, and assembling parts[cite: 2].
   - Features a screw mechanism driven by a torsion bar that opens and closes the clamping jaws[cite: 2].
2. **Other Types of Vices:**
   - **Hand Vice:** A compact, portable vice held in the hand, used for gripping small workpieces during drilling, riveting, and light bench tasks[cite: 2].
   - **Machine Vice:** Clamped or bolted directly to the table of machine tools (such as drill presses) to secure workpieces beneath cutting spindles[cite: 2].
3. **Care and Maintenance of the Vice:**
   - Always keep the vice clean and free of sawdust and metal chips[cite: 2].
   - Oil the internal screw threads regularly to ensure smooth movement[cite: 2].
   - Never use the vice jaws as an anvil for hammering[cite: 2].
   - Tighten the vice using hand force only on the torsion bar; never strike it with a hammer[cite: 2].
4. **Bench Stop and Bench Hook:**
   - **Bench Stop:** A strip of wood or metal fixed on top of the workbench that prevents wood from slipping off the bench top during planing[cite: 2].
   - **Bench Hook:** A portable wooden device hooked over the bench edge used to hold workpieces down during sawing or chiseling while protecting the bench surface from saw cuts[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: Workshop Clamps (G-Clamp, F-Clamp, and Sash Clamp)
Clamps are portable holding tools used to hold timber components together temporarily during fabrication or permanently while adhesive cures[cite: 2]:
1. **G-Clamp:**
   - A rigid metal frame shaped like the letter **"G"** fitted with a threaded clamping screw[cite: 2].
   - Ideal for holding small pieces of wood together or securing small jobs to the workbench[cite: 2].
2. **F-Clamp:**
   - Shaped like the letter **"F"** with a long sliding bar that provides an adjustable clamping capacity wider than a standard G-clamp[cite: 2].
   - Used like the G-clamp for holding workpieces securely during assembly[cite: 2].
3. **Sash Clamp:**
   - A heavy, elongated clamp consisting of a long steel bar, an adjustable tail stop, and a screw head[cite: 2].
   - Used for holding and drawing large wooden assemblies, wide table panels, door frames, and casework tightly together during gluing and assembly[cite: 2].

### Aspect 3: Straight Cutting: Bench Saws
Saws are cutting tools used to part timber stock[cite: 2]. Saws are broadly divided into **bench saws** (for straight cutting) and **curve cutting saws**[cite: 2]:
- **Rip Saw:** Designed specifically for cutting timber **along the grain** (lengthwise)[cite: 2]. Its teeth act like a row of small chisels that rip through wood fibers[cite: 2].
- **Cross-cut Saw:** Designed specifically for cutting timber **across the grain**[cite: 2]. Its teeth are shaped like knife points that sever fibers cleanly[cite: 2].
- **Panel Saw:** A saw with finer teeth used for sawing thin timber across the wood[cite: 2].
- **Tenon Saw:** Features a thin blade reinforced with a heavy brass or steel spine along the back to keep the blade rigid[cite: 2]. It is used for cutting shoulders to tenons and sawing precise recesses in boards[cite: 2].
- **Dovetail Saw:** Similar in design to the tenon saw but significantly shorter with finer teeth[cite: 2]. It is used for cutting fine joints (like dovetails) and light, delicate sawing tasks[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: Curved Cutting: Curve Cutting Saws
When timber designs require circular openings, hollow cutouts, or contoured edges, specialized curve cutting saws are used[cite: 2]:
1. **Coping Saw:**
   - Features a thin, flexible steel blade mounted inside a deep C-shaped metal frame[cite: 2].
   - Used for cutting curve marked lines in wood[cite: 2].
   - The blade can be rotated and adjusted to cut in any direction[cite: 2].
2. **Fret Saw:**
   - Features a deep metal frame and a very fine blade[cite: 2].
   - Used to cut intricate curves in thin wood of **$8\text{ mm}$ thickness or less**, and is commonly used for cutting plywood[cite: 2].
3. **Bow Saw:**
   - Features a wooden frame tensioned with a twisted cord or rod[cite: 2].
   - Used to cut along curve marked lines in heavier timber up to **$50\text{ mm}$ thick**[cite: 2].
4. **Compass Saw:**
   - Features a narrow, tapering blade attached to an open pistol-grip handle[cite: 2].
   - Used for cutting large interior curves and openings where a framed saw cannot reach[cite: 2].
5. **Keyhole Saw:**
   - Features a very slender, pointed blade designed to enter small pre-drilled holes[cite: 2].
   - Used mainly for cutting internal curves and narrow slots where the bow saw cannot be used[cite: 2].

### Aspect 5: Paring and Smoothing Tools: Types and Uses of Planes
Bench planes are shaving tools used to obtain a smooth surface, square true edges, and bring timber to required finished dimensions[cite: 2]:
1. **Jack Plane:**
   - The general-purpose workhorse of the wood shop, measuring roughly $350\text{ mm}$ to $400\text{ mm}$ in length[cite: 2].
   - Used to smoothen rough wood, remove initial saw marks from timber, and dress surfaces and edges down to approximate size[cite: 2].
2. **Trying Plane:**
   - The longest bench plane (typically $550\text{ mm}$ to $600\text{ mm}$ long)[cite: 2].
   - Used to produce true flat surfaces and perfectly straight edges on long boards[cite: 2].
3. **Smoothing Plane:**
   - A short, compact bench plane (measuring roughly $200\text{ mm}$ to $250\text{ mm}$)[cite: 2].
   - Used for the final cleaning and smoothing of timber surfaces and edges ready for assembling[cite: 2].
4. **Block Plane:**
   - A small, compact plane operated with one hand, featuring a low-angle cutting iron[cite: 2].
   - Used for planing end grain and small workpieces that are not easily accessible with large bench planes[cite: 2].
5. **Spoke Shave:**
   - A specialized curve-cutting plane featuring a narrow blade mounted between two lateral handles[cite: 2].
   - Used to dress and produce smooth curved surfaces and rounded edges on timber components like tool handles and chair legs[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using a rip saw to cut timber across the grain[cite: 2].
  Fact: Rip saw teeth are shaped to cut along the grain; using them across grain tears the wood fibers and leaves splintered edges[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Using a bench vice as an anvil for heavy hammering[cite: 2].
  Fact: Vice jaws are made of cast metal that cracks or fractures under hammering impacts; use an anvil for hammering and tighten vices with hand force only[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Attempting to smooth an inside curve with a trying plane or smoothing plane[cite: 2].
  Fact: Flat bench planes cannot ride inside curved surfaces; smooth curved edges using a spoke shave[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Using a tenon saw without supporting timber in a bench hook or vice[cite: 2].
  Fact: Unsupported sawing allows the timber to shift, causing binding and inaccurate joint shoulders; always use a bench hook or bench vice[cite: 2].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Selecting Clamps for Gluing a Tabletop
Context: A carpenter has planed four long timber boards and glued their edges to form a wide dining tabletop measuring $1800\text{ mm} \times 900\text{ mm}$[cite: 2].
Problem: Identify the appropriate clamping tool to pull the glued joints tightly together until the adhesive dries[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Tool Selected:** **Sash Clamps**[cite: 2].
- **Explanation:** Sash clamps feature long steel bars capable of spanning wide assemblies, drawing the boards tightly together along their glued edges[cite: 2]. G-clamps and F-clamps lack the span required for wide panel gluing[cite: 2].

### Example B: Cutting Internal Openings and Joints
Context: A student needs to cut out an internal circular speaker opening in a $15\text{ mm}$ plywood board and saw a clean mortise-and-tenon shoulder on a cabinet rail[cite: 2].
Problem: Select the correct saw for each cutting task[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Tenon Shoulder:** Cut using a **Tenon Saw**[cite: 2]. Its stiffened brass back keeps the blade rigid, producing straight cuts to the tenon shoulder line[cite: 2].
- **Internal Curved Opening:** Cut using a **Compass Saw** or **Keyhole Saw**[cite: 2]. After boring a starter hole with a brace, the slender blade enters the opening to cut the internal curve[cite: 2].

### Example C: Surface Preparation for Finished Furniture
Context: A student has sawn rough timber boards and must dress them into smooth components ready for joint assembly[cite: 2].
Problem: Outline the correct sequence of bench planes to use from rough timber to finished assembly[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Step 1 (Rough Dressing):** Use a **Jack Plane** to remove rough saw marks and dress surfaces down close to required dimensions[cite: 2].
- **Step 2 (Truing Surfaces):** Use a **Trying Plane** to produce true, flat faces and straight edges[cite: 2].
- **Step 3 (Final Finishing):** Use a **Smoothing Plane** to clear surfaces ready for final assembly[cite: 2].
