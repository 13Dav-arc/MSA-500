## Hook
Once a craftsman measures a length of timber, cutting straight into the wood without marking clear reference lines invites errors that ruin entire assemblies[cite: 2]. A saw blade cannot follow an invisible boundary, and a table leg cut at an improper angle leaves the furniture rocking on the workshop floor[cite: 2]. Setting and marking out tools translate raw numerical measurements into precise physical lines, right angles, and joint bevels directly on the grain of the wood[cite: 2]. Mastering tools like the try-square, mitre square, sliding bevel, and compass ensures that every saw cut, mortise, and chamfer aligns with millimeter accuracy[cite: 2].

## Key Terms
- Term: Marking Out
  Definition: The essential workshop operation that immediately follows measurement, involving the scribing or drawing of boundary lines, joint locations, and centers on a workpiece before cutting begins[cite: 2].
- Term: Try-Square
  Definition: An L-shaped woodworking tool consisting of a thick wooden or cast-iron stock and a steel blade fixed at an angle of $90^\circ$, used to mark right-angled lines and test the squareness of planed timber edges[cite: 2].
- Term: Mitre Square
  Definition: A marking tool with a blade permanently set into its stock at an angle of $45^\circ$, used specifically for marking and checking mitre angles for frames and corner joints[cite: 2].
- Term: Sliding Bevel
  Definition: An adjustable marking tool with an adjustable blade held by a locking screw, used for duplicating, transferring, and setting out non-standard angles and bevels[cite: 2].
- Term: Compass
  Definition: An instrument with two legs joined by a friction pivot, fitted with a pencil lead, used to scribe circular arcs, circles, and curved profiles on timber[cite: 2].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: The Role and Sequence of Marking Out in Woodwork
In both woodwork and metalwork, **marking out** is the direct operational step that immediately follows measurement[cite: 2]:
- Measurement determines and records the physical distance between two points, while marking out clearly establishes the physical boundaries, cut lines, shoulders, and centers directly onto the timber workpiece[cite: 2].
- Attempting to saw, plane, or chisel without marking out leads to dimension errors, uneven joints, and wasted timber stock[cite: 2].
- Marking out tools include the **try-square**, **sliding bevel**, **mitre square**, **compass**, and **pairs of dividers**[cite: 2].

### Aspect 2: The Try-Square (Structure and Functions)
The **try-square** is one of the most frequently used setting and testing tools in the wood shop[cite: 2]:
- **Construction:** It consists of two primary components joined at an angle of $90^\circ$: a thick **stock** (traditionally made of wood or cast iron with an internal brass face plate) and a thin steel **blade**[cite: 2].
- **Core Functions:**
  1. **Marking Lines at Right Angles ($90^\circ$):** The stock is pressed against the planed face or edge of the timber while a pencil or marking knife draws a line along the blade perpendicular to the edge[cite: 2].
  2. **Testing Squareness:** It is placed across timber faces and edges to verify that they are planed flat and meet at an angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 2]. If light shines under the blade when resting against the wood, the edge is not square and requires further planing[cite: 2].

### Aspect 3: The Mitre Square (Marking $45^\circ$ Angles)
When joining two pieces of wood to form corner frames (such as picture frames, window architraves, or door moldings), right angles are often formed by joining two $45^\circ$ cuts called **mitres**[cite: 2]:
- **Design:** The blade of the mitre square is fixed into the stock at an angle of **$45^\circ$** (or $135^\circ$ internally)[cite: 2].
- **Function:** It is specifically used to mark and verify lines at an angle of $45^\circ$ across the face or edge of a board before sawing[cite: 2].
- Unlike the sliding bevel, the angle on a mitre square is fixed permanently during manufacturing to maintain $45^\circ$ accuracy[cite: 2].

### Aspect 4: The Sliding Bevel (Duplicating and Transferring Angles)
Not all woodwork joints meet at standard angles of $90^\circ$ or $45^\circ$[cite: 2]. Structures like roof trusses, splayed leg stools, dovetail pins, and decorative chamfers require variable angles[cite: 2]:
- **Construction:** The sliding bevel features a slotted steel blade that pivots inside a wooden or metal stock and is clamped at any chosen angle by a thumbscrew or locking lever[cite: 2].
- **Function:** It is used for **duplicating angles** from an existing template or drawing and for **setting out bevels** across timber stock[cite: 2].
- **Angular Constraint:** The lines and angles set by a sliding bevel should not be $45^\circ$ (for which a mitre square is dedicated) or $90^\circ$ (for which a try-square is dedicated); it is reserved for irregular, adjustable inclinations[cite: 2].

[IMAGE: alt="Comparison diagram showing a try-square (90 degrees), a mitre square (45 degrees), and an adjustable sliding bevel set to an arbitrary angle" caption="Comparison of Angular Setting Tools: Try-Square, Mitre Square, and Sliding Bevel"]

### Aspect 5: Compasses and Setting Out Arcs
In woodwork, curved lines, rounded corners, and circular recesses are set out using compasses[cite: 2]:
- **Function:** A pencil compass is used to mark and scribe the arcs of a circle, curved profiles, and rounded shoulders directly onto the wood surface[cite: 2].
- **Setting the Radius:** The compass legs are opened, and the sharp steel pivot point and pencil tip are adjusted to the required radius against a metric rule before scribing the arc[cite: 2].
- **Dividers Integration:** While a compass carries a pencil lead to mark visible lines, dividers feature two steel points used to step off equal spaces and transfer layout points from drawing plans to timber faces[cite: 2].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Sawing timber immediately after taking measurements without marking out guidelines[cite: 2].
  Fact: Marking out is the required intermediate step after measurement; sawing without lines produces inaccurate, uneven cuts[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Using a sliding bevel to mark standard $45^\circ$ mitre cuts[cite: 2].
  Fact: Standard $45^\circ$ angles should be marked with a dedicated mitre square to ensure consistent accuracy without slippage[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Letting the stock of the try-square tilt away from the timber edge while drawing lines[cite: 2].
  Fact: The stock must be pressed firmly flush against the face edge of the wood; if the stock tilts, the line will not be a true $90^\circ$ right angle[cite: 2].
- Mistake: Dropping try-squares or using them to pry open paint cans.
  Fact: Impact or prying loosens the rivets between the stock and blade, ruining the $90^\circ$ alignment of the tool[cite: 2].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Preparing a Timber Board for Squaring
Context: A student has planed the face side and face edge of a wooden plank and needs to verify if the two surfaces are perpendicular[cite: 2].
Problem: Explain the procedure for testing the squareness of the edge using a try-square[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Step 1:** Hold the wooden plank steady on the workbench or mount it in the bench vice[cite: 2].
- **Step 2:** Place the stock of the try-square flush against the planed face side of the timber[cite: 2].
- **Step 3:** Lower the steel blade gently across the planed face edge[cite: 2].
- **Step 4:** Look toward a light source; if no light passes between the blade and the timber edge, the edge is square ($90^\circ$)[cite: 2]. If light is visible, note the high spots and plane them down[cite: 2].

### Example B: Marking a $45^\circ$ Picture Frame Joint
Context: A student is cutting pieces of molding to make a wooden photo frame[cite: 2].
Problem: State which tool to select and explain how to mark the cut lines[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Tool Selected:** **Mitre Square**[cite: 2].
- **Procedure:**
  - Measure the required frame length with a metric rule and mark the endpoint with a pencil mark[cite: 2].
  - Press the stock of the mitre square firmly against the outer edge of the molding[cite: 2].
  - Align the $45^\circ$ blade directly over the measured mark and scribe a line across the face of the wood[cite: 2].

### Example C: Transferring a Splayed Angle for a Stool Leg
Context: A carpenter needs to cut four stool legs inclined at an angle of $80^\circ$ to the tabletop[cite: 2].
Problem: Describe how to set, duplicate, and transfer this angle across all four legs[cite: 2].
Fix:
- **Tool Selected:** **Sliding Bevel**[cite: 2].
- **Procedure:**
  - Loosen the thumbscrew on the sliding bevel and set the blade against a protractor or drawing template at $80^\circ$[cite: 2].
  - Tighten the thumbscrew firmly to lock the blade in position[cite: 2].
  - Press the stock against the reference edge of each stool leg and draw the angled cut line along the blade, duplicating the angle across all four legs[cite: 2].
