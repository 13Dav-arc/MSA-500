## Hook
Every line in a technical drawing carries a specific meaning, functioning like words and grammar in a written language[cite: 1]. A thick, solid line tells an engineer that an edge is clearly visible, while a line of short dashes reveals hidden chambers inside a machine part that the eye cannot see directly from the outside[cite: 1]. Without standardized lines, an artisan would confuse a dimension mark with a solid steel wall or mistake an imaginary centerline for a physical cut[cite: 1]. Mastering the standard technical lines—from thick continuous outlines to thin zig-zag break lines and arrowheads—allows you to draft plans that can be read and interpreted across any engineering workshop in the world[cite: 1].

## Key Terms
- Term: Technical Line Convention
  Definition: The standardized system of varying line thicknesses, patterns, and forms used in technical drawing to convey specific geometric features, boundaries, and measurements[cite: 1].
- Term: Thick Continuous Line
  Definition: A bold, solid line used to depict visible outlines and prominent physical edges of an object[cite: 1].
- Term: Thin Continuous Line
  Definition: A light, unbroken line used for dimension lines, projection lines, and construction lines[cite: 1].
- Term: Thin Long Chain Line
  Definition: A line consisting of alternating long lines and short dashes or dots, used to indicate centerlines, path lines of motion, and cutting planes[cite: 1].
- Term: Hidden Detail Line (Thin Short Dashes)
  Definition: A line composed of short, evenly spaced dashes used to represent edges, outlines, and surfaces that are hidden from direct view[cite: 1].

## Core Concept
### Aspect 1: Using the Tee-Square and Set-Squares for Board Practice
Combining the tee-square and set-squares on the drawing board allows draftsmen to produce lines in exact orientations[cite: 1]:
1. **Drawing Horizontal Lines:**
   - The stock of the tee-square is held firmly against the left working edge of the board with the non-drawing hand[cite: 1].
   - Horizontal lines are drawn smoothly along the top edge of the tee-square blade from left to right[cite: 1].
2. **Drawing Vertical Lines:**
   - The set-square sits squarely on top of the horizontal blade of the tee-square[cite: 1].
   - Vertical lines are drawn upward along the vertical edge of the set-square, producing true perpendicular lines at an angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 1].
3. **Drawing Inclined Lines and Angles:**
   - By sliding a $45^\circ$ or $30^\circ/60^\circ$ set-square along the tee-square blade, inclined lines can be drawn directly at angles of $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$[cite: 1].
   - Combining both set-squares upon the tee-square enables the drafting of angles such as $15^\circ$ ($45^\circ - 30^\circ$) and $75^\circ$ ($45^\circ + 30^\circ$)[cite: 1].
4. **Measuring Dimensions:**
   - Linear lengths along vertical and diagonal lines can be measured and marked directly using the millimeter graduations on the set-square while it rests on the tee-square blade[cite: 1].

### Aspect 2: Visible Outlines vs. Construction and Dimension Lines
The contrast between line thicknesses gives technical drawings depth and structural hierarchy[cite: 1]:
1. **Thick Continuous Line:**
   - **Form:** A solid, continuous dark line drawn with an HB or medium pencil[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:** Strictly designated for **visible outlines and edges** of the object being drawn[cite: 1]. It defines the physical boundaries of the component that are directly visible to an observer[cite: 1].
2. **Thin Continuous Line:**
   - **Form:** A faint, sharp, unbroken line drawn using a hard pencil such as 2H[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:**
     - **Dimension lines:** Lines terminating with arrowheads that indicate the length, width, or diameter of an object[cite: 1].
     - **Projection lines:** Lines extending outward from an object feature to show where a dimension begins and ends[cite: 1].
     - **Construction lines:** Initial faint layout lines used to position and construct geometric shapes before darkening final outlines[cite: 1].

### Aspect 3: Centerlines and Hidden Detail Lines
Lines that represent non-visible or abstract geometric properties follow strict pattern conventions[cite: 1]:
1. **Thin Long Chain Line:**
   - **Form:** Alternating long lines and short dashes or dots (e.g., long line, short gap, short dash, short gap, long line)[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:**
     - Indicating **centre lines** of symmetric features, cylinders, holes, and circles[cite: 1].
     - Marking **path lines for indicating movement** of mechanical links or rotating gears[cite: 1].
     - Representing **cutting and viewing planes** when an object is sliced open to reveal internal views[cite: 1].
2. **Thin Short Dashes:**
   - **Form:** A series of short, uniform dashes (approximately $2\text{ mm}$ to $3\text{ mm}$ long with $1\text{ mm}$ spaces between them)[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:** Strictly used for **hidden outlines and edges**[cite: 1]. When a hole, interior recess, or rear edge is blocked from view by a foreground solid surface, it is drawn using short dashes[cite: 1].

### Aspect 4: Break Lines and Boundary Conventions
When drafting long uniform shafts or partial component views, special break lines are employed to save space on the paper sheet[cite: 1]:
1. **Thick Continuous Wavy Line:**
   - **Form:** A thick, freehand wavy line drawn continuously[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:** Used for **short break lines and boundary lines** where an object is broken off irregularly to reveal a cross-section[cite: 1].
2. **Thin Continuous Wavy Line:**
   - **Form:** A light, thin continuous wavy line[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:** Used for **limits of partial views** when only a portion of a larger component is drawn[cite: 1].
3. **Thin Ruled Line with Short Zig-Zags:**
   - **Form:** A straight, ruled line interrupted at intervals by small sharp zig-zag strokes[cite: 1].
   - **Uses:** Designated for **long break lines** across lengthy continuous parts (such as long structural pipes or timber beams) to condense their representation within the drawing sheet[cite: 1].

### Aspect 5: Dimensioning Conventions and Arrowheads
Dimensioning provides the manufacturing team with exact physical measurements[cite: 1]:
- **Arrowheads:**
  - **Form:** Pointed triangular terminations constructed with a length approximately three times their width, filled in solid black[cite: 1].
  - **Uses:** Strictly **used at the end of dimension lines** to touch the projection lines and establish the precise boundaries of the measurement[cite: 1].
- **Dimension Placement:**
  - The thin continuous dimension line is placed parallel to the edge being measured[cite: 1].
  - Projection lines extend perpendicularly from the feature, leaving a small gap of $1\text{ mm}$ to $2\text{ mm}$ from the outline[cite: 1].
  - Dimension figures (numbers in millimeters) are lettered either centrally above the dimension line or into an opening in the line[cite: 1].

## Common Mistakes
- Mistake: Using a thick continuous line for dimension or construction lines[cite: 1].
  Fact: Dimension and construction lines must always be thin and faint so they do not overpower the actual physical shape of the object[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Drawing hidden details using solid continuous lines[cite: 1].
  Fact: Solid continuous lines indicate visible surfaces; concealed features must strictly be drawn with thin short dashes[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Constructing arrowheads with wide, blunt, or unproportioned shapes[cite: 1].
  Fact: Arrowheads should be sharp, elongated points (length roughly three times width) pointing cleanly to projection lines[cite: 1].
- Mistake: Drawing vertical lines with a set-square placed directly on the board without resting on the tee-square[cite: 1].
  Fact: The set-square must sit squarely on the horizontal blade of the tee-square to maintain true $90^\circ$ perpendicularity[cite: 1].

## Worked Examples
### Example A: Selecting Lines for a Cylindrical Component
Context: A student needs to prepare an engineering drawing of a hollow steel shaft measuring $150\text{ mm}$ long and $40\text{ mm}$ in diameter, with a central hole running along its axis[cite: 1].
Problem: Identify the appropriate technical line types required to represent its visible exterior, internal hole, symmetry axis, and overall length[cite: 1].
Fix:
- **Visible Outer Shaft Boundaries:** Drawn using a **Thick continuous line**[cite: 1].
- **Internal Concealed Hole:** Drawn using **Thin short dashes** (hidden outlines)[cite: 1].
- **Longitudinal Center Axis:** Drawn through the center using a **Thin long chain line**[cite: 1].
- **Overall Length Dimension Line:** Drawn alongside the shaft using a **Thin continuous line** ending with **Arrowheads** touching projection lines[cite: 1].

### Example B: Condensing an Extended Beam Drawing
Context: A wooden timber beam is $4000\text{ mm}$ long with a uniform cross-section, making it too long to fit completely onto an A3 drawing sheet at a $1:1$ scale[cite: 1].
Problem: State which line type allows the draftsman to break and condense the middle section of the beam on paper[cite: 1].
Fix:
- **Selected Line Type:** **Thin ruled line with short zig-zags**[cite: 1].
- **Application:** The draftsman draws the two ends of the beam and connects them with a straight ruled line interrupted by sharp zig-zags, indicating a **long break line** that shortens the drawing while recording the true $4000\text{ mm}$ dimension above the line[cite: 1].

### Example C: Constructing Perpendicular and Inclined Lines
Context: A draftsman needs to construct a vertical wall and an inclined roof strut at an angle of $60^\circ$ to the floor[cite: 1].
Problem: Describe how the tee-square and set-squares are positioned on the board to draw both features accurately[cite: 1].
Fix:
- **Floor Line (Horizontal):** Align the tee-square stock on the left board edge and draw a line along the top of the blade[cite: 1].
- **Vertical Wall ($90^\circ$):** Place the $30^\circ/60^\circ$ or $45^\circ$ set-square sitting squarely on the tee-square blade and draw upward along its vertical edge[cite: 1].
- **Inclined Roof Strut ($60^\circ$):** Slide the $30^\circ/60^\circ$ set-square along the tee-square blade and draw along the angled edge that makes $60^\circ$ with the horizontal blade[cite: 1].
