Why This Matters to You

If you tell a farmer that you saw a "coat" chewing grass in his field, he might wonder why a piece of clothing has an appetite—unless he realizes you meant a "goat"! The difference between voiceless /k/ and voiced /g/ hinges on a single microscopic muscular action: the vibration of your vocal cords. In written grammar, ensuring that singular subjects pair smoothly with singular verbs—the golden law of Concord—is what elevates your writing from clumsy sentences to scholarly excellence. As we arrive at the culmination of our first term, synthesizing our skills in speech, grammar, varied writing styles, and literary analysis equips you to express your thoughts with undeniable authority and distinction.

Key Terms

Velar Plosive A consonant stop sound produced by raising the back of the tongue to form an airtight seal against the soft palate (velum), followed by an explosive burst of released air (such as /k/ and /g/).
Subject-Verb Concord The fundamental grammatical agreement in number (singular or plural) and person between the subject of a sentence and its finite verb.
Sentence Pattern The structural arrangement and grammatical sequence of essential functional sentence components (Subject, Verb, Object, Complement, Adverbial).
Onomatopoeia A literary sound device wherein a word phonetically imitates, resembles, or suggests the natural sound that it describes (e.g., *buzz*, *hiss*, *clatter*, *splash*).
Alliteration The repetition of identical consonant sounds at the beginning of adjacent or closely connected words within a poetic line or sentence (e.g., *"Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers"*).

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Velar Plosives /k/ and /g/

Both /k/ and /g/ are velar plosives articulated by pressing the back of the tongue against the soft palate (velum).

/k/ (Voiceless Velar Plosive):
Articulated with the back of the tongue raised against the soft palate to block the airstream completely. The vocal cords do not vibrate (voiceless). In initial stressed positions, it is aspirated with an audible puff of air.
Common spelling symbols: `c` (cat, coat, cup, clean), `k` (king, keep, kettle), `ck` (back, lock, sick), `q` or `qu` (queen /kwiːn/, equal), and `ch` in Greek-origin words (school, ache, chemist, character).
(Note: `k` is silent before `n` in initial positions: knife, knee, knot, know, knight).
/g/ (Voiced Velar Plosive):
Articulated identically against the soft palate, but the vocal cords vibrate energetically (voiced). It is never aspirated.
Common spelling symbols: `g` (go, get, goat, big, bag), `gg` (egg, struggle, dagger), and `gu` (guest, guide, guard, guilt).
(Note: `g` is silent before `n` or `m` in words like gnat, gnaw, sign, reign, phlegm, foreign).

| Word Position | Voiceless Plosive /k/ | Voiced Plosive /g/ |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Initial | coat, cap, class, cold, curl | goat, gap, glass, gold, girl |

| Medial | baker, locker, anchor, market | bagger, logger, anger, target |

| Final | back, lock, pick, dock, leak | bag, log, pig, dog, league |

Aspect 2: Structure — Basic Sentence Patterns and Subject-Verb Concord

1.
Five Core Sentence Structural Patterns:
SV (Subject + Verb): "The birds [S] sang [V]." / "The sun [S] shines [V]."
SVO (Subject + Verb + Direct Object): "The girl [S] kicked [V] the football [O]."
SVC (Subject + Verb + Complement): "Amina [S] is [V] a doctor [C]." / "The soup [S] tastes [V] delicious [C]."
SVOO (Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object): "The teacher [S] gave [V] the student [IO] a prize [DO]."
SVOC (Subject + Verb + Direct Object + Object Complement): "The class [S] elected [V] Chidi [O] prefect [C]."
1.
Cardinal Rules of Subject-Verb Concord (Agreement):
Rule 1 (Number Agreement): A singular subject requires a singular verb; a plural subject requires a plural verb ("The student writes"; "The students write").
Rule 2 (Compound Subjects with 'And'): Two or more subjects joined by and generally take a plural verb ("Chidi and Ngozi are diligent"). (Exception: When two nouns express a single unified concept, they take a singular verb: "Bread and butter is my favorite breakfast").
Rule 3 (The Proximity Rule with 'Either...Or' / 'Neither...Nor'): When subjects are joined by either...or or neither...nor, the verb agrees in person and number with the closer subject ("Neither the teacher nor the students were present"; "Neither the students nor the teacher was present").
Rule 4 (Parenthetical Interventions): Words introduced by as well as, together with, along with, in addition to, or accompanied by do not affect the subject's number ("The principal, together with his staff, is attending the meeting").
Rule 5 (Indefinite Pronouns): Pronouns such as everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, neither, either, each are grammatically singular and require singular verbs ("Each of the boys has a notebook").
Rule 6 (Plural in Form, Singular in Meaning): Nouns denoting academic subjects or diseases ending in "-s" (Mathematics, Physics, Civics, Measles, News) take singular verbs ("Mathematics is my best subject"; "The news was encouraging").

Reading Passage:

> In the competitive arena of modern education, academic excellence is a universally celebrated milestone. Students, parents, and teachers invest tremendous energy, financial resources, and sleepless nights preparing for high-stakes promotional and national examinations. However, true education transcends the mere acquisition of stellar grades on a printed terminal report card. Intellectual competence that is detached from uncompromising moral integrity produces educated scoundrels who weaponize their knowledge to defraud society, embezzle public funds, and subvert the rule of law.

>

> In recent years, educational authorities have waged an unyielding war against examination malpractice across Nigeria. Unscrupulous syndicates, compromised invigilators, and deceitful candidates employ covert technological gadgets and unauthorized cheat sheets to subvert academic testing protocols. Such dishonest practices erode public confidence in certified credentials and devalue the genuine sweat of diligent scholars. A student who cheats to attain an unearned distinction has already surrendered their self-respect, trading lifelong honor for momentary, hollow praise.

>

> True academic excellence thrives solely in an environment founded upon transparent honesty, personal diligence, and moral rectitude. When a scholar embraces the rigorous discipline of personal study, confronts difficult mathematical equations with resilient perseverance, and accepts honest mistakes as constructive learning milestones, they cultivate an unshakeable character. Society does not solely require brilliant doctors, inventive engineers, and astute accountants; it desperately hungers for upright citizens whose professional brilliance is anchored in profound ethical integrity.

Aspect 4: Composition — Sessional Composition Review: Comparing Core Writing Genres

Throughout Term 01, we have explored distinct modes of written expression. Mastering English requires selecting the right genre, register, and organizational strategy for your communicative purpose:

| Composition Genre | Primary Objective | Dominant Grammatical Features | Key Structural Markers |

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Narrative | Recount a story or sequence of events in time | Past simple tense, active verbs, dialogue, temporal sequence | Beginning, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution |

| Descriptive | Paint a vivid sensory picture of a person, place, or thing | Present simple tense, vivid adjectives, sensory imagery, spatial order | Top-to-bottom, near-to-far, panoramic overview to specific details |

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Literary Synthesis: Genres and Sound Devices

To culminate our first-term literary appreciation, we synthesize the three overarching genres and examine powerful sound devices:

1.
Synthesis of the Three Genres:
Prose: Narrative text in continuous paragraphs (Fiction: novels, short stories; Non-Fiction: biographies, essays).
Drama: Enacted theatrical performance in dialogue, acts, and scenes (Tragedy: catastrophe and sorrow; Comedy: humor and joyful reconciliation).
Poetry: Concentrated rhythmic verse in lines and stanzas (Couplets, Quatrains, Rhyme Scheme, Sensory Imagery).
1.
Core Literary Sound Devices:
Alliteration: Repetition of identical initial consonant sounds in closely linked words (e.g., "The soft sea sighed silently").
Assonance: Repetition of identical or similar vowel sounds within nearby words (e.g., "Fleet feet sweep by sleeping greens").
Onomatopoeia: Words that imitate the acoustic sound of what they name (e.g., "The dry leaves crunched underfoot"; "The bees buzzed around the hive").
Irony: A contrast between expectation and reality, or saying the opposite of what is actually meant (e.g., "The pilot had a morbid fear of heights").

Comparing Key Sound, Grammar, and Literary Distinctions

Plosive /k/ vs Plosive /g/

Voicing State and Articulation

/k/ is voiceless with no vocal cord vibration and aspirated initially (as in 'coat'); /g/ is voiced with vocal cord vibration and unaspirated (as in 'goat').

Singular vs Plural Concord

Agreement in Number

Singular subjects require singular verbs with '-s' inflection in the present tense (e.g., 'he writes'); plural subjects require base verbs without '-s' (e.g., 'they write').

Alliteration vs Assonance

Consonant vs Vowel Sound Repetition

Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds (e.g., 'proud princes'); assonance repeats internal vowel sounds (e.g., 'deep green sea').

Simile vs Metaphor vs Personification

Nature of Figurative Identification

Simile uses 'like/as' to compare; metaphor identifies directly without 'like/as'; personification assigns human qualities to non-human entities.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Violating concord with plural-looking nouns that are singular in meaning, such as writing *"Mathematics are difficult"* or *"The news are bad"*.

Scientific Fact

Academic subjects like *Mathematics* and mass nouns like *news* are singular and take singular verbs (*"Mathematics **is**..."*; *"The news **was**..."*).

Common Mistake

Violating the proximity rule with *either...or*, writing *"Neither the boys nor the teacher were present"*.

Scientific Fact

The verb must agree with the closer subject; since *the teacher* is singular and closest to the verb, write *"was present"*.

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the initial "k" in words like *knife*, *knee*, and *know*.

Scientific Fact

In standard English, initial "k" before "n" is completely silent.
