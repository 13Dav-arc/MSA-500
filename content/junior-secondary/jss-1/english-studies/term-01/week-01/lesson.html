Key Terms

Pure Vowel (Monophthong) A vowel sound produced with an unchanging vocal tract posture and steady tongue position from the beginning of its articulation to the end, without gliding toward another sound.
Proper Noun A specific name assigned to a particular individual person, place, day of the week, month, institution, or deity, which is always capitalized in English writing.
Orientation A formal period or program organized by an educational institution to familiarize newly admitted students with academic rules, facilities, routines, and campus geography.
Topic Sentence The central organizing sentence of a paragraph that clearly expresses its main idea, guiding all subsequent supporting and illustrative details.
Literature Any creative imaginative work of artistic value expressed in written text or oral performance that mirrors human experiences, emotions, culture, and society.

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Organs of Speech & Pure Vowels /ɪ/ and /iː/

Human speech sounds are produced by modifying an airstream expelled from the lungs using active articulators (such as the lower lip, tongue tip, blade, and back) against passive articulators (such as the upper front teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, and soft palate / velum).

/ɪ/ (Short Close Front Unrounded Vowel):
Articulated with the front of the tongue raised toward the hard palate, just below the close position. The lips are loosely spread, and the vocal cords vibrate (voiced sound). It is short in duration.
Common spelling symbols: `i` (sit, hit, pin, tin), `y` (myth, hymn, rhythm), `e` (pretty, English), and `ui` (build, guilt).
/iː/ (Long Close Front Unrounded Vowel):
Articulated with the front of the tongue raised high toward the hard palate in a fully close position. The lips are firmly spread into a smile, and the vocal cords vibrate. The colon mark (ː) indicates a long, prolonged vowel duration.
Common spelling symbols: `ee` (see, meet, feed), `ea` (seat, meat, read), `ie` (thief, piece), `ei` (receive, deceit), and `e` (he, she, be).

| Word Position | Short Vowel /ɪ/ | Long Vowel /iː/ |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Initial | it, ill, in, ink, instant | eat, each, east, eaves, eagle |

| Medial | ship, fit, live, slip, grin | sheep, feet, leave, sleep, green |

| Final | (rarely final in standard RP) | see, tree, flee, key, plea |

Aspect 2: Structure — Parts of Speech Overview & Nouns

All English words belong to eight grammatical classes called the Parts of Speech: Nouns, Pronouns, Verbs, Adjectives, Adverbs, Prepositions, Conjunctions, and Interjections.

Definition of a Noun: A noun is a naming word for a person, animal, place, thing, substance, state, quality, or idea.
Major Classes of Nouns:
1.
Proper Nouns: Particular names of persons, specific places, days, months, and organizations; always capitalized (e.g., Amina, Lagos, Nigeria, Monday, September, United Nations).
2.
Common Nouns: General names shared by all members of a class or category (e.g., student, classroom, country, river, teacher).
3.
Concrete Nouns: Physical entities that can be touched, seen, heard, tasted, or smelled (e.g., desk, chalkboard, water, mango, bell).
4.
Abstract Nouns: Names of qualities, concepts, emotions, states, or ideas that cannot be perceived with physical senses (e.g., courage, honesty, patriotism, anger, wisdom, poverty).

Aspect 4: Composition — Introduction to Continuous Writing & The Paragraph

Continuous writing refers to the structured composition of connected sentences and paragraphs to communicate thoughts, recount events, describe scenes, or defend arguments.

What is a Paragraph?

A paragraph is a group of related sentences focused entirely on developing a single central theme or idea.

Essential Structural Components of an Effective Paragraph:
1.
Topic Sentence: Typically the first or second sentence; states the main idea of the paragraph clearly.
2.
Supporting Sentences: Three to five sentences that provide explanations, factual evidence, concrete examples, or sensory details that elaborate upon the topic sentence.
3.
Transitional Devices (Linkers): Words that ensure seamless coherence and logical flow between ideas (e.g., furthermore, in addition, consequently, for instance, on the other hand).
4.
Concluding Sentence: Ties the ideas together or transitions smoothly into the subsequent paragraph.
Model Paragraph Outline: "My First Impression of JSS 1"
Topic Sentence: Resuming at Federal Unity College marked the most memorable and exciting morning of my academic life.
Supporting Details: The massive multi-story buildings, the welcoming smile of the senior prefects, and the neat rows of laboratory equipment inspired me.
Concluding Sentence: Walking into my new classroom, I realized that secondary school had truly begun.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Introduction to Literature

Meaning of Literature:

Derived from the Latin word littera (meaning a letter of the alphabet), literature refers broadly to written works of high artistic quality, but in African and world culture it encompasses both Oral Literature (Orature) and Written Literature.

The Core Functions of Literature in Society:
1.
Entertainment and Pleasure: Engaging the reader's emotions and offering relaxation through compelling stories and verse.
2.
Moral Education and Guidance: Teaching societal values, distinguishing right from wrong, and demonstrating the consequences of greed, dishonesty, and arrogance.
3.
Cultural Preservation: Recording traditions, oral histories, customs, folk songs, and community proverbs across generations.
4.
Information and Social Mirroring: Exposing political corruption, injustice, and social problems so that society can reform itself.
The Two Primary Branches of Literature:
Oral Literature: Spoken from memory and passed by word of mouth (folktales, myths, praise poetry, proverbs, riddles).
Written Literature: Preserved permanently on paper or electronic media (novels, published stage plays, printed poetry).

Comparing Core First-Week Language and Literary Distinctions

Short /ɪ/ vs Long /iː/

Phonetic Duration and Articulatory Tension

/ɪ/ is short, relaxed, and articulated below close position (as in 'ship'); /iː/ is long, tense, and fully close with widely spread lips (as in 'sheep').

Proper Noun vs Common Noun

Specific Individual Identity vs General Category

Proper nouns name specific persons or places and always start with capital letters (e.g., 'Kaduna', 'Chidi'); common nouns name general classes without capitalization (e.g., 'city', 'boy').

Concrete Noun vs Abstract Noun

Physical Tangibility vs Conceptual Idea

Concrete nouns can be perceived with physical senses (e.g., 'desk', 'chalk'); abstract nouns denote intangible feelings, virtues, or states (e.g., 'pride', 'discipline').

Oral Literature vs Written Literature

Spoken Performance vs Scripted Preservation

Oral literature lives in spoken performance and collective memory (folktales, chants); written literature is preserved permanently through print or digital text (novels, poems).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the short vowel /ɪ/ and long vowel /iː/ identically, leading listeners to confuse "live" with "leave" or "fit" with "feet".

Scientific Fact

/iː/ requires deliberate vowel prolongation and greater muscular tension; failing to lengthen /iː/ completely alters word meanings.

Common Mistake

Writing proper nouns like days of the week, months, and town names in lowercase letters (e.g., writing "abuja" or "friday").

Scientific Fact

In English grammar, all proper nouns must begin with a capital letter regardless of where they appear in a sentence.

Common Mistake

Creating a paragraph with three different, unconnected ideas without a single unified topic sentence.

Scientific Fact

A paragraph must possess unity; every supporting sentence must directly explain and develop the central topic sentence.
