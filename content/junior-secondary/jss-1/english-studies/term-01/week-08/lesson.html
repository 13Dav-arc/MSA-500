Week 08: Plosives /t/ and /d/, Conjunctions, Argumentative Writing, and Figures of Speech

Foundational curriculum module in English Studies covering Plosives /t/ and /d/, Conjunctions, Argumentative Writing, and Figures of Speech for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When you sit in a noisy room and someone mentions a "tin", would you mistake it for a deafening "din"? That vital difference between a small metal container and an overwhelming noise rests entirely on whether your vocal cords vibrate—distinguishing voiceless /t/ from voiced /d/! In grammar, small conjunctions like because, although, and yet act as strong bridges connecting isolated thoughts into powerful, persuasive arguments. When you learn to defend your opinions in a spirited school debate and adorn your language with sparkling figures of speech like similes, metaphors, and personification, you transform ordinary speech into unforgettable, magnetic eloquence.

Key Terms

Alveolar Plosive A consonant stop sound produced by blocking the flow of air with the tip of the tongue pressed against the alveolar (tooth) ridge, followed by a sudden explosive release (such as /t/ and /d/).
Conjunction A grammatical connector word used to join words, phrases, clauses, or sentences together to express relationships like addition, contrast, cause, or condition.
Coordinating Conjunction A conjunction that connects two grammatically equal and independent clauses or words (memorized via the acronym FANBOYS: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*).
Argumentative Writing A form of composition or debate in which a writer takes a clear stance on a controversial proposition and defends it using reasoned arguments, factual evidence, and counter-argument refutations.
Figure of Speech A rhetorical or literary device that departs from the literal meaning of words to produce heightened emphasis, fresh imagery, or dramatic artistic effect.

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Alveolar Plosives /t/ and /d/

Both /t/ and /d/ are alveolar plosives produced by pressing the tip of the tongue against the alveolar ridge (the gum ridge just behind the upper front teeth), but they contrast in vocal cord vibration (voicing).

/t/ (Voiceless Alveolar Plosive):
Articulated with the tongue tip forming an airtight seal against the alveolar ridge. The soft palate is raised. The vocal cords do not vibrate (voiceless).
In initial stressed positions before a vowel, /t/ is pronounced with an energetic puff of air (aspiration), as in ten, time, tall.
Common spelling symbols: `t` (ten, top, water, cat), `tt` (bottle, butter), `ed` after voiceless consonants (laughed /lɑːft/, jumped /dʒʌmpt/, passed /pɑːst/).
(Note: `t` is silent in words like castle, listen, fasten, whistle, ballet, depot).
/d/ (Voiced Alveolar Plosive):
Articulated identically against the alveolar ridge, but the vocal cords vibrate strongly (voiced). It is never aspirated.
Common spelling symbols: `d` (day, door, under, bed), `dd` (ladder, puddle), `ed` after voiced sounds (played /pleɪd/, cleaned /kliːnd/).
(Note: `d` is silent in words like Wednesday, handkerchief, sandwich, handsome).

| Word Position | Voiceless Plosive /t/ | Voiced Plosive /d/ |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Initial | tin, ten, tear, tie, toad | din, den, dear, die, doad |

| Medial | latter, water, metal, writing | ladder, wader, medal, riding |

| Final | bet, heart, seat, cart, bat | bed, hard, seed, card, bad |

Aspect 2: Structure — Types of Conjunctions

Conjunctions establish logical relationships between ideas:

1.
Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS):
Connect words or independent clauses of equal grammatical rank:
For (explains cause: "He ran, for he was late").
And (adds information: "Chidi bought a pen and a notebook").
Nor (presents non-contrasting negative: "He cannot swim, nor does he want to learn").
But (expresses contrast: "The examination was tough, but Ngozi passed").
Or (presents alternatives: "You may read a book or write an essay").
Yet (shows surprising contrast: "The weather was hot, yet he wore a thick coat").
So (indicates result: "It rained heavily, so the match was postponed").
1.
Subordinating Conjunctions:
Connect a dependent (subordinate) clause to an independent main clause, indicating:
Cause / Reason: because, since, as ("We succeeded because we studied diligently").
Condition: if, unless, provided that ("You will not pass unless you revise").
Concession / Contrast: although, even though, while ("Although it was late, he continued reading").
Time: when, whenever, before, after, while, until ("Wash your hands before you eat").
1.
Correlative Conjunctions:
Pairs of conjunctions that work together in tandem:
Either ... or ("Either Tolu or Emeka will lead the assembly").
Neither ... nor ("He has neither the book nor the pencil").
Both ... and ("She is both brilliant and humble").
Not only ... but also ("He is not only athletic but also academically gifted").

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — "Scientific Inventions in Daily Life"

Reading Passage:

> The modern world is profoundly shaped by the relentless march of scientific ingenuity and technological innovation. From the very moment we awake to the musical chime of a digital alarm clock until we switch off an energy-efficient LED luminaire at bedtime, our daily existence is sustained by scientific devices. Inventions that appeared miraculous to previous generations—such as wireless telecommunications, automated refrigeration, and motorized transport—are today regarded as commonplace necessities across Nigerian households.

>

> Perhaps the most transformative breakthrough of our era is the advent of the personal computer and the internet. In educational citadels, pupils no longer depend exclusively upon physical libraries with dusty reference volumes. Instead, a student conducting research on solar energy or Nigerian civil history can access thousands of peer-reviewed articles, dynamic instructional diagrams, and digital encyclopedias in fractions of a second. Furthermore, digital smartphones have democratized information, enabling rural farmers to monitor real-time grain market prices and empowering telemedicine doctors to diagnose distant illnesses through encrypted video links.

>

> Nevertheless, scientific inventions constitute a double-edged sword that demands ethical stewardship and disciplined moderation. Excessive immersion in digital entertainment and unregulated social media platforms frequently undermines students' reading stamina, disrupts healthy sleep patterns, and exposes impressionable minds to online predators and cybercrime. Consequently, the ultimate measure of scientific progress does not lie in the sheer proliferation of gadgets, but in humanity's moral capacity to harness these technological marvels to enhance human welfare, eradicate poverty, and preserve the natural environment.

Aspect 4: Composition — Argumentative Writing Basics: "Day School vs Boarding School"

Argumentative writing is designed to persuade the reader or listener that a particular viewpoint on a controversial subject is valid through reasoned logic and factual evidence.

Essential Structural Anatomy of an Argumentative Essay / School Debate:
1.
Motion / Proposition: A clear declaration of the topic (e.g., "Boarding schools provide a more disciplined foundation for secondary students than day schools").
2.
Protocols / Vocatives (Debate Setting): Acknowledging dignitaries politely ("Mr. Chairman, Panel of Judges, Accurate Timekeeper, Distinguished Audience, and my Co-Debaters, good day").
3.
Declaration of Stance: Stating clearly whether you support (propose) or oppose the motion.
4.
Body Paragraphs (Three Strong Points): Presenting well-reasoned arguments supported by illustrations and real-world facts:
Point 1: Independent life skills and self-reliance acquired by managing personal time and laundry without parental spoon-feeding.
Point 2: Structured prep hours and uninterrupted study routines supervised by dedicated housemasters.
Point 3: Rich inter-ethnic camaraderie and bonding with peers from diverse regions of Nigeria.
1.
Rebuttal (Refuting Opposing Viewpoint): Anticipating the opponent's claim (e.g., "My worthy opponent argued that boarding schools cause homesickness; however, homesickness is merely a temporary phase that builds emotional resilience").
2.
Conclusion: Re-affirming your position strongly with a memorable summary statement.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Figures of Speech: Simile, Metaphor, Personification, Hyperbole

Figures of speech are artistic linguistic devices used by poets and prose writers to convey fresh, imaginative meaning beyond literal interpretations.

1.
Simile:
An explicit, direct comparison between two fundamentally dissimilar entities using the comparative words "like" or "as".
Examples:
"The brave soldier fought like a lion in the battlefield."
"Her voice was as sweet as honey."
1.
Metaphor:
An implicit, direct identification or comparison between two different entities without using "like" or "as", stating that one thing is another.
Examples:
"The disciplined student is a beacon of light in our school."
"Time is a thief that steals our youth."
1.
Personification:
Giving human attributes, emotions, speech, or actions to inanimate objects, animals, or abstract concepts.
Examples:
"The hungry wind howled and screamed through the window shutters."
"Opportunity knocked softly at his door."
1.
Hyperbole:
A deliberate, gross exaggeration used for rhetorical emphasis, humor, or heightened dramatic effect, not intended to be taken literally.
Examples:
"I have told you a million times to close the gate!"
"The headpan of yam was so heavy that it broke my neck into pieces."

Comparing Key Sound, Grammar, and Figurative Distinctions

Plosive /t/ vs Plosive /d/

Voicing State and Aspiration

/t/ is voiceless with zero vocal cord vibration and is aspirated in initial stressed positions (as in 'tin'); /d/ is voiced with vocal cord vibration and is never aspirated (as in 'din').

Coordinating vs Subordinating Conjunction

Clause Rank and Grammatical Equality

Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) link clauses of equal grammatical rank; subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses subordinate to a main clause.

Simile vs Metaphor

Explicit vs Implicit Comparison

A simile explicitly compares using 'like' or 'as' (e.g., 'he is as brave as a lion'); a metaphor makes a direct symbolic identification without 'like' or 'as' (e.g., 'he is a lion').

Argumentative vs Narrative Composition

Persuasive Defense vs Storytelling Recount

Argumentative writing defends a stance using logic, facts, and rebuttals; narrative writing recounts a temporal sequence of events.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the alveolar plosives /t/ and /d/ with the tongue between the teeth (like dental fricatives /θ/ and /ð/).

Scientific Fact

Articulate /t/ and /d/ by firmly pressing the tongue tip against the alveolar ridge above the upper teeth, not against the teeth themselves.

Common Mistake

Using a comma splice to connect two independent sentences without a coordinating conjunction (e.g., writing *"She revised hard, she scored an A"*).

Scientific Fact

Independent clauses must be linked with a conjunction (e.g., *"She revised hard, **and** she scored an A"*) or separated with a full stop or semicolon.

Common Mistake

Confusing a simile with a metaphor by claiming that *"He fought like a tiger"* is a metaphor.

Scientific Fact

The presence of *"like"* or *"as"* instantly classifies the figure of speech as a simile, not a metaphor.
