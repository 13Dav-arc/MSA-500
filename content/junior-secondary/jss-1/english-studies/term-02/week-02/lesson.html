Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /s/ and /z/

The consonants /s/ and /z/ are alveolar fricatives. They are articulated in almost the exact same way: the blade of the tongue touches the alveolar (upper tooth) ridge firmly for /s/ and gently for /z/.

/s/ is a voiceless alveolar fricative because the vocal cords do not vibrate during its articulation.
Common spelling symbols for /s/: `s` (sip, seal, bus), `c` (cease, price, ice), and `sc` (scene).
/z/ is a voiced alveolar fricative because the airstream causes the vocal cords to vibrate during its articulation.
Common spelling symbols for /z/: `z` (zip, zoo, zeal, buzz, prize), `s` (cause, houses, lies, eyes), and `se` (size).

Aspect 2: Structure — Tenses: Present Tense Forms

Tenses express the time of an action in a sentence, where the verb plays the primary functional role. There are three major forms of the present tense:

1.
Present Simple Tense:
Used for habitual, consistent actions that happen every time: He comes here often; She dances everyday.
Used for universal truths or permanent conditions: I am a teacher; She is my wife; We are gentle people.
Used for live sports commentaries reporting actions as they unfold: Okocha passes the ball; He heads the ball.
Rule: Singular subjects take singular verbs (He runs), while plural subjects (and pronouns I and you) take base plural verbs (They run; I sing).
1.
Present Continuous Tense:
Used for actions taking place right at the time of speaking.
Formed using the auxiliary verb am/is/are + present participle (-ing verb): Mrs Adewole is teaching us English Language; I am eating my dinner; We are going to church.
1.
Present Perfect Tense:
Used for actions that have just been completed in the recent past: He has gone; She has just arrived; They have completed the building.
Used for an action that began in the past and continues into the present: I have lived here for ten years; She has taught English for five years.
Concord Rule: Use have with I, we, you, and they; use has with he, she, and it.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — Avoiding Accidents on Our Roads

Reading Passage:

> Road traffic accidents have been responsible for the tragic loss of many precious human lives and the wanton destruction of valuable properties across Nigeria. A day hardly passes on our federal highways and city streets without a distressing report of motor vehicles colliding, pedestrians being knocked down, or commercial buses veering into deep roadside ditches. Many families have been thrown into sudden mourning, and promising breadwinners have been incapacitated due to avoidable road crashes.

>

> The primary causes of these frequent vehicular disasters are not spiritual forces, but purely human errors and widespread negligence. Over-speeding stands out as the chief culprit, as reckless drivers treat public highways as racing tracks. Many motorists drive under the toxic influence of alcohol, while others operate unroadworthy vehicles fitted with worn-out tyres, defective headlamps, and failing brakes. Distracted driving, particularly making calls or browsing social media while steering a moving vehicle, has also contributed immensely to catastrophic road carnages.

>

> To curb this alarming menace, all road users must adopt a strict culture of discipline and safety consciousness. Motorists must observe statutory speed limits, obey traffic signals, and undergo mandatory periodic vehicular maintenance before embarking on any journey. Pedestrians must use designated zebra crossings and pedestrian overhead bridges rather than dashing across expressways. Furthermore, regulatory agencies like the Federal Road Safety Corps (FRSC) must enforce strict highway penalties without compromise. If every citizen obeys traffic rules, road traffic accidents will be drastically reduced, and human lives and properties will remain safe.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — More on Descriptive Composition

A descriptive composition gives a clear mental picture of a person, place, or object. It requires the writer to describe things with such sensory accuracy that the reader can visualize, hear, and feel what is being described. Examples include: "My School Compound", "My Favourite Teacher", and "My Favourite Game".

Content: Provide a detailed description of features, proportions, colors, and behavior, noting the significance or qualities you admire.
Organisation:
Introduction: Introduce the scene, object, or person, giving general outward appearance and location.
Body Paragraphs: Move systematically (e.g., from head to toe for a person, or from entrance gate to inner classrooms for a compound). Do not itemize with bullet points; write in cohesive prose paragraphs.
Conclusion: Provide a brief summary, general impression, proverb, or personal remark that leaves an impact on the reader.
Expression: Use precise adjectives (e.g., towering, tranquil, sparkling) and sensory verbs that evoke sights, sounds, and textures.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Prose: Core Literary Terms

Prose refers to non-poetic writing that follows conventional sentences and paragraphs. Key literary terms in prose include:

Plot: The organized sequence of events that forms the story of a novel or play.
Conflict: The struggle between opposing forces that drives the plot forward; it may be external (a character against nature, society, or another person) or internal (two competing desires within a character).
Climax: The turning point and emotional peak of the plot where the tension reaches its highest level and action begins falling.
Dénouement: The resolution or conclusion of the plot where loose ends are tied up and the outcome is settled.
Protagonist vs Antagonist: The protagonist is the central main character, while the antagonist is the character or force that opposes the protagonist.
Character Types:
Flat Character: Simple, two-dimensional character with few traits and predictable behavior.
Round Character: Complex, dynamic, multi-faceted character capable of surprising the reader.
Static Character: Remains unchanged throughout the story; a dynamic character undergoes significant growth or transformation.
Setting: The specific physical place and historical time period in which the events occur.
Theme: The central idea, moral lesson, or underlying message explored throughout the work.
Narrative Techniques: Suspense (deliberately withholding information to build anticipation), Flashback (shifting back in time to reveal earlier occurrences), and Foreshadowing (giving subtle hints about future happenings).

Comparing Core Linguistic and Literary Contrasts

Phonetic Voicing

Consonant /s/ vs Consonant /z/

Both are alveolar fricatives produced with the blade of the tongue at the teeth ridge, but /s/ is voiceless with no vocal cord vibration (*sip*, *price*), whereas /z/ is voiced with clear vocal cord vibration (*zip*, *prize*)[cite: 107].

Temporal Continuity

Present Simple vs Present Continuous

Present simple expresses habitual routines and permanent truths (*She dances everyday*), while present continuous expresses actions currently in progress at the moment of speaking (*She is dancing now*)[cite: 107].

Character Complexity

Flat Character vs Round Character

A flat character has few traits, is predictable, and undergoes no change, whereas a round character is multifaceted, realistic, and undergoes growth throughout the narrative[cite: 107].

Compositional Modality

Narrative vs Descriptive Essay

Narrative essays focus on the chronological passage of time and action (*what happened first, next, and last*), while descriptive essays focus on spatial arrangement and sensory imagery (*how something looks, sounds, and feels*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the plural noun marker `-es` or final `s` as voiceless /s/ in words where it should be voiced /z/[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

In words like *houses*, *lies*, *eyes*, and *buzz*, the final sound is the voiced fricative /z/, not voiceless /s/[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Using the pronoun *have* with third-person singular subjects (e.g., saying *"He have gone"* or *"She have arrived"*)[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Singular third-person pronouns (*he*, *she*, *it*) take **has**, while *I*, *we*, *you*, and *they* take **have**[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Confusing the climax of a prose plot with its dénouement[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

The climax is the highest point of dramatic tension, whereas the dénouement is the subsequent resolution and untying of the plot's conflicts[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Listing features in numbered bullet points within a descriptive essay.

Scientific Fact

Descriptive essays must be written in continuous, well-developed prose paragraphs rather than bulleted lists[cite: 107].
