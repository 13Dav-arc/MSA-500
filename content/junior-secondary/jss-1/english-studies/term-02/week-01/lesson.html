Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /f/ and /v/

Consonants /f/ and /v/ are labio-dental fricative sounds articulated by bringing the front upper teeth into partial contact with the lower lip, creating a continuous frictional noise as the airstream escapes:

/f/ is a voiceless consonant sound because the glottis is wide open and the vocal cords do not vibrate during production.
Common spelling symbols for /f/: `f` (feet, fun, float), `ph` (Physics, phase), `gh` (laugh, cough), and `ff` (staff).
/v/ is a voiced consonant sound because the vocal cords vibrate noticeably as the airstream escapes between the lower lip and upper front teeth.
Common spelling symbol for /v/: `v` (vane, vast, voice, weave, starve), with the notable exception of `ph` in Stephen.

| Position | Voiceless /f/ | Voiced /v/ |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Initial | fine, fast, fan, few, fry, flow | vine, vast, van, view, verse, veal |

| Medial | wafer, refuse, effort, defend | waiver, lever, every, divide |

| Final | safe, staff, serf, belief, loaf, grief | save, starve, serve, believe, naive, weave |

Aspect 2: Structure — Adverbs: Forms and Functions

The primary function of an adverb is to give more information about (modify) a verb by limiting or describing the action. Adverbs can also modify other adverbs (extremely suddenly), adjectives (gravely ill), prepositions (just after dinner), and conjunctions (exactly what he said). When a group of words performs the function of an adverb, it is called an adverbial phrase or adverbial.

Types of Adverbs:

1.
Adverbs of Manner: Indicate how or the circumstances in which something happens, frequently formed by adding -ly to an adjective (e.g., She was neatly dressed; They spoke eloquently; We waited patiently).
2.
Adverbs of Time: Indicate when something happens (e.g., I'll see you soon; It was her birthday today; Afterwards, we had afternoon tea).
3.
Adverbs of Frequency: Indicate how frequently something occurs (e.g., It always snowed; We rarely meet; He never drinks alcohol; We play tennis regularly).
4.
Adverbs of Duration: Indicate how long something lasts (e.g., She is living with her grandmother temporarily; He has been suspended indefinitely; They stopped briefly).
5.
Adverbs of Place: Indicate where something takes place (e.g., He has gone overseas; They live nearby; She went inside; We waded ashore).
6.
Adverbs of Degree & Emphasis: Indicate the extent or intensity of an action (e.g., I enjoyed the film immensely; She was slightly hurt; We totally disagree; I was utterly devastated).
7.
Interrogative Adverbs: Wh- words used to ask questions (e.g., When did you last see him? Where was the money hidden? How are you feeling? Why was he there?).

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — "Hard Work Has a Reward"

Reading Passage:

> After the New Year celebrations, the second term began and ended in the middle of April when the school, Kaduna Comprehensive High School, closed for the second term holiday. The second term holiday was very brief, and before anyone realized it, it was already time to resume for another session of serious academic work. While many students treated the short vacation as an excuse to abandon their books and roam the streets idly, a disciplined Junior Secondary 1 student named Tunde drew up a personal study timetable on the very first day of the break.

>

> Every morning during the holiday, Tunde woke up at dawn to assist his parents with domestic chores and help his mother arrange her goods at the local market. By afternoon, when the house was quiet, he retreated to his reading table to review his first and second term notebooks, practicing Mathematics problems and summarizing his English Literature texts. Whenever he encountered a difficult topic, he consulted his elder sister rather than giving up in frustration. His friends often mocked his dedication, calling him a "holiday bookworm," but Tunde remained focused on his academic goals.

>

> When the third term commenced and the promotion examinations were eventually conducted, the fruits of Tunde's perseverance became undeniable to the entire school community. While the students who had wasted their holidays struggled anxiously in the examination hall, Tunde answered his papers with calm confidence. On Speech and Prize-Giving Day, the Principal of Kaduna Comprehensive High School called Tunde to the podium to receive the Overall Academic Excellence Award, proving beyond doubt that hard work truly has a rich reward.

Aspect 4: Composition — More on Narrative Composition (Paragraph Development)

A narrative composition is an essay in which the writer tells a story or gives an orderly account of events that have happened. Whether the story is true or imagined, it must be plausible (believable and true to life).

Verb Tense Rule: The primary tense used in narrative writing is the simple past tense, supported occasionally by the past continuous or past perfect tense, with careful attention to tense sequence in reported speech.
Four Pillars of a Good Narrative Essay:
1.
Content: Present a detailed, engaging sequence of events addressing what happened, who was involved, when and where it occurred, how it unfolded, and the consequences.
2.
Organization: Begin with an introductory paragraph establishing the time, place, scene, and key characters; develop the episode chronologically across well-linked body paragraphs; and end with a brief, memorable conclusion.
3.
Expression: Choose vivid, appropriate vocabulary and adverbs that clearly convey the action to the reader.
4.
Conclusion: Round off the essay in line with the topic using a reflection on what was gained, a proverb, or how you felt about the outcome.
Common Narrative Topics: My First Day at School; A Day I Will Never Forget in My Life; My First Journey to Lagos; How I Spent My Last Holiday.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Review on the Genres of Literature

The genres of literature are the major varieties or categories of literary composition: Prose, Drama, and Poetry:

1.
Prose: A free-flowing, extended narrative written in ordinary grammatical structure (sentences, paragraphs, and chapters). It is divided into fiction (novels, short stories) and non-fiction (biographies, essays). Someone who writes a prose work or novel is called a novelist or author (e.g., The Costly Mistake by Chinelo Ifezulike).
2.
Drama: A story written in dialogue form to be performed on stage by actors before an audience, projecting human conflict through speech and action. Its major sub-genres include tragedy and comedy (such as farce, satire, and comedy of manners). Someone who writes a drama is called a playwright (e.g., Vengeance of the Spirit by Maxwell Onyeka).
3.
Poetry: An imaginative rendition that gives powerful expression to experiences, ideas, and feelings in condensed, rhythmic, and metaphoric language written in verse (lines and stanzas). Sub-genres include epic, lyric, sonnet, and ode (e.g., the poem "Nightfall" by Oiwona Andrew).

Comparing Core Linguistic and Literary Contrasts

Phonetic Voicing

Consonant /f/ vs Consonant /v/

Both are labio-dental fricatives produced with the upper teeth and lower lip, but /f/ is voiceless (no vocal cord vibration, as in *safe* and *fan*) whereas /v/ is voiced (vocal cords vibrate, as in *save* and *van*)[cite: 107].

Temporal vs Spatial Modification

Adverbs of Duration vs Adverbs of Frequency

Adverbs of duration indicate how long an action continues (*temporarily*, *indefinitely*, *overnight*), whereas adverbs of frequency indicate how often an action repeats (*always*, *regularly*, *seldom*)[cite: 107].

Literary Presentation

Prose vs Drama vs Poetry

Prose flows in continuous paragraphs and chapters (*The Costly Mistake*), Drama unfolds through stage dialogue and action (*Vengeance of the Spirit*), and Poetry uses condensed verse and metaphoric rhythm (*"Nightfall"*)[cite: 107].

Prose Sub-Genres

Fiction vs Non-Fiction

Fiction consists of imaginative narratives invented by a novelist (such as novels and short stories), while non-fiction recounts factual real-life people and events (such as biographies and essays)[cite: 107].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Confusing the spelling and pronunciation of final /f/ and /v/ in noun-verb pairs like *belief/believe*, *safe/save*, and *grief/grieve*[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Nouns like *belief* and *grief* end with the voiceless sound /f/, whereas verbs like *believe* and *save* end with the voiced sound /v/[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Assuming that every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb of manner[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

While many adverbs of manner end in *-ly* (*neatly*, *quickly*), several *-ly* words are adverbs of time (*lately*), frequency (*regularly*, *rarely*), duration (*temporarily*, *briefly*), or degree (*immensely*, *partly*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Writing a narrative composition in the simple present tense or switching tenses randomly mid-paragraph[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Narrative essays recount events that have already happened and must be anchored consistently in the past tense[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Referring to the author of a stage play as a "novelist" or spelling playwright as "playwrite"[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

A novelist writes prose novels, whereas a writer of drama is a **playwright**[cite: 107].
