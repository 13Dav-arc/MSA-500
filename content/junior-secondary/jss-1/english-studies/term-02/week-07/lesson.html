Why This Matters to You

When you speak, write, or act, your mastery of vowel glides and descriptive timing shapes how your audience feels and reacts. Gliding smoothly between vowels allows you to distinguish between late and let, or cheer and chair, preventing embarrassing miscommunications. Meanwhile, combining robust verb tenses with expressive adverbials gives rhythm to your sentences, whether you are analyzing the electric mood of a crowded football stadium, delivering a persuasive debate speech, or untangling the dramatic conflicts of Maxwell Onyeka’s Vengeance of the Spirit.

Key Terms

Diphthong An independent vowel sound produced by a continuous, smooth glide of the tongue from one vowel position to another within the same syllable[cite: 107].
Adverbial Any word, phrase, or clause that functions as an adverb by modifying a verb, an adjective, or another adverb, indicating time, manner, place, condition, or purpose[cite: 107].
Author's Mood The emotional atmosphere, feeling, or state of mind that a writer intentionally creates for the reader through vivid descriptions, word choices, and sentence rhythms[cite: 107].
Counter-Argument An opposing viewpoint or objection raised by an opponent in an argumentative essay, which the writer acknowledges and systematically refutes with evidence[cite: 107].
Theme The central idea, underlying message, or core philosophical concern explored by a playwright or author throughout a literary text[cite: 107].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Introduction to Diphthongs (/eɪ/, /əʊ/, /ɪə/, /eə/)

A diphthong is a double vowel sound articulated as the tongue glides smoothly from an initial vowel quality toward a second vowel quality within one syllable.

/eɪ/: A closing diphthong where the glide begins at /e/ and moves toward /ɪ/ with spread lips. Common spelling symbols include:
`a` (face, mate, lame), `ai` (rain, main, gain), `ay` (pay, way, ray), `eigh` (eight), `ey` (they), and `ea` (break).
Learner Pitfall: Avoid pronouncing /eɪ/ as the short monophthong /e/ (e.g., confusing late with let, or gate with get).
/əʊ/: A closing diphthong where the glide begins at the central position /ə/ and moves toward the back rounded vowel /ʊ/ with lips becoming progressively rounded. Common spelling symbols include:
`o` (close, no, smoke), `oe` (hoe, roe), `oa` (moan, boast, oath), `ou` (soul, mould, boulder), `ow` (grow, flow, bestow), and `ol` (cold, gold, told).
/ɪə/: A centring diphthong that glides from the close-mid front /ɪ/ toward the central vowel /ə/. Word examples: steer, tear, hear, cheer, mere, beard, spear, ear.
/eə/: A centring diphthong that glides from the front open-mid /e/ toward the central vowel /ə/. Word examples: chair, their, there, pear, heir, air, share, care.

Aspect 2: Structure — Tenses and Adverbials (Continued)

Tenses specify the chronological occurrence of an action through changes in the verb, while adverbials add contextual precision regarding time, manner, place, condition, and purpose:

Present Tenses & Adverbials: Express routine habits, current states, or ongoing commentary.
Habitual present with adverb of frequency: Paul and Silas pray everyday.
Habitual present with time adverbial: We do our assignments on weekends.
Past Tenses & Adverbials: Detail finished events and past states of affairs.
Simple past with time adverbial: I travelled to London last week.
Past action with habitual time phrase: Akin washed his clothes on Fridays.
Future Tenses & Adverbials: Express prospective plans using shall (first person I, we) and will (second/third person you, he, she, they).
Simple future with time adverbial: I shall travel next year; We shall meet next week.
Future intention with condition: His father will pay his school fees if he passes.
Future action with purpose: He will wake up early in order to prepare for the test.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — At the Entrance to the Stadium

Reading Passage:

> A few minutes before the anticipated football encounter between Enyimba Football Club and Yankari Football Club was scheduled to begin, the massive iron gates to the Jokolo Stadium were abruptly shut. The colossal arena had already absorbed thousands of passionate supporters, packing the concrete terraces far beyond safe capacity. Outside, a desperate, surging sea of ticket-holders clamoured fiercely against the perimeter turnstiles, chanting battle songs and pleading with stern riot policemen to grant them entry. The charged atmosphere hung heavy with deafening noise, rising dust, and palpable tension as latecomers realized they were marooned outside the sporting battlefield.

>

> Inside the stadium bowl, the mood escalated from suspenseful excitement into sheer hysteria as both teams strode onto the emerald pitch. The referee’s piercing whistle ignited ninety minutes of fierce, breathtaking physical contests. The spectators cheered every dazzling tackle and groaned loudly at near misses, their collective heartbeats fluctuating with every swift counter-attack. The match was so evenly matched that neither defence yielded an inch, pushing players to the outer limits of their physical stamina.

>

> When regulation time elapsed without a decisive breakthrough, the exhausted combatants were granted extra time to break the deadlock. Despite frantic shots, acrobatic saves, and desperate tactical substitutions in the final minutes, the monumental football battle ended goalless. As the final whistle blew, a stunned, weary silence descended upon the vast crowd before both factions of supporters rose in unison to applaud twenty-two gallant athletes who had defended their club flags with unflinching bravery.

Target Vocabulary in Context:

Encounter (Noun): A sporting contest, match, or unexpected confrontation between opposing sides.
Terraces (Noun): Tiered concrete platforms in a sports stadium where spectators stand or sit.
Clamoured (Verb): Shouted loudly, persistently, and confusedly to make a demand or express protest.
Palpable (Adjective): So intense or obvious as to be almost felt physically; noticeable.
Marooned (Verb/Adjective): Left abandoned, stranded, or isolated without the means to escape.
Hysteria (Noun): An overwhelming, uncontrollable state of excitement, panic, or wild emotion.
Goalless (Adjective): Describing a sporting contest in which neither opposing team scores a goal ($0–0$).

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Argumentative Essay (Guided Composition)

An argumentative essay requires the candidate to present a controversial issue that has two distinct sides, arguing persuasively to convince the reader to agree with the writer's stance.

Core Requirements for Effective Argumentative Writing:
1.
Clear Understanding: Possess a thorough grasp of the topic or proposition.
2.
Jotted Points: Brainstorm and jot down at least four solid supporting points with clear evidence.
3.
Orderly Arrangement: Structure points logically from strongest to weakest, refuting opposing claims.
4.
Clarity of Expression: Use concise, persuasive language without rambling emotional abuse.
5.
Mechanical Accuracy: Ensure the essay is completely free of grammatical, concord, and spelling errors.
Guided Debate Topic: "Male Children Are More Beneficial to Their Parents Than Female Children" (or the reverse motion).
Step-by-Step Writing Blueprint:
Title: Boldly declared at the top (e.g., WHY FEMALE CHILDREN ARE EQUALLY OR MORE BENEFICIAL TO PARENTS THAN MALE CHILDREN).
Vocatives / Opening: Address the Chairman, panel of judges, timekeeper, co-debaters, and audience.
Body Paragraph 1 (Affirmation): Define key terms and state your stance unambiguously.
Body Paragraphs 2 & 3 (Evidence): Present compelling examples of women excelling in leadership, caregiving, education, and elder-care.
Body Paragraph 4 (Rebuttal): Refute the counter-argument that only male children preserve the family name, explaining modern legal and socio-economic realities.
Conclusion: Reiterate your central stance and deliver an impactful final appeal.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Drama: Themes in Maxwell Onyeka's *Vengeance of the Spirit*

In drama, a theme is the central subject, core idea, or moral inquiry explored through character actions and dialogue. In Maxwell Onyeka's recommended play Vengeance of the Spirit, several major themes intertwine:

1.
Theme of Affection and Love: Explores romantic loyalty and emotional attachment between young lovers, demonstrating how genuine affection often conflicts with rigid parental expectations.
2.
Theme of Wrong Decisions and Greed: Portrays how parents who make materialistic marital choices for their children create catastrophe and generational heartbreak.
3.
Theme of Retribution and Supernatural Vengeance: Emphasizes that unjust human actions, breaches of sacred oaths, and moral defiance inevitably awaken ancestral wrath and spiritual justice.
4.
Theme of Cultural Identity and Traditional Rites: Highlights traditional African marriage customs, bride prices, respect for elders, and the sacred authority of deities in regulating community morality.

Comparing Linguistic and Literary Contrasts

Vowel Motion

Closing Diphthongs vs Centring Diphthongs

Closing diphthongs (/eɪ/, /əʊ/) glide toward close vowels (/ɪ/, /ʊ/) with narrowing jaw movements, while centring diphthongs (/ɪə/, /eə/) glide toward the central vowel /ə/[cite: 107].

Sound Contrast

Diphthong /ɪə/ vs Diphthong /eə/

/ɪə/ starts with the close front vowel /ɪ/ as in *hear* and *cheer*, whereas /eə/ starts with the open-mid vowel /e/ as in *hair* and *chair*[cite: 107].

Expressive Intent

Persuasive Argument vs Dramatic Dialogue

Argumentative essays present logical points and refutations to persuade a reader[cite: 107], while dramatic dialogue portrays human conflict through spoken character exchanges on stage[cite: 107].

Literary Focus

Theme vs Plot in Drama

The plot is the sequential chain of events and actions that happen on stage, whereas the theme is the deeper philosophical or moral idea revealed by those events[cite: 107].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Replacing the diphthong /eɪ/ with the short vowel /e/ (e.g., pronouncing *late* as *let*, or *wait* as *wet*)[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

The sound /eɪ/ is a glide from /e/ toward /ɪ/ and requires longer articulation with spread lips[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the centring diphthongs /ɪə/ and /eə/ identically (e.g., confusing *steer* with *stare*, or *hear* with *hair*)[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

/ɪə/ starts with the tongue raised close to /ɪ/ (*hear*), while /eə/ begins lower at /e/ (*hair*) before gliding to /ə/[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Resorting to abusive insults against opponents in an argumentative essay or debate[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Argumentative writing demands objective evidence, logical deduction, and courteous persuasion rather than emotional hostility[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Retelling the plot summary when asked to analyze a theme in a drama text[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Analyzing a theme requires explaining the underlying moral, social, or philosophical message of the play, not just recounting the story[cite: 107].
