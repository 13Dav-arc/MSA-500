Key Terms

Closing Diphthong A double vowel sound produced when the tongue glides smoothly from an open or central vowel position toward a closer vowel position like /ɪ/ or /ʊ/[cite: 107].
Determiner A word placed before a noun or noun phrase to specify, identify, or quantify it without strictly serving as a descriptive adjective[cite: 107].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Diphthongs /aɪ/, /aʊ/, /ɔɪ/, and /ʊə/

Diphthongs are dynamic vowel glides produced as the tongue shifts smoothly from one articulatory posture to another within the same syllable:

/aɪ/: A closing diphthong gliding from the open vowel /a/ toward the close-mid front vowel /ɪ/.
Common spelling symbols: `i` (write, climb, bite), `y` (by, fry), `ie` (lie, tie, cries), `igh` (right, flight), `uy` (buy, guy), `ui` (guide, guile), `ei`/`ey` (either, height, eye), and `ai` (aisle).
/aʊ/: A closing diphthong starting between the central and back region and gliding toward /ʊ/, while the lips change from neutral to rounded.
Common spelling symbols: `ow` (cow, clown, crown, bow, owl), `ou` (round, bounce, voucher, doubt, devout), and `ough` (drought, plough, bough).
/ɔɪ/: A closing diphthong that glides from the back open-mid rounded vowel /ɔ/ toward /ɪ/.
Common spelling symbols: `oi` (avoid, boil, voice, joint, anoint), `oy` (boy, cloy, alloy), and `uoy` (buoy).
/ʊə/: A centring diphthong that glides from the close-mid rounded vowel /ʊ/ toward the central unrounded vowel /ə/.
Common spelling symbols: `oor` (poor, moor, boor), `our` (tourist, dour), `ure` (sure, cure, pure, lure), `ua` (continual), and `eur` (liqueur).

Aspect 2: Structure — Determiners: Types and Functions

A determiner is a word placed before a noun or pronoun to identify, point out, or quantify it. Unlike adjectives, determiners do not describe physical attributes like color or texture; they define grammatical reference.

Major Classes of Determiners:

1.
Articles:
Definite Article ("the"): Identifies a specific person or object already mentioned, unique entities (the Bible, the Prime Minister), or an entire class (the big cats).
Indefinite Article ("a" / "an"): Refers to non-specific singular countable nouns or means "one".
Use a before words beginning with consonant sounds (e.g., a box, a road, a unit because unit begins with the consonant sound /j/).
Use an before words beginning with vowel sounds (e.g., an apple, an hour, an honest man, an heir where the letter h is silent).
Zero Article: The deliberate absence of an article in general expressions (e.g., We went by train; He is in hospital; She is at work).
1.
Demonstrative Determiners: Point out specific nouns in space or time: this, that, these, those (this cake, those flowers, that house, these students).
2.
Possessive Determiners: Show ownership or connection: my, our, your, his, her, its, their (my book, his car, their new bikes, its leg).
3.
Indefinite (General) Determiners: Quantify nouns in an indefinite manner without naming exact amounts: all, any, both, each, either, every, few, little, less, many, much, more, neither, no, several, some, enough.
4.
Number Determiners: Express quantities or positions:
Cardinal Numbers: Express exact counting (one, two, seven, sixty, five hundred).
Ordinal Numbers: Express sequence or ranking (first, second, third, twenty-fifth).

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — Reading to Interpret Diagrams, Maps, and Sketches

Reading Passage:

> Reading comprehension extends beyond deciphering conventional printed sentences; it encompasses the critical ability to interpret graphical illustrations, directional maps, engineering sketches, and structural diagrams. In our increasingly technical world, navigating through a bustling metropolitan transport interchange, understanding the floor plan of an architectural complex, or assembling a piece of mechanical laboratory apparatus relies entirely on visual literacy. Diagrams condense vast volumes of intricate geographic and operational data into clear, spatial representations that can be grasped at a glance.

>

> When examining a geographic map or architectural sketch, a skilled reader must first consult the legend or key. The key interprets the specialized cartographic symbols: a cluster of wavy blue lines signifies a river, a bold dashed line marks an administrative boundary, while triangular icons often represent towering mountain peaks. Furthermore, paying close attention to the scale of the map enables the reader to calculate real-world physical distances accurately, while the cardinal compass rose indicates true orientation. Misreading these graphical signposts can cause travelers to lose their bearings entirely.

>

> In secondary school and technical training, interpreting flowchart diagrams and scientific process sketches is equally indispensable. A well-designed scientific flowchart illustrates how raw inputs undergo sequential transformations before yielding a finished product. Directional arrows indicate the continuous flow of energy or materials, while rectangular blocks outline specific operational phases. By learning to synthesize visual data alongside written prose, a student develops the analytical precision required to solve complex real-world problems efficiently.

Guided Comprehension Questions & Model Responses:

1.
Question: Why does reading comprehension include the interpretation of diagrams, maps, and sketches?
Model Answer: Comprehension includes visual materials because modern life requires the ability to navigate physical environments, understand architectural plans, and assemble equipment using visual literacy.
1.
Question: What is the vital function of a legend or key on a geographic map?
Model Answer: The legend or key explains the meanings of specialized symbols, colors, and markings used across the map.
1.
Question: What two map features enable a reader to calculate physical distances and determine direction?
Model Answer: The scale of the map allows the calculation of real-world distances, while the compass rose indicates directional orientation.
1.
Question: In a scientific flowchart, what do directional arrows and rectangular blocks represent?
Model Answer: Directional arrows represent the movement or flow of materials and energy, while rectangular blocks denote specific operational phases or steps.
1.

Target Vocabulary in Context:

Deciphering (Verb): Interpreting, decoding, or making sense of written text, symbols, or obscure markings.
Cartographic (Adjective): Relating to the scientific art, practice, or technique of drawing and interpreting maps.
Legend (Noun): An explanatory table or list of symbols and keys accompanying an illustration, map, or chart.
Orientation (Noun): The positioning or alignment of a map or object in relation to cardinal points of the compass.
Synthesize (Verb): To combine diverse ideas, data, and visual information into a coherent, unified whole.
Indispensable (Adjective): Absolutely essential, necessary, and impossible to manage without.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay (Explaining a Process)

An expository essay explains a process, gives instructions, or clarifies how something works or operates. While it shares descriptive features, its main purpose is to inform, instruct, and explain mechanisms or recipes systematically.

Core Features of Expository Writing:
Written primarily in the simple present tense (get, boil, mix, serve).
Demands high clarity, precision, and sequential logic.
Uses transitional time connectives (first, subsequently, next, meanwhile, finally).
Common topics: How to Prepare My Favourite Meal, How Bean Balls Are Produced from Beans, A Traditional Marriage Ceremony, The Way to the Staff Quarters from the School Gate.
Standard Structural Blueprint:
1.
Title: Stated clearly in bold or capital letters (e.g., HOW TO PREPARE WHITE RICE AND TOMATO STEW).
2.
Introduction: Introduce the subject, define its significance, and name the requirements (e.g., stating why white rice and stew is a nutritious, popular dish).
3.
Body Paragraphs (Step-by-Step Explanation):
Step One: Assembly and preparation of utensils and raw ingredients (rice, fresh tomatoes, peppers, onions, cooking oil, seasonings).
Step Two: Parboiling and washing the rice to reduce starch, followed by boiling until tender.
Step Three: Blending, boiling, and frying the tomato and pepper base with seasoned meat or fish.
Step Four: Simmering the stew with appropriate spices until the oil rises cleanly to the top.
1.
Conclusion: Summarize how the meal should be served (hot, garnished with vegetables) and state a concluding remark on its dietary value.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Figures of Speech: Simile and Metaphor

Figures of speech are imaginative linguistic devices used by poets, playwrights, and novelists to create vivid mental images, elevate emotional tone, and deepen meaning:

Simile: A figure of speech that compares two fundamentally different things that share a notable characteristic, using explicit connective words such as "like" or "as".
Alaba's shirt is as white as snow.
He swims like a fish.
On our entering the premises, it was as quiet as a graveyard.
The old warrior fights like a lion.
Metaphor: A direct comparison between two distinct things without the use of "like" or "as", asserting that one thing is another in order to transfer qualities between them.
Ade was a lion in the fight (he possessed the fierce courage of a lion).
My elder sister is the pillar of our family (she single-handedly sustains and carries the burdens of the household).
Jemima is the apple of her father's eyes (she is cherished above all others).
The striker is the shining light in the school football team (he serves as the prime source of inspiration and guidance).

Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Concepts

Diphthong Endpoints

Closing Diphthong vs Centring Diphthong

Closing diphthongs (/aɪ/, /aʊ/, /ɔɪ/) glide upward toward close vowels /ɪ/ or /ʊ/ with jaw closure, while centring diphthongs (/ʊə/, /ɪə/, /eə/) glide toward the neutral central vowel /ə/[cite: 107].

Determinative Reference

Definite Article vs Indefinite Article

The definite article (*the*) identifies a specific, known entity or unique item (*the Bible*), whereas the indefinite article (*a* / *an*) introduces a non-specific countable entity (*a doctor*, *an hour*)[cite: 107].

Number Classification

Cardinal Numbers vs Ordinal Numbers

Cardinal numbers denote exact numerical quantity or counting (*one*, *two*, *seven*), while ordinal numbers denote sequential ranking or position (*first*, *second*, *twenty-fifth*)[cite: 107].

Figurative Comparison

Simile vs Metaphor

A simile states that one object is *like* or *as* another using connective words (*swims like a fish*), whereas a metaphor makes a direct equation without connectives (*Ade was a lion in the fight*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using the indefinite article "an" before words starting with the letter *u* that are pronounced with an initial consonant glide /j/[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

The choice between *a* and *an* depends on sound, not spelling; words like *unit*, *university*, and *union* begin with the consonant sound /j/ and take **a** (*a unit*, not *an unit*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Using the indefinite article "a" before words starting with a silent letter *h*[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

In words like *hour*, *heir*, and *honest*, the initial *h* is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound and requires **an** (*an hour*, *an honest man*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Writing an expository recipe or process in the past tense like a story[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Expository writing explains timeless or general procedures and must be written in the **simple present tense** (*wash the rice*, *boil the water*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Calling a direct metaphor a simile simply because two things are compared[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

A comparison is only a simile if it explicitly uses connective markers such as *like* or *as*; without those words, it is a metaphor[cite: 107].
