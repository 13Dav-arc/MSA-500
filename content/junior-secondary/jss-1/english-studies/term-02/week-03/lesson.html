Why This Matters to You

When you speak, read, and write in English, every subtle sound and grammatical choice carries meaning and emotion. Placing the tip of your tongue between your teeth allows you to pronounce words like thick and those accurately without confusing them with tick or dose. At the same time, mastering past tenses enables you to recount past actions with exact timing, whether you are writing an official formal application to an administrative office, discerning an author's mood in an inspirational passage, or exploring the ancient supernatural realms of myths and legends.

Key Terms

Dental Fricative A consonant sound produced when the tip of the tongue is placed lightly against or between the upper front teeth, forcing the airstream through a narrow friction-producing opening[cite: 107].
Past Tense A verb form used to describe actions, states of being, or habitual events that started or were completed at a time prior to the moment of speaking[cite: 107].
Author's Mood The emotional atmosphere, attitude, or tone conveyed by a writer toward their subject matter, detectable through word choice and viewpoint[cite: 107].
Formal Letter A business or official letter addressed to persons in their professional or administrative capacity, characterized by formal language, a specific layout with two addresses, and a stated subject heading[cite: 107].
Legend A traditional folk narrative regarded as historical fact by both narrator and audience, which recounts the heroic adventures of past figures or supernatural encounters after the creation of the world[cite: 107].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Consonants /θ/ and /ð/

Consonants /θ/ and /ð/ are dental fricatives produced by placing the tip of the tongue gently between or behind the front upper teeth:

/θ/ is a voiceless dental fricative articulated without vocal cord vibration.
Common spelling symbol for /θ/: `th` as in anthem, thick, both, bath, north, sheath, thorn, theme, thank, breath, wrath, method, ether, filthy, earthly, and myth.
/ð/ is a voiced dental fricative articulated with noticeable vocal cord vibration as air escapes past the tongue tip.
Common spelling symbol for /ð/: `th` as in then, that, this, those, thee, bathe, writhe, loathe, soothe, clothe, than, either, worthy, father, mother, further, brother, and rather.

Aspect 2: Structure — Past Tenses

The past tense denotes actions, habits, or states of being that were initiated or completed in the past. Regular verbs form their past tense by adding -ed to the base form (e.g., worked, passed), while irregular verbs change root vowels or adopt unique forms (e.g., made, sang, kept).

The Four Subdivisions of the Past Tense:

1.
Simple Past Tense: Indicates an action that started and finished in the past, or describes a past habit no longer practiced.
I worked yesterday.
Olu passed the last stage of the examination last year.
He used to travel home when he was young.
1.
Past Continuous Tense: Expresses an action that was ongoing in the past, often interrupted by another past action.
Formed using was/were + -ing verb.
I was teaching the students yesterday when the principal sent for me.
While the bus was moving, the woman jumped down.
1.
Past Perfect Tense: Used when two actions occurred in the past and one was completed before the other. The earlier action takes the past perfect (had + past participle), while the subsequent action takes the simple past.
The boy had died before help reached him.
By the time I got to the office, Olu had left for the meeting.
The suspect had escaped when the police raised the alarm.
1.
Past Perfect Continuous Tense: Indicates an action that had been underway for an extended duration before a specific point in the past.
Formed using had been + -ing verb.
We had been praying since 1998 before God intervened in 2008.
The girl had been writing SSCE for five years before she passed it last year.

Reading Passage:

Dedicated teachers observe their pupils' silent anxieties, patiently explain complex concepts repeatedly, and guide immature minds from ignorance into enlightenment.

>

> Regrettably, some rebellious students treat the wise counsel of their teachers with contempt, skipping remedial classes and displaying insolent attitudes toward school authority. Such recalcitrant youths often discover too late that scholastic success cannot be attained through defiance. In every civilized society, the guidance of elders and educators remains an unshakeable foundation; indeed, history continually reminds us that nobody has ever beaten his elders and made it in life.

>

> When learners approach their teachers with humility, reverence, and earnest inquisitiveness, an enriching academic mentorship flourishes. The classroom transforms into a fertile nursery where latent talents are discovered and nurtured to fruition. Decades after leaving school walls, celebrated doctors, engineers, and national leaders consistently point back to the patient, encouraging words of their early teachers as the true spark that ignited their greatness.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Format and Features of Formal Letters

Formal letters are letters written to offices, organizations, institutions, and administrative dignitaries. They are also called official or business letters.

Structural Layout of a Formal Letter:

1.
Writer's Address & Date: Placed at the top right-hand side of the page with appropriate punctuation and the date written in full (e.g., 25th August, 2026.).
2.
Addressee's Designation & Address: Placed at the left margin below the level of the date, stating the recipient's official title and organization (e.g., The Education Secretary, Zenith Heights High School, Lagos.).
3.
Salutation: Placed at the left margin: Dear Sir, or Dear Madam, (never informal greetings like Hi or Dear John).
4.
Letter Heading (Title): Written centrally in capital letters or title case with underlining, stating the subject matter concisely: APPLICATION FOR ADMISSION INTO JS 1.
5.
Body of the Letter: Well-paragraphed formal prose without colloquial slang or contracted forms (use do not instead of don't).
Introduction: State clearly the purpose of writing.
Content Paragraphs: Provide supporting facts, qualifications, or reasons.
Conclusion: A polite closing request or statement of appreciation.
1.
Subscript / Complimentary Close: Written at the bottom right-hand or left-hand side as Yours faithfully, (with a capital Y and small f, followed by a comma).
2.
Signature and Full Name: The writer's signature, followed by the full official name clearly written underneath.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Oral Literature: Myths and Legends

Myths and legends belong to traditional oral literature—highly imaginative narratives passed down across generations:

Myths: Religious folktales that explain the origins of the universe, natural phenomena, and the creation of humanity and animals. Both the narrator and audience historically regard them as sacred truth. The central characters are typically gods, spirits, or divine supernatural deities operating in an early, primeval world.
Legends: Traditional folk history dealing with real human experiences that occurred after the creation of the world was accomplished. While often incorporating supernatural embellishments (such as ghosts, enchanted weapons, or monsters), legends are based on historical human heroes, heroines, kings, and warriors who performed extraordinary adventures (e.g., Queen Amina of Zaria, Oduduwa, Moremi, or King Arthur).

Comparing Core Linguistic and Literary Contrasts

Phonetic Voicing

Voiceless /θ/ vs Voiced /ð/

Both are dental fricatives formed with the tongue tip at the upper front teeth, but /θ/ has no vocal cord vibration (*anthem*, *bath*, *myth*), whereas /ð/ produces active vocal cord vibration (*mother*, *bathe*, *this*)[cite: 107].

Past Tense Ordering

Simple Past vs Past Perfect

Simple past indicates a standalone past event (*Olu left*), while past perfect establishes that one past action concluded prior to another past event (*Olu had left before we arrived*)[cite: 107].

Oral Literature Scope

Myth vs Legend

A myth explains primeval cosmic creation involving gods and deities[cite: 107], while a legend recounts the historical adventures of human heroes after creation[cite: 107].

Letter Subscriptions

Formal vs Informal Letters

A formal letter requires two addresses and concludes with *Yours faithfully,* followed by a signature and full name[cite: 107], while an informal letter uses one address and ends with *Yours sincerely,* or a first name[cite: 107].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Substituting /t/ or /d/ for the dental fricatives /θ/ and /ð/ (e.g., pronouncing *think* as *tink*, or *this* as *dis*)[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

Place the tip of your tongue directly between your teeth so that air escapes with friction, rather than letting the tongue strike the roof of the mouth as a plosive[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Using the past perfect tense for a single standalone past action (e.g., saying *"I had eaten yesterday"* without an accompanying second past action)[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

The past perfect (*had eaten*) is specifically reserved to show that an action occurred before another past event; a single past event requires the simple past (*I ate yesterday*)[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Ending a formal letter addressed to an unknown official with *Yours sincerely* instead of *Yours faithfully*[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

When addressing an official as *Dear Sir* or *Dear Madam*, the mandatory complimentary close is *Yours faithfully,*[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Omitting the writer's signature in a formal letter before typing or writing the full name[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

A formal letter is invalid without the writer's physical signature placed directly above the full name[cite: 107].
