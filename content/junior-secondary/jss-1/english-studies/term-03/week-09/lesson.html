Aspect 1: Speech Work Remediation — Liquids, Silent Consonants, Syllabic Stress, and Intonation

Speech proficiency requires coordinated articulatory placement, awareness of orthographic silent letters, and melodic pitch control:

1.
Liquids /l/ and /r/:
/l/ (Lateral): The tongue tip firmly contacts the alveolar ridge, while the voiced airstream escapes around the lowered sides of the tongue (leak, bleed, play, flesh).
/r/ (Post-Alveolar Continuant): The tongue tip curls toward the rear of the tooth ridge without touching it, letting air flow centrally without friction (rack, breed, pray, fresh).
1.
Silent Consonant Clusters:
Silent "l": Preceded by vowels in words like walk, talk, calm, palm, yolk, could, and should.
Silent "b" (final -mb): The letter b is unpronounced after m (climb, comb, dumb, plumb, lamb, crumb).
Silent "n" (final -mn): The letter n is unpronounced after m (hymn, condemn, solemn, column).
Silent "k" (initial kn-): The letter k is silent before n (know, knot, knew, knit, knowledge).
Silent "g" (initial gn-): The letter g is silent before n (gnash, gnaw, gnat).
1.
Disyllabic Stress Shift:
Nouns are stressed on the first syllable (IM-port, EX-port, CON-duct, STU-dent, DOC-tor).
Verbs are stressed on the second syllable (im-PORT, ex-PORT, con-DUCT, de-PEND, pro-TECT).
1.
Trisyllabic Stress Distribution:
First Syllable: CHA-rac-ter, DE-li-cate, FOR-tu-nate, BA-che-lor.
Second Syllable: dis-CUS-sion, pro-JEC-tion, oc-CA-sion, stra-TE-gic.
Third Syllable: dis-a-GREE, af-ter-NOON, re-fu-GEE, un-der-STAND.
1.
Intonation on Question Tags:
Falling Tune ($\searrow$): Expresses certainty and invites agreement with what is already known.
Rising Tune ($\nearrow$): Expresses genuine uncertainty, doubt, or an authentic request for information.

Aspect 2: Structure Remediation — Voice Transformations Across Tenses and Question Tag Rules

Mastering English syntax requires exact concord in active-passive conversions and question tags:

Voice Transformations Across All Aspectual Tenses:
1.
Present Simple: He writes two letters $\rightarrow$ Two letters are written by him.
2.
Past Simple: Obi cleaned the blackboard $\rightarrow$ The blackboard was cleaned by Obi.
3.
Present Continuous: I am kicking a ball $\rightarrow$ A ball is being kicked by me.
4.
Present Perfect: He has written two letters $\rightarrow$ Two letters have been written by him.
5.
Past Continuous: I was writing my note $\rightarrow$ My note was being written by me.
6.
Past Perfect: The farmer had killed a snake $\rightarrow$ A snake had been killed by the farmer.
Agentless Passives: Used when the doer is unknown, obvious, or unimportant: The window was broken; The safe was blown open with dynamite.
Question Tag Inversion Rules:
Opposing Polarity: Positive statement $\rightarrow$ negative tag (She is here, isn't she?); Negative statement $\rightarrow$ positive tag (You are not leaving, are you?).
Non-Human Pronoun "It": Used for infants, animals, objects, and abstract nouns (The dog has eaten, hasn't it?; The rain is heavy, isn't it?).
Special "I am" Tag: Forms with aren't I? (I am your teacher, aren't I?).
Imperative Commands & Suggestions: Commands take will you? (Close the door, will you?); suggestions with Let's take shall we? (Let's read, shall we?).
Indefinite Human Pronouns: Everybody, somebody, and nobody take the plural pronoun they in the tag (Everybody understands, don't they?; Nobody called, did they?).

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing Remediation — Expository Essay Architecture and Cohesion

An expository essay explains how something is made, how a game is played, or how a social challenge can be solved. It explains the "how" and "why" using objective, present-tense prose:

Four Core Structural Phases:
1.
Title & Byline: Clear and bold; no letter addresses, salutations, or complimentary closes.
2.
Introduction: Defines the process, product, or challenge and states its importance.
3.
Sequential Body Paragraphs: Each paragraph develops one brainstormed point, connected by transitions:
Addition: Also, In addition to these, Apart from these, Furthermore.
Contrast: However, On the contrary, Nevertheless.
Consequence: Consequently, Therefore, As a direct result.
1.
Conclusion: Summarizes the process or suggests remedies (e.g., recommending vocational youth empowerment to curb unemployment).

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English Remediation — Poetic Forms, Language, and Thematic Synthesis

Poetry condenses human emotion and philosophy through structured verse and figurative imagery:

Definitions & Genres: A poem is written in verse; poetry is the study or body of poems; an anthology is a collection of poems; a poet writes poems.
Epic: A lengthy narrative poem celebrating the deeds of heroic figures.
Ode: A serious, dignified lyric poem praising an individual, object, or abstract concept.
Dirge & Elegy: Poems of mourning; a dirge is a funeral chant, while an elegy is a reflective lament on human mortality.
Sonnet: A strict lyric poem consisting of exactly fourteen lines with an established rhyme scheme.
Figurative Devices in Third Term Poems:
Simile & Metaphor: Direct comparison with like/as versus implied direct equation.
Personification & Apostrophe: Giving human traits to non-human things versus directly addressing an absent person or lifeless object.
Symbolism & Repetition: Concrete objects representing abstract meanings (e.g., crown = kingship) and repeating words for rhythmic emphasis.
Synecdoche: Using a physical part to represent the entire whole (All hands on deck).
Thematic Synthesis: Akeem Ajibade's "Let My Mother Sing" honors maternal selflessness and domestic stability, while Sanyaolu's poem on racism confronts racial discrimination by asserting universal human equality. Together, they demonstrate how poetry defends human dignity across domestic and societal spheres.

Articulatory Dynamics

Consonant /l/ vs Consonant /r/

/l/ requires alveolar tongue-tip contact with lateral airflow (*play*, *bleed*)[cite: 1], whereas /r/ curves near the ridge without touching it, letting air escape centrally without friction (*pray*, *breed*)[cite: 1].

Voice Transformation

Active Voice vs Passive Voice

Active voice features the subject performing the action (*Obi cleaned the blackboard*)[cite: 1], while passive voice features the subject receiving the action (*The blackboard was cleaned by Obi*)[cite: 1].

Question Tag Pitch

Falling Intonation ($\searrow$) vs Rising Intonation ($\nearrow$)

A falling tune indicates certainty and invites agreement (*She is here, isn't she? $\searrow$*)[cite: 1], whereas a rising tune indicates genuine uncertainty and asks an authentic question (*She is here, isn't she? $\nearrow$*).

Poetic Mode

Maternal Tribute vs Anti-Racism Protest

Ajibade's poem focuses on gratitude, sacrifice, and maternal devotion[cite: 1], while Sanyaolu's poem denounces racial prejudice and affirms shared biological equality[cite: 1].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the silent "b" in words ending in *-mb* (e.g., pronouncing *crumb* as /krʌmb/)[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

In words ending in *-mb*, the letter "b" is silent; pronounce them ending cleanly in /m/ (*crumb* /krʌm/, *climb* /klaɪm/)[cite: 1].

Common Mistake

Omitting the auxiliary "being" in continuous passive transformations (e.g., writing *"The letter is written"* instead of *"The letter is being written"*)[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Continuous active tenses require *being* alongside the past participle in passive constructions to preserve ongoing aspect[cite: 1].

Common Mistake

Pairing positive statements with positive tags, or using *"isn't it?"* for human subjects (e.g., *"He is coming, isn't it?"*)[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Question tags require opposing polarity and pronoun concord; say *"He is coming, isn't he?"*[cite: 1]

Common Mistake

Confusing synecdoche with metaphor in poetry analysis[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Metaphor equates two different things (*Ade was a lion*), while synecdoche uses an actual physical part of a person or object to represent the whole (*All hands on deck*)[cite: 1].
