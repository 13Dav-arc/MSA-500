Why This Matters to You

When reading an English text aloud, our eyes often encounter letters that our voices must never speak, from the silent b in climb to the quiet k in knowledge. Mastering silent letters ensures you sound polished and educated, while controlling the passive voice across multiple tenses lets you report events with precise timing and focus, whether the subject is scoring a goal or an essay is being written. When you combine that grammatical agility with sequential paragraph linkers to analyze issues like unemployment and explore poetry confronting social prejudice, you develop the comprehensive command needed for mature expression.

Key Terms

Silent Letter An alphabetic letter that appears in the written spelling of a word but is completely omitted during oral pronunciation, such as *b* in *comb* or *k* in *know*[cite: 2].
Tense-Voice Concord The systematic grammatical rule ensuring that when an active sentence transforms into the passive voice, the auxiliary verb *to be* matches the exact tense of the original active verb[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Nasal Consonants and Silent Letters (mb, mn, kn, gn)

English spelling often retains historical letters that have ceased to be pronounced in modern speech. Understanding the distribution of silent letters prevents pronunciation errors:

*Silent "b" in final -mb clusters: When the letter b follows m at the end of a word or root syllable, the end sound is /m/; the b* is completely silent.
Pronounced examples ending in /m/: climb, comb, dumb, plumb, tomb, bomb, numb, lamb, crumb.
*Silent "n" in final -mn clusters: When n follows m at the end of a word, the sound /m/ is articulated while n* remains silent.
Pronounced examples ending in /m/: damn, hymn, condemn, solemn, column.
*Silent "k" in initial kn- clusters: When k precedes n at the beginning of a word, the k* is completely silent and only /n/ is pronounced.
Pronounced examples starting with /n/: know, knowledge, knot, knew, knit, knee, knife.
*Silent "g" in initial gn- clusters: When g is placed before n at word beginnings, the g* is silent while /n/ is pronounced.
Pronounced examples starting with /n/: gnash, gnaw, gnat, gnome.
Regular Nasals: In standard positions without silent pairings, /m/ and /n/ are voiced nasal sonorants (e.g., manner, banner, nanny, funnel, cranny, grammar, commission).

Aspect 2: Structure — Active and Passive Voice Across Tenses

When converting a sentence from active to passive voice, the auxiliary verb to be must adopt the exact tense and aspect of the original active verb, followed immediately by the past participle of the main verb:

1.
Present Simple Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{Base Verb} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{is/are} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I kick a ball $\rightarrow$ Passive: A ball is kicked by me.
Active: He writes two letters $\rightarrow$ Passive: Two letters are written by him.
1.
Past Simple Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{Past Verb} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{was/were} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I kicked the ball $\rightarrow$ Passive: The ball was kicked by me.
Active: The boy wrote a letter $\rightarrow$ Passive: A letter was written by the boy.
1.
Present Continuous Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{am/is/are} + \text{-ing Verb} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{am/is/are} + \text{being} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I am kicking a ball $\rightarrow$ Passive: A ball is being kicked by me.
Active: He is writing two letters $\rightarrow$ Passive: Two letters are being written by him.
1.
Present Perfect Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{have/has} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{have/has} + \text{been} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I have kicked the ball $\rightarrow$ Passive: The ball has been kicked by me.
Active: He has written two letters $\rightarrow$ Passive: Two letters have been written by him.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — Behaviour of Students

Reading Passage:

> The alarming deterioration in the behaviour of some secondary school students nowadays gives educational authorities, parents, and community elders profound concern. Classrooms that were once revered as sacred citadels of learning, moral rectitude, and mutual respect are increasingly disrupted by recalcitrant youths who display blatant insolence toward their teachers. Many educators struggle to comprehend why some teenagers deliberately choose rebellion, loitering along school corridors during instructional periods, defying uniform regulations, and engaging in unprovoked physical squabbles with their classmates.

>

> An inquisitive observer will discover that peer pressure, unmonitored digital media exposure, and parental negligence serve as primary catalysts for this breakdown in school discipline. When students associate with indisciplined cliques, they swiftly adopt vulgar language, treat academic homework with disdain, and vandalize laboratory equipment. Rather than recognizing fellow students as academic companions and supportive brothers and sisters working toward common intellectual goals, disruptive elements create an atmosphere of hostility and intimidation that impairs the learning progress of the entire class.

>

> Remedying this moral decay requires concerted collaboration between school administrations and families. Guidance and counselling units must be actively empowered to mentor troubled learners, while disciplinary committees enforce statutory rules impartially. Concurrently, parents must resume their foundational duty of instilling ethical values at home. When order, mutual empathy, and disciplined habits are restored to the classroom, students rediscover their academic purpose and fulfill their potential as responsible future leaders.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Logical Sequencing and Paragraph Linkers

An expository essay explains how things work, analyzes societal phenomena, or instructs readers through a systematic breakdown of facts. To ensure coherence, paragraphs must be arranged in a logical sequence, unified by paragraph linkers (transitional devices):

Brainstorming and Outlining:
Understand the topic thoroughly and brainstorm key issues into an itemized list.
Group related ideas together and re-arrange them in logical order, ensuring that each distinct point occupies its own well-developed paragraph.
Essential Paragraph Linkers:
Addition: Also, In addition to these, Apart from these, Furthermore.
Contrast: However, On the other hand, Nevertheless, But.
Time & Concurrence: Meanwhile, Subsequently, At the same time.
Model Topic Outline: "The Evil of Unemployment in My Country":
Title: Centered at the top in bold or capital letters.
Introduction: Defines unemployment (the inability of qualified, energetic citizens to secure gainful work) and states its national prevalence.
Body Paragraph 1 (Crime & Insecurity): Analyzes how idleness drives frustrated youths into armed robbery, thuggery, and hired assassination (Paragraph linker: "First and foremost...").
Body Paragraph 2 (Social Vices): Discusses the rise of prostitution, substance abuse, and moral degradation among jobless citizens (Paragraph linker: "In addition to these security challenges...").
Body Paragraph 3 (Health & Family Breakdown): Details the psychological affliction of diseases, malnutrition, broken marriages, and premature death/suicide (Paragraph linker: "Apart from these social vices, unemployment inflicts severe domestic devastation...").
Conclusion: Recommends viable solutions, such as vocational education, agricultural empowerment, and industrial expansion, summarizing the overall message.

Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Distinctions

Silent Position

Silent Initial "k" vs Silent Final "b"

Initial *k* is completely unpronounced before *n* at word beginnings (*know*, *knot*)[cite: 2], while final *b* is unpronounced after *m* at word ends (*climb*, *dumb*)[cite: 2].

Voice Aspect

Present Continuous Passive vs Present Perfect Passive

Present continuous passive uses *am/is/are being* + past participle (*A ball is being kicked*)[cite: 2], whereas present perfect passive uses *have/has been* + past participle (*A ball has been kicked*)[cite: 2].

Textual Cohesion

Additive Linkers vs Adversative Linkers

Additive linkers (*In addition*, *Apart from these*) introduce supplementary points[cite: 2], while adversative linkers (*However*, *On the contrary*) introduce contrasting perspectives or exceptions[cite: 2].

Poetic Mode

Social Protest Poem vs Funeral Dirge

A social protest poem (like Sanyaolu's poem on racism) challenges societal discrimination and promotes equality[cite: 2], whereas a funeral dirge laments the death of an individual during bereavement[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pronouncing the letter "b" in words ending in *-mb* (e.g., pronouncing *plumb* as /plʌmb/ or *climb* as /klaɪmb/)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

When "b" follows "m" at the end of a word, it is silent; pronounce them cleanly as /plʌm/ and /klaɪm/[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Omitting the continuous auxiliary "being" when converting continuous tenses into the passive voice[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Present continuous active (*is writing*) must become *is being written* in the passive; omitting *being* erroneously converts the tense to simple present (*is written*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Failing to use transitional linkers between body paragraphs in an expository essay[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Paragraphs should open with linking phrases like *Furthermore*, *In addition to these*, or *However* to preserve logical flow[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Assuming that silent "n" in *-mn* applies at the beginning of words[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

The letter "n" is only silent when preceded by "m" at the end of a word (*hymn*, *condemn*)[cite: 2]; initial "n" is always pronounced (*name*, *note*)[cite: 2].
