Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Stress in Three-Syllable Words

In English, words of three syllables distribute stress across different positions depending on their morphological structure and historical roots. Unlike two-syllable words where stress predominantly falls on the first or second syllable, three-syllable words can bear primary stress on the first, second, or third syllable:

1.
Stress on the First Syllable (Antepenultimate Stress):
In many English three-syllable nouns, adjectives, and adverbs, the primary stress falls on the first syllable.
Examples: DE-li-cate, FOR-tu-nate, PRE-sent-ly, CHA-rac-ter, BA-che-lor.
1.
Stress on the Second Syllable (Penultimate Stress):
In words featuring specific prefixes, suffixes, or Latinate roots, the primary stress falls on the second syllable.
Examples: dis-CUS-sion, pro-JEC-tion, oc-CA-sion, stra-TE-gic, co-LO-ni-al.
1.
Stress on the Third Syllable (Ultimate Stress):
Certain loanwords, compound constructions, and prefixed words bear their strongest prominence on the final (third) syllable.
Examples: dis-a-GREE, dis-be-LIEF, af-ter-NOON, re-fu-GEE, un-der-STAND.

Aspect 2: Structure — Question Tags: Polarity and Concord Rules

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement. It converts a declarative sentence into an inquiry to confirm information or invite agreement.

Fundamental Rules Governing Question Tags:

1.
Rule of Opposing Polarity:
A positive statement takes a negative tag:
You are leaving now, aren't you?
Sola took the book, didn't she?
She is here, isn't she?
A negative statement takes a positive tag:
You are not leaving now, are you?
The boys are not leaving yet, are they?
1.
Pronoun and Auxiliary Concord:
The auxiliary verb in the statement is repeated in the tag (or replaced by do/does/did for simple tense verbs without auxiliaries).
The subject pronoun of the statement is repeated in the tag.
1.
Special Use of the Pronoun "It":
Use the neuter pronoun "it" in the tag when the subject refers to a little baby, an animal, an inanimate object, or an abstract noun:
The dog has eaten its food, hasn't it?
My baby is sleeping, isn't it?
A good idea is vital to progress, isn't it?
The rain is heavy today, isn't it?
1.
Answering Tag Questions:
Answers reflect factual reality rather than the grammatical phrasing of the tag:
You are leaving now, aren't you? $\rightarrow$ Yes, I am (if leaving) or No, I am not (if staying).
The dog has eaten its food, hasn't it? $\rightarrow$ Yes, it has or No, it hasn't.
You bring them every day, don't you? $\rightarrow$ Yes, I do or No, I don't.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The Value of Obedience

Reading Passage:

> The paramount importance of obedience in the life of any individual, institution, or civilized society cannot be overstated. Obedience is the moral adhesive that binds diverse human beings into harmonious communities governed by shared legal statutes and ethical norms. Without collective obedience to constituted authority, societal structures rapidly disintegrate into violent chaos, unchecked banditry, and crippling disorder. From the nuclear family unit to the classroom, and from corporate establishments to sovereign nations, disciplined compliance with established rules guarantees mutual protection and sustainable advancement.

>

> In an educational setting, a student who embraces prompt obedience toward school regulations and teachers creates an optimal atmosphere for intellectual growth. Punctual arrival at morning assemblies, dedicated submission of assignments, and respectful decorum during lectures are not oppressive burdens designed to restrict youthful freedom. Rather, they are character-building habits that shield learners from destructive peer distractions and academic failure. Conversely, defiant students who celebrate disobedience find that their stubbornness alienates mentors, incurs severe disciplinary sanctions, and frequently culminates in derailed ambitions.

>

> In the wider world, obedience to civic responsibilities—such as obeying traffic signals, paying statutory taxes, and protecting public utilities—distinguishes prosperous societies from failing states. Citizens who abide by traffic guidelines preserve lives, while merchants who obey commercial standards ensure fair economic transactions. History consistently demonstrates that order yields tranquility, while rebellion breeds misery; therefore, every youth must recognize that disciplined obedience is not weakness, but the ultimate foundation of personal greatness and national honor.

Guided Comprehension Questions & Model Responses:

1.
Question: Why is obedience described as essential in the life of any person or organization?
Model Answer: Obedience is essential because it maintains societal order, prevents chaos and lawlessness, and ensures peaceful coexistence.
1.
Question: What happens to a community when citizens refuse to obey constituted authority?
Model Answer: The societal structure disintegrates into violent chaos, lawlessness, and disorder.
1.
Question: Mention two practical ways students demonstrate obedience within a secondary school.
Model Answer: Students demonstrate obedience by arriving punctually at morning assemblies and submitting their academic homework faithfully.

1.

Target Vocabulary in Context:

Paramount (Adjective): More important than anything else; supreme.
Disintegrate (Verb): To break up, fall apart, or decompose into small parts or complete ruin.
Decorum (Noun): Behavior in keeping with good taste, etiquette, and social propriety.
Constitued (Adjective): Established, authorized, or appointed by formal legal authority.
Alienates (Verb): Causes someone to feel isolated, estranged, or hostile.
Sanctions (Noun): Penalties or punitive measures imposed for disobeying rules or laws.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Guided Composition ("The Food I Like Best")

A guided expository composition utilizes a series of structured prompting questions to assist the writer in developing a systematic, paragraph-by-paragraph account of a process, personal preference, or object.

Writing Constraint: Do NOT number your points or write answers as fragmented list items; organize your responses into cohesive, well-developed prose paragraphs.
Guiding Prompts and Essay Structure for "The Food I Like Best":
1.
Title: Boldly stated at the top (e.g., THE FOOD I LIKE BEST: POUNDED YAM AND EGUSI SOUP).
2.
Introduction (Identification & Source): Which type of food do you enjoy most? Describe it generally, stating its agricultural origins (e.g., white yams, melon seeds) and cultural significance.
3.
Body Paragraph 1 (Preparation Process): How is the meal prepared? Detail the peeling, boiling, and pounding of the yam in a wooden mortar, alongside the blending and simmering of melon seeds with palm oil, leafy vegetables, and seasoned meats.
4.
Body Paragraph 2 (Personal Affinity & Frequency): Why do you prefer this meal over other foods, when did you first develop a taste for it, and how frequently do you consume it?
5.
Body Paragraph 3 (Nutritional Benefits): What dietary value do you gain from it (e.g., carbohydrates for energy, proteins for tissue building, vitamins for immune health)?
6.
Conclusion (Recommendation): Would you recommend this dish to friends or visitors to Nigeria? Summarize its appeal and conclude with a warm, inviting remark.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Poetic Devices in Recommended Poems

Poets employ figurative devices to express human experiences with striking imagery and musical cadence. In recommended selections such as Akeem Ajibade's "Let My Mother Sing" and Sanyaolu's poem on racism, several major literary devices feature prominently:

1.
Symbolism: The use of concrete symbols to represent abstract concepts beyond their literal definitions (e.g., a crown symbolizing sovereign kingship; chains symbolizing racial oppression; nursing arms symbolizing maternal protection).
2.
Apostrophe: A figure of speech in which the speaker turns aside to directly address an absent person, a dead ancestor, or an inanimate object as though present and listening (e.g., "O Mother, let your songs heal our weary souls!").
3.
Synecdoche: Using a part of something to stand for the whole entity, or the whole to represent a part (e.g., "All hands on deck", where hands represents the entire workforce of persons).
4.
Repetition: The deliberate re-occurrence of specific words, phrases, or full lines to create musical emphasis, intensify emotional mood, and reinforce thematic resonance (e.g., "He that will come, will come").
5.
Personification: Investing inanimate objects, animals, or abstract forces with human emotions and capabilities (e.g., "The whispering night embraced the sleepy village").

Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Distinctions

Trisyllabic Stress Position

Antepenultimate vs Ultimate Stress

Antepenultimate stress falls on the first syllable of a three-syllable word (**DE-li-cate**, **CHA-rac-ter**)[cite: 2], whereas ultimate stress falls on the final (third) syllable (**dis-a-GREE**, **af-ter-NOON**)[cite: 2].

Tag Polarity

Positive Statement Tag vs Negative Statement Tag

A positive statement requires a negative tag (*She is here, isn't she?*)[cite: 2], while a negative statement requires a positive tag (*She is not here, is she?*)[cite: 2].

Non-Human Concord

Personal Pronouns vs Neuter "It" in Tags

Human subjects take gendered/plural pronouns in tags (*he*, *she*, *they*)[cite: 2], whereas babies, animals, objects, and abstract nouns require the neuter pronoun **it** (*The dog has eaten, hasn't it?*)[cite: 2].

Figurative Scope

Apostrophe vs Personification

Personification describes an inanimate object acting like a human (*The sun smiled*)[cite: 2], while apostrophe addresses an absent person or entity directly (*O sun, why do you burn so fierce?*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Stressing the second syllable of words like "character" or "delicate" (e.g., pronouncing *cha-RAC-ter* instead of **CHA-rac-ter**)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

In standard English, words like *character*, *delicate*, *fortunate*, and *bachelor* have primary stress on the first syllable (**CHA-rac-ter**, **DE-li-cate**)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Pairing a positive statement with a positive question tag (e.g., saying *"You are coming, are you?"* when seeking ordinary confirmation)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Standard question tags must follow opposing polarity; a positive statement takes a negative tag (*You are coming, aren't you?*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using the pronoun "he" or "she" for unknown animals or abstract nouns in tags (e.g., saying *"The rain is falling, isn't he?"*)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Abstract nouns, natural phenomena, and animals take the neuter pronoun **it** (*The rain is heavy today, isn't it?*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Numbering points in a guided composition instead of writing continuous prose paragraphs[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Guiding questions should be synthesized into cohesive, well-developed paragraphs joined by logical linkers[cite: 2].
