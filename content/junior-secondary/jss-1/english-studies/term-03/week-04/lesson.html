Why This Matters to You

When speaking or reading English, placing emphasis on the wrong syllable can completely alter a word's grammatical identity, turning a noun like an IM-port into a verb like to im-PORT. Similarly, choosing the passive voice allows a speaker to report past incidents with precision, highlighting what was done rather than who did it, such as noting that a forgotten bag of money had been returned. Whether exploring the inspiring honesty of a Lagos taxi driver, arranging an expository essay in logical paragraphs, or chanting oral verses passed down across generations, controlling these skills empowers you to communicate with clarity, authority, and moral purpose.

Key Terms

Syllabic Stress The extra breath force, loudness, pitch prominence, or muscular effort placed on a specific syllable within a multi-syllable word to make it stand out[cite: 2].
Past Perfect Passive A grammatical passive construction formed with *had been* followed by the past participle of the main verb, showing that an action had been performed on the subject before another past event[cite: 2].
Contentment The mental state of being peacefully satisfied with what one has, shielding an individual from greedy desires and dishonest practices[cite: 2].
Brainstorming The creative pre-writing stage where a writer reflects deeply on a topic, generating and recording a comprehensive list of ideas before organizing them logically[cite: 2].
Oral Poetry Poetry composed, performed, and transmitted by word of mouth across generations through chanting, singing, or recitation rather than written printed texts[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Introduction to Syllabic Stress

A syllable is a unit of spoken sound pronounced with a single chest pulse or breath effort. In multi-syllabic words, syllables are not uttered with equal loudness; the syllable that receives extra breath force and pitch height is the stressed syllable:

One-Syllable Words: Words consisting of only one syllable (e.g., come, sit, pen) cannot bear independent word stress because there is no competing syllable to contrast with; word stress operates on words of two or more syllables.
Word-Class Stress Shift in Disyllabic Words: Many two-syllable words in English have identical spelling but belong to different parts of speech. As a general rule:
Nouns place stress on the first syllable.
Verbs place stress on the second syllable.

| Word | Noun Pronunciation (1st Syllable Stressed) | Verb Pronunciation (2nd Syllable Stressed) | Contextual Usage |

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Import | IM-port | im-PORT | Rice is an expensive IM-port; we must im-PORT less. |

| Export | EX-port | ex-PORT | Cocoa is a major EX-port; farmers ex-PORT cocoa. |

| Conduct | CON-duct | con-DUCT | His moral CON-duct was good; she will con-DUCT tests. |

| Escort | ES-cort | es-CORT | The police provided an ES-cort to es-CORT the cash. |

Other Two-Syllable Words:
First Syllable Stressed: EN-ter, O-pen, PA-per, MO-ney, BO-rrow.
Second Syllable Stressed: a-LIVE, di-VINE, a-PPLY, be-WARE, en-JOY.

Aspect 2: Structure — Passive Transformations: Past Continuous and Past Perfect Tenses

When transforming active sentences in past aspectual forms into the passive voice, the auxiliary verb to be must carry the exact past aspect, followed by the past participle of the action verb:

1.
Past Continuous Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{was/were} + \text{-ing Verb} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{was/were} + \text{being} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I was writing my note.
Passive: My note was being written by me.
Active: The butcher was killing a goat.
Passive: A goat was being killed by the butcher.
Active: The man was questioning the driver.
Passive: The driver was being questioned by the man.
1.
Past Perfect Tense:
Active: $\text{Subject} + \text{had} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{Object}$
Passive: $\text{Object} + \text{had been} + \text{Past Participle} + \text{by Agent}$
Active: I had kicked the ball before he arrived.
Passive: The ball had been kicked by me before he arrived.
Active: The farmer had killed a snake before daybreak.
Passive: A snake had been killed by the farmer before daybreak.
Active: The police had arrested the thief before noon.
Passive: The thief had been arrested by the police before noon.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The Taxi Driver

Reading Passage:

> Mr. Okonkwo was once a humble commercial taxi driver navigating the congested highways and labyrinthine streets of Lagos. Though he worked tirelessly from the break of dawn until late at night, he was a very poor man who struggled constantly to pay the modest rent on his one-room apartment and provide adequate nourishment for his young family. Despite his biting financial deprivation, Mr. Okonkwo possessed an unshakeable moral compass; he steadfastly refused to overcharge stranded commuters during torrential downpours or engage in the dubious practices common among unscrupulous transporters.

>

> One sweltering Friday afternoon, after dropping off an agitated passenger at the international airport terminal, Mr. Okonkwo parked under an almond tree to clean his vehicle. While dusting the rear passenger seat, his hand struck a bulky leather briefcase that had slipped beneath the driver's seat. Upon unzipping the compartment to search for identity documents, his eyes widened in utter disbelief: neatly stacked bundles of crisp, high-denomination foreign currencies totaled several millions of naira. A voice of desperation whispered that this sudden fortune could eradicate his poverty forever, buy him a fleet of modern commercial buses, and secure his family's comfort.

>

> However, Mr. Okonkwo promptly rejected the temptation to retain ill-gotten wealth. Reminding himself of his father's sacred counsel that an untainted name is superior to stolen gold, he drove straight to the airport police station and surrendered the intact briefcase. The owner, a foreign investor who was weeping in agony over the catastrophic loss, was astonished by the driver's extraordinary uprightness. The grateful merchant rewarded Mr. Okonkwo with a brand-new vehicle and an executive employment contract as his company's logistics manager, proving beyond all doubt that it truly pays to be contented and honest.

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Structuring Logical Sequences and Brainstorming

An expository essay explains how things are done, reveals facts about a process, or clarifies the causes and effects of social phenomena. Its structure requires methodical planning:

Brainstorming Stage:
Before writing, think deeply about the topic to uncover all relevant angles and consequences.
Jot down every idea in rough notes without editing immediately.
Group related ideas together, eliminating irrelevant points, and arrange the finalized concepts into a coherent outline.
Paragraph Development and Logical Sequencing:
Unity: Every paragraph must develop one central controlling idea introduced by a clear topic sentence.
Orderly Progression: Move logically from causes to consequences and solutions.
Transitional Linkers: Smoothly bridge ideas using connective adverbs: firstly, furthermore, in addition to these, however, meanwhile, consequently, apart from these.
Standard Four-Part Blueprint:
1.
Title: Written boldly at the top (e.g., THE EVIL OF UNEMPLOYMENT IN MY COUNTRY).
2.
Introduction: Defines the core subject, sets the national context, and highlights why the issue demands urgent analysis.
3.
Body Paragraphs: Develops the brainstormed points systematically (one major consequence per paragraph).
4.
Conclusion: Summarizes the main arguments and proposes constructive recommendations.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Oral Poetry vs. Written Poetry

Poetry represents one of the oldest forms of human artistic expression, existing in two distinct traditions:

1.
Oral Poetry:
Composed and transmitted by word of mouth from generation to generation before or alongside written literacy.
Performed dynamically before a community audience, often accompanied by musical instruments, clapping, drumming, and dance.
Relies heavily on mnemonic devices: strong rhythmic beats, repetitive refrains, parallel sentence structures, and evocative sound effects.
Examples: Traditional praise chants (oriki), occupational hunting chants, cradle songs (lullabies), heroic war chants, and burial incantations.
1.
Written Poetry:
Composed through written text by an individual poet who arranges words deliberately into lines and stanzas on the page.
Relies on visual typography, line breaks, punctuation pauses, and intricate literary devices designed for private reading and reflective recitation.
Examples: Sonnets, formal odes, elegies, and modern social protest verse.

Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Distinctions

Stress Distribution

Disyllabic Nouns vs Disyllabic Verbs

Two-syllable nouns typically take stress on the first syllable (*CON-duct*, *EX-port*)[cite: 2], while related two-syllable verbs shift stress to the second syllable (*con-DUCT*, *ex-PORT*)[cite: 2].

Aspectual Passive

Past Continuous Passive vs Past Perfect Passive

Past continuous passive uses *was/were being* + past participle (*The car was being cleaned*)[cite: 2], whereas past perfect passive uses *had been* + past participle (*The car had been cleaned*)[cite: 2].

Poetic Transmission

Oral Poetry vs Written Poetry

Oral poetry is performed dynamically by voice with music and refrains for a live audience[cite: 2], while written poetry is recorded in stanzas on paper for silent reading and structured recitation[cite: 2].

Character Response

Contentment vs Greed

Contentment accepts one's lawful earnings with peaceful satisfaction[cite: 2], while greed craves ill-gotten wealth regardless of moral boundaries[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Stressing single-syllable words in English sentences (e.g., attempting to place word-class stress on *come* or *sit*)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

A one-syllable word cannot have word stress within itself because stress is a relative prominence between two or more syllables in a word[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Stressing the second syllable of two-syllable nouns (e.g., pronouncing *IM-port* or *EX-port* as *im-PORT* or *ex-PORT* when using them as nouns)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

When used as nouns, these words must be stressed on the first syllable (**IM-port**, **EX-port**)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Confusing *being* with *been* in passive verb forms[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Continuous tenses take **being** (*was being written*)[cite: 2], while perfect tenses take **been** (*had been written*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Cramming multiple brainstormed points into a single, unfocused body paragraph in an expository essay[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Each distinct brainstormed point must occupy its own separate paragraph, linked logically with transitional connectives[cite: 2].
