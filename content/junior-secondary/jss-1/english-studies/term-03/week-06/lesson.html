When speaking in public, drafting a feature article for your school magazine, or reciting a moving poem about maternal love, your mastery of linguistic stress and structural emphasis shapes your impact on the audience. Shifting stress from the first syllable to the second can turn a person, like a DOC-tor, into an action, like to pro-TECT. Agentless Passive A passive construction in which the doer or performer of the action is intentionally omitted because the agent is unknown, obvious, or unimportant[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Speech Work — Stress Patterns in Two-Syllable Words

In English, stress pattern refers to the conventional position of primary stress in multi-syllabic words. In disyllabic (two-syllable) words, stress distribution often distinguishes grammatical word classes:

1.
Disyllabic Nouns (First Syllable Stressed):
The majority of two-syllable nouns take primary stress on their first syllable.
Examples: STU-dent, PIC-ture, MO-ther, TEA-cher, FA-ther, BUt-cher, TAI-lor, VEN-dor, TRA-der, DOC-tor, WOR-ker, DRI-ver, BUIL-der, FAR-mer, LAW-yer.
1.
Disyllabic Verbs (Second Syllable Stressed):
In two-syllable verbs, primary stress commonly shifts to the second syllable.
Examples: de-PEND, a-CHIEVE, pro-TECT, ex-PLAIN, in-SULT, ex-PORT, ap-PROACH.
1.
Weak Syllables:
Any syllable that contains a reduced vowel sound (such as schwa /ə/) or weak /ɪ/ is generally unstressed and spoken quickly with reduced breath force.

Aspect 2: Structure — Verbs: Passive Voice Constructions (With and Without By-Agent)

A sentence is in the passive voice when the grammatical subject is acted upon by the verb rather than performing the action.

In many passive constructions, the doer is indicated using a prepositional phrase introduced by by:
The injured man was helped by a passer-by.
The man was being questioned by the police.
The patient was operated on by a team of five surgeons.
My brother was given extra tuition by his teacher.
Agentless Passives (Passive Without "By"):

When the identity of the performer is unknown, obvious from context, general, or unimportant, the by-agent phrase is omitted:

The window was broken (the culprit is unknown).
The safe was blown open with dynamite (focuses on the criminal outcome rather than the identity of the thieves).
The sorting is done by machine (instrumental focus).
Roger has been given his promotion (the appointing committee is obvious).
An elderly man was run over while crossing the road.
I was showered with presents on my eighteenth birthday.

Aspect 3: Comprehension & Vocabulary — The New Alhaji

Reading Passage:

> When Alhaji Abdullahi returned safely to his home compound from the holy city of Mecca, his compound was filled with relatives, well-wishers, and neighbours who gathered to celebrate his spiritual return. Among the early visitors was his lifelong companion, Mr. Makama, who embraced him warmly and inquired with deep curiosity: "What was the holy Hajj truly like, Alhaji?" Alhaji Abdullahi smiled serenely, adjusting his embroidered white cap, and explained that performing the sacred pilgrimage was the most humbling, spiritually transformative experience of his entire existence.

>

> He recounted how millions of diverse worshippers from every continent on earth congregated as equal souls before their Creator, shedding all external markers of worldly wealth, ethnic prestige, and political influence. Dressed in simple, unadorned white garments, royalty walked alongside impoverished laborers, circumambulating the Kaaba in prayerful harmony and seeking forgiveness on the plain of Mount Arafat. The intense desert heat and demanding physical rites tested the endurance of every pilgrim, but the collective atmosphere of faith and brotherhood filled every heart with peace.

>

> After listening with rapt fascination to the vivid narration, Mr. Makama noticed a small, beautifully carved wooden prayer bead resting reverently upon a velvet cushion on the side table. Pointing toward the treasured heirloom, Mr. Makama asked if it had been bought at the holy markets of Medina. Alhaji Abdullahi gently picked up the beads and shook his head, remarking with tender affection: "No, my friend; this prayer bead accompanied my late father on his own pilgrimage four decades ago, and I carried it across the holy sands to keep it in sacred memory of his guidance".

Aspect 4: Continuous Writing — Expository Essay: Writing for Publication (School Magazine Article)

Writing an expository article for publication in your school magazine requires presenting verified facts, detailed characteristics, and methods of solving social issues in an objective, orderly manner.

Key Formatting Rules for Magazine Articles:
Do NOT write personal postal addresses, formal salutations (such as Dear Sir), or complimentary closes (such as Yours faithfully).
The composition must carry an eye-catching, informative title followed by a byline (e.g., By: [Your Name], JSS 1B).
The essay must proceed in coherent, well-linked prose paragraphs following a logical progression.
Model Topic: "The Evil of Unemployment in My Country":
Title: Written boldly at the top (e.g., THE MENACE OF UNEMPLOYMENT: CAUSES, CONSEQUENCES, AND REMEDIES).
Introduction: Defines unemployment and exposes its prevalence among energetic educated youths across urban and rural communities.
Body Paragraph 1 (Security Challenges): Discusses how idleness and financial frustration push jobless youths into violent crimes, armed robbery, pipeline vandalism, and thuggery.
Body Paragraph 2 (Social Vices): Details the surge in substance addiction, prostitution, and hired assassination fueled by economic hopelessness.
Body Paragraph 3 (Domestic & Health Crises): Analyzes the breakdown of marriages, disease affliction, chronic malnutrition, and depression leading to suicide.
Conclusion: Proposes practical remedies, including vocational technical training, agricultural funding, and industrial revival.

Aspect 5: Literature-in-English — Poetry: Figures of Speech in Akeem Ajibade's "Let My Mother Sing"

Poets deploy figures of speech to express complex ideas and deep emotions vividly. In Akeem Ajibade’s poem "Let My Mother Sing", the poet celebrates the sacrificial nature and enduring dignity of motherhood.

Major Poetic Devices and Figures of Speech in the Recommended Text:

1.
Simile: Directly comparing two different things using connective words like "like" or "as" (e.g., Her gentle voice is like morning dew).
2.
Metaphor: An implied comparison that treats two distinct things as one without using "like" or "as" (e.g., A mother is the radiant sun of the household).
3.
Personification: Attributing human life, emotions, or actions to inanimate objects or non-human entities (e.g., The restless wind wept through the night).
4.
Apostrophe: Addressing an absent person, a deceased relative, or an inanimate object directly as if listening (e.g., O Mother, hear your children's grateful chorus!).
5.
Symbolism: An image or concrete object that represents a deeper, abstract meaning beyond its literal sense (e.g., a crown symbolizing royal authority; a nursing bowl symbolizing maternal nurture).
6.
Repetition: The deliberate recurring use of words, phrases, or full lines to create musical rhythm and emphasize central emotions (e.g., Let my mother sing, let her sweet voice rise).
7.
Synecdoche: A figure of speech in which a part of something represents the whole, or the whole represents a part (e.g., "All hands on deck" where hands represents the entire crew of workers).

Comparing Core Phonetic, Grammatical, and Literary Distinctions

Syllabic Weight

Noun Stress vs Verb Stress in Disyllabic Words

Two-syllable nouns place stress on the first syllable (**TEA-cher**, **MO-ther**)[cite: 2], while two-syllable verbs place stress on the second syllable (pro-**TECT**, de-**PEND**)[cite: 2].

Structural Agency

Agentive Passive vs Agentless Passive

Agentive passives state the performer using a "by-phrase" (*The patient was operated on by five surgeons*)[cite: 2], while agentless passives omit the performer entirely because the agent is obvious or unknown (*The window was broken*)[cite: 2].

Direct Address

Personification vs Apostrophe

Personification gives human attributes to non-human things (*The flowers danced*)[cite: 2], while apostrophe directly addresses an absent person or inanimate object as if they were present to hear (*O death, where is thy sting?*)[cite: 2].

Figurative Representation

Metaphor vs Synecdoche

A metaphor equates two fundamentally different things (*Life is a battlefield*)[cite: 2], while synecdoche uses an actual physical part of an entity to represent the entire whole (*All hands on deck*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Stressing the second syllable of two-syllable professional nouns (e.g., pronouncing *DOC-tor* as *doc-TOR*, or *TEA-cher* as *tea-CHER*)[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

In standard English, two-syllable nouns denoting professions and objects are stressed on the first syllable (**DOC-tor**, **TEA-cher**, **TAI-lor**)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Adding an unnecessary "by someone" phrase to every passive construction[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

When the performer is unknown or unimportant, omit the agent to create a concise, natural agentless passive (*The car was stolen*, not *The car was stolen by someone*)[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Including personal letters headers (addresses and salutations like *Dear Sir*) when writing an article for a school magazine[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Magazine articles require an eye-catching title and byline, opening directly with the introductory paragraph without letter formatting[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Confusing apostrophe (the figure of speech) with apostrophe (the punctuation mark)[cite: 1, 2].

Scientific Fact

The punctuation mark `'` indicates omission or possession[cite: 1], whereas the figure of speech *apostrophe* is a poetic direct address to an absent entity[cite: 2].
