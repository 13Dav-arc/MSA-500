Core Concept

What is Democracy?

The word "Democracy" originates from two ancient Greek words: demos (meaning "the people") and kratos (meaning "rule" or "power"). Literally, democracy means "Rule by the People". In 1863, American President Abraham Lincoln delivered the world's most famous and enduring definition of democracy in his Gettysburg Address:

> "Government of the people, by the people, for the people."

In a true democracy, citizens are not subjects of an imperial king or victims of military commanders. Citizens are the sovereign masters who hire leaders through the ballot box and can fire them in the next election if they fail to perform!

Core Features of Democratic Governance

1.
Popular Sovereignty & Consent: Leaders derive their authority strictly from the mandate and votes of the people, not from the barrel of a gun or hereditary royal blood.
2.
Periodic, Free, and Fair Elections: Elections are conducted regularly (e.g., every four years under Nigeria's 1999 Constitution) by an independent umpire (INEC), ensuring secret ballots and transparent vote counting.
3.
Multi-Party Competition: Citizens have genuine choices between competing political parties offering diverse manifestos, economic policies, and candidates.
4.
Majority Rule with Minority Rights: While the candidate with the highest lawful votes wins, the human rights, property, and freedom of the minority voters remain fully protected by the constitution.
5.
Separation of Powers & Checks and Balances: Power is decentralized across the Legislature (National Assembly), the Executive (Presidency), and the Judiciary (Courts) to prevent any one branch from becoming tyrannical.
6.
Rule of Law & Free Press: The constitution is supreme; leaders are accountable to the courts, and independent media holds government officials to account.

Meaning and Importance of Popular Participation

Democracy is not a spectator sport where citizens sit back like football fans eating popcorn. It requires active, continuous Popular Participation:

Electoral Engagement: Registering to vote, voting in elections, and protecting ballot integrity.
Civic Advocacy & Public Debate: Attending local government town halls, writing petitions, and demanding transparency from elected representatives.
Membership in Civil Society: Joining community development associations, professional unions (e.g., NBA, NMA, NUT), and human rights groups.
Constructive Opposition: Challenging bad policies through lawful, peaceful protests and public debate rather than armed rebellion.
