Week 03: Fractions I — Meaning, Types, and Equivalence

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Fractions I — Meaning, Types, and Equivalence for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Suppose your family buys a circular loaf of freshly baked bread and slices it into eight identical pieces. If you eat three slices, what proportion of the entire loaf have you consumed, and what fraction remains for your siblings? In daily life, quantities rarely come in neat, indivisible whole units. A tailor measures two and three-quarter yards of fabric, a carpenter cuts half an inch from a door frame, and an athlete completes four and a half laps of a running track. Fractions are the mathematical tool that allows us to measure, divide, and represent broken quantities and parts of a whole with absolute precision.

Key Terms

Fraction A numerical representation of one or more equal parts of a whole unit or set, written in the form $\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ is the numerator and $b$ is the non-zero denominator.
Numerator The upper number in a fraction that indicates how many equal parts of the whole are being taken or considered.
Denominator The lower number in a fraction that indicates the total number of equal parts into which the whole has been divided ($b \neq 0$).
Proper Fraction A fraction whose numerator is strictly less than its denominator ($a < b$), representing a value less than one whole unit.
Equivalent Fractions Two or more fractions that represent the identical mathematical proportion or value, obtained by multiplying or dividing the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

Core Concept

1. The Anatomy of a Fraction

A fraction represents division: $\frac{a}{b} = a \div b$.

The denominator ($b$) names the fractional unit (e.g., thirds, quarters, eighths). The denominator can never be zero because division by zero is mathematically undefined.
The numerator ($a$) counts the number of those fractional units.
In a unit fraction, the numerator is always $1$ (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{10}$).

2. The Three Major Types of Fractions

1.
Proper Fractions:
The numerator is smaller than the denominator: $\frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} < 1$.
Examples: $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{5}{9}, \frac{11}{15}$.
1.
Improper Fractions:
The numerator is greater than or equal to the denominator: $\frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} \geq 1$.
Examples: $\frac{4}{3}, \frac{7}{4}, \frac{13}{5}, \frac{8}{8}$. Note that $\frac{8}{8} = 1$.
1.
Mixed Numbers (Mixed Fractions):
Consists of a whole number part alongside a proper fraction part.
Examples: $1\frac{1}{2}, 2\frac{3}{4}, 5\frac{2}{3}$.

3. Conversions Between Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions

Converting Mixed Numbers to Improper Fractions:

Multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, and place the result over the original denominator:

$$W\frac{a}{b} = \frac{(W \times b) + a}{b}$$

Example: Convert $3\frac{2}{5}$ to an improper fraction:

$$\frac{(3 \times 5) + 2}{5} = \frac{15 + 2}{5} = \frac{17}{5}$$

Converting Improper Fractions to Mixed Numbers:

Divide the numerator by the denominator using integer division:

$$\frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} = \text{Quotient}\frac{\text{Remainder}}{\text{Denominator}}$$

Example: Convert $\frac{23}{4}$ to a mixed number:

$23 \div 4 = 5$ with a remainder of $3$.

$$\frac{23}{4} = 5\frac{3}{4}$$

4. Equivalent Fractions and Simplification to Lowest Terms

Equivalent Fractions: Multiplying or dividing both the numerator and denominator by the exact same non-zero number produces an equivalent fraction:

$$\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2 \times 2}{3 \times 2} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2 \times 3}{3 \times 3} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = \frac{8}{12}$$

Simplification (Reducing to Lowest Terms):

A fraction is in its lowest terms (simplest form) when its numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1 (i.e., their HCF is 1).

To reduce a fraction in one step, divide both numerator and denominator by their Highest Common Factor (HCF):

Reduce $\frac{36}{48}$: $\text{HCF}(36, 48) = 12$.

$$\frac{36 \div 12}{48 \div 12} = \frac{3}{4}$$

5. Comparing and Ordering Fractions

To arrange fractions in ascending (increasing) or descending (decreasing) order:

1.
Find the Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) of all the denominators.
2.
Convert each fraction into an equivalent fraction having this common LCM denominator.
3.
Compare the resulting numerators directly.
Example: Compare $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{5}{6}$:
$\text{LCM}(4, 6) = 12$.
$\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3 \times 3}{4 \times 3} = \frac{9}{12}$
$\frac{5}{6} = \frac{5 \times 2}{6 \times 2} = \frac{10}{12}$
Since $9 < 10$, $\frac{3}{4} < \frac{5}{6}$.

Comparing Fraction Classes and Structural Conversions

Proper vs Improper

Less than 1 vs Greater than or Equal to 1

Proper fractions have numerators smaller than denominators and represent values less than 1; improper fractions have numerators equal to or greater than denominators and represent values of 1 or more.

Improper vs Mixed

Single Rational Quotient vs Integer with Remainder

Improper fractions express whole plus fractional parts as a single top-heavy fraction (e.g., 7/4); mixed numbers display the whole integer separately alongside the remaining proper fraction (e.g., 1 3/4).

Expansion vs Reduction

Generating Multiples vs Dividing by HCF

Expanding multiplies top and bottom by a common integer to find equal fractions; reducing divides top and bottom by their HCF to reach the simplest canonical form.

Common Denominator vs Unequal Denominator

Direct Comparison vs Conversion via LCM

Fractions with equal denominators are compared directly by their numerators; fractions with unequal denominators require converting to a common denominator using the LCM.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Adding or subtracting the same number to both numerator and denominator to generate equivalent fractions (e.g., thinking $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1+2}{2+2} = \frac{3}{4}$).

Scientific Fact

Equivalent fractions can ONLY be formed by multiplication or division, never by addition or subtraction. $\frac{1}{2} \neq \frac{3}{4}$.

Common Mistake

Believing that a fraction with a larger denominator is always smaller than a fraction with a smaller denominator.

Scientific Fact

This rule applies ONLY to unit fractions where the numerator is 1 (e.g., $\frac{1}{8} < \frac{1}{2}$). When numerators differ, fractions must be converted to a common denominator before comparing.

Common Mistake

Leaving an improper fraction like $\frac{25}{4}$ as the final answer in a practical measurement word problem.

Scientific Fact

In real-life measurements, improper fractions should be converted into mixed numbers ($6\frac{1}{4}$) so the physical quantity can be easily understood.
