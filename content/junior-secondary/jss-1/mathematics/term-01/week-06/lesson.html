Week 06: Decimals I — Meaning, Place Value, and Conversions

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Decimals I — Meaning, Place Value, and Conversions for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When a clinical thermometer measures your body temperature as $37.4^\circ\text{C}$, an Olympic stopwatch clocks a 100-metre sprinter at $9.93\text{ seconds}$, or your local filling station dispenses $15.65\text{ litres}$ of petrol, whole numbers alone are not precise enough. Decimals provide the fine language of exact measurement by extending our familiar base-ten place value system into fractional parts smaller than one whole. By understanding how the decimal point anchors values into tenths, hundredths, and thousandths, you bridge the gap between everyday fractions and scientific precision.

Key Terms

Decimal Fraction A fraction whose denominator is a power of ten ($10, 100, 1000, \dots$), conventionally written in positional notation using a decimal point separating the whole number from the fractional parts.
Decimal Point The period dot (.) placed between the units digit and the tenths digit in a decimal number to indicate the transition from whole numbers to fractional parts.
Tenths The first place value position to the right of the decimal point, representing fractional parts of one divided into ten equal units ($\frac{1}{10} = 0.1$).
Terminating Decimal A decimal number that contains a finite number of digits after the decimal point because division results in a remainder of zero (e.g., $\frac{3}{4} = 0.75$).
Recurring (Repeating) Decimal A decimal fraction in which one or more digits after the decimal point repeat indefinitely without terminating, conventionally marked with a dot or bar over the repeating cycle (e.g., $\frac{1}{3} = 0.333\dots = 0.\dot{3}$).

Core Concept

1. Meaning and Place Value of Decimals

In the decimal number system, every step to the right of the units column divides the value by $10$:

Units ($10^0 = 1$)
[Decimal Point]
Tenths ($10^{-1} = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1$)
Hundredths ($10^{-2} = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01$)
Thousandths ($10^{-3} = \frac{1}{1000} = 0.001$)
Ten-Thousandths ($10^{-4} = \frac{1}{10000} = 0.0001$)

| Tens ($10$) | Units ($1$) | . | Tenths ($\frac{1}{10}$) | Hundredths ($\frac{1}{100}$) | Thousandths ($\frac{1}{1000}$) | Value Breakdown |

| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |

| 4 | 7 | . | 2 | 8 | 5 | $40 + 7 + 0.2 + 0.08 + 0.005$ |

In the number $47.285$:

The digit 2 has a place value of Tenths and a value of $\frac{2}{10} = 0.2$.
The digit 8 has a place value of Hundredths and a value of $\frac{8}{100} = 0.08$.
The digit 5 has a place value of Thousandths and a value of $\frac{5}{1000} = 0.005$.

2. Converting Decimals to Common Fractions

To convert a terminating decimal into a common fraction:

1.
Count the number of decimal places (digits to the right of the decimal point).
2.
Write the digits as the numerator over a power of 10 having that exact number of zeros.
3.
Divide numerator and denominator by their Highest Common Factor (HCF) to reduce to lowest terms.
Examples:
$0.6 = \frac{6}{10} = \frac{6 \div 2}{10 \div 2} = \frac{3}{5}$
$0.75 = \frac{75}{100} = \frac{75 \div 25}{100 \div 25} = \frac{3}{4}$
$0.125 = \frac{125}{1000} = \frac{125 \div 125}{1000 \div 125} = \frac{1}{8}$
$3.45 = 3\frac{45}{100} = 3\frac{45 \div 5}{100 \div 5} = 3\frac{9}{20}$
Decimal Place Value Architecture and Fraction-to-Decimal Conversion Systems

3. Converting Common Fractions to Decimals

Method 1: Equivalent Fractions with Powers of 10 Denominators

If the denominator can be multiplied easily to make $10, 100,$ or $1000$:

$\frac{3}{5} = \frac{3 \times 2}{5 \times 2} = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6$
$\frac{7}{20} = \frac{7 \times 5}{20 \times 5} = \frac{35}{100} = 0.35$
$\frac{13}{250} = \frac{13 \times 4}{250 \times 4} = \frac{52}{1000} = 0.052$
Method 2: Long Division (Numerator Divided by Denominator)

Divide the numerator by the denominator, placing a decimal point and adding trailing zeros:

Convert $\frac{3}{8}$ to decimal:

$$3 \div 8 = 0.375$$

4. Terminating vs. Recurring Decimals

Terminating Decimals: The division terminates with a remainder of zero. A fraction in lowest terms produces a terminating decimal if and only if the prime factors of its denominator contain ONLY $2$ and/or $5$ (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{5}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{10}, \frac{1}{16}, \frac{1}{20}, \frac{1}{25}$).
Recurring (Repeating) Decimals: The division never ends; a digit or pattern of digits repeats infinitely. This occurs whenever the reduced denominator contains prime factors other than 2 or 5 (e.g., $3, 7, 11, 13$):
$\frac{1}{3} = 0.3333\dots = 0.\dot{3}$
$\frac{2}{3} = 0.6666\dots = 0.\dot{6}$
$\frac{1}{6} = 0.1666\dots = 0.1\dot{6}$
$\frac{1}{7} = 0.142857142857\dots = 0.\overline{142857}$

5. Comparing and Ordering Decimals

To compare decimals:

1.
Compare the whole number parts first.
2.
If whole numbers are equal, align decimal points and compare tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths.
3.
Adding trailing zeros to the right does not change a decimal's value: $0.4 = 0.40 = 0.400$.
Example: Compare $0.385$ and $0.4$:

$0.4 = 0.400$. Looking at tenths, $4 > 3$, so $0.4 > 0.385$.

Comparing Decimal Classes and Numerical Forms

Terminating vs Recurring

Finite Decimal vs Infinite Repeating Cycle

Terminating decimals end cleanly because denominators have only prime factors of 2 and 5; recurring decimals repeat infinitely because denominators contain other primes like 3 or 7.

Tenths vs Hundredths

First Decimal Place vs Second Decimal Place

Tenths represent parts out of 10 (0.1) and hold ten times the value of hundredths, which represent parts out of 100 (0.01).

Decimal Fraction vs Common Fraction

Positional Notation vs Top-Over-Bottom Form

Decimal fractions express tenths, hundredths, and thousandths using a decimal point; common fractions express parts using explicit numerators over denominators (e.g., 0.5 vs 1/2).

Trailing Zero vs Leading Zero

Insignificant Suffix vs Significant Placeholder

Adding trailing zeros after the last decimal digit does not change value ($0.5 = 0.50$); adding leading zeros after the decimal point divides value by 10 ($0.5 \neq 0.05$).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that a decimal with more digits is always larger (e.g., thinking $0.185$ is larger than $0.4$ because $185 > 4$).

Scientific Fact

Decimals must be compared by place value from left to right. Comparing tenths shows that $0.400$ ($4$ tenths) is much larger than $0.185$ ($1$ tenth).

Common Mistake

Misplacing the decimal point when converting fractions like $\frac{7}{100}$ into decimals, writing $0.7$ instead of $0.07$.

Scientific Fact

The denominator 100 has two zeros, requiring two decimal places: $\frac{7}{100} = 0.07$.

Common Mistake

Claiming that $\frac{1}{3} = 0.3$ exactly.

Scientific Fact

$0.3 = \frac{3}{10}$. $\frac{1}{3}$ is a recurring decimal equal to $0.3333\dots$ or $0.\dot{3}$, never an exact single-digit decimal.
