Week 08: Statistics & Averages — Data Presentation and Central Tendency

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Statistics & Averages — Data Presentation and Central Tendency for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When the Nigerian government plans how many hospital beds, schools, or roads to build in the coming fiscal year, or when a shopkeeper decides how many loaves of bread to order each morning, they do not rely on wild guesses. They gather numbers, arrange them into tables and charts, and calculate typical summary figures. This science of collecting, organizing, presenting, and analyzing data is called statistics, and its central averages—the mean, median, and mode—help us make sense of the world.

Key Terms

Statistics The branch of mathematics concerned with the collection, organization, presentation, analysis, and interpretation of numerical data for decision-making and planning[cite: 84, 98].
Data Raw, unorganized information (such as facts, numbers, or observations) gathered from counts or measurements[cite: 84, 88].
Frequency The number of times a particular data value or event occurs within a dataset[cite: 89, 98].
Mean (Arithmetic Average) The sum of all numerical values in a dataset divided by the total number of values[cite: 101, 107].
Median The middle value in a set of data after the values have been arranged in ascending or descending order of magnitude[cite: 102, 107].
Mode The particular value or category that appears most frequently within a given dataset[cite: 103, 107].

Core Concept

Purpose of Statistics and Data Collection

Statistics provides essential quantitative evidence used in everyday life, industry, and national governance:

1.
Record Keeping: Storing systematic historical information.
2.
Planning: Aiding economic planning, such as using daily petroleum export data to project national budgets or tracking school enrolment to plan classroom expansions.
3.
Forecasting and Prediction: Projecting future outcomes and community needs based on past trends.
4.
Decision-Making: Guiding shopkeepers, doctors, and institutions to select the best actions based on real-world evidence.

Organizing and Presenting Data

Rank-Ordered List: Arranging raw numerical scores or categories in sequential order from lowest to highest or highest to lowest.
Frequency Table with Tally Marks: A structured grid showing values and the number of times they occur. Tally marks are bundled in groups of five (four vertical strokes crossed by a diagonal stroke) to avoid miscounting.
Pictogram: A visual presentation where standardized pictures or symbols represent specific quantities of items.
Bar Chart: A graphical representation where the heights or lengths of separate, rectangular bars of equal width correspond to the frequencies of each category.
Pie Chart: A circular statistical graphic where a full circle ($360^\circ$) represents the total dataset, and the angle of each sector is proportional to its fraction of the whole:

$$\text{Sector Angle} = \frac{\text{Frequency of Item}}{\text{Total Frequency}} \times 360^\circ$$

Methods of Presenting Statistical Data: Frequency Tables, Bar Charts, and Pie Charts

Measures of Central Tendency (Averages)

Averages summarize an entire group of figures into a single representative benchmark:

1.
Mean ($\bar{x}$): The arithmetic average of all entries:
For an ungrouped list of $n$ numbers:

$$\text{Mean} = \frac{\text{Sum of all values}}{n}$$

For a tabulated frequency distribution:

$$\text{Mean} = \frac{\sum fx}{\sum f}$$

1.
Median: The value occupying the exact centre position when data is ordered by size:
When the count $n$ is odd, the median is the single middle number.
When the count $n$ is even, two numbers share the middle; their sum is divided by 2 to determine the median:

$$\text{Median} = \frac{\text{First middle value} + \text{Second middle value}}{2}$$

1.
Mode: The most frequent score. A distribution with one most frequent value is unimodal; if two values share the highest frequency, the distribution is bimodal.

Comparing Measures of Central Tendency

Mathematical Sensitivity

Mean vs Median

The mean incorporates the exact numerical value of every score and is heavily pulled by extreme outliers[cite: 101, 103], while the median is positional and unaffected by extreme scores[cite: 102, 106, 107].

Categorical Suitability

Mode vs Mean

The mode is the only measure of central tendency applicable to non-numerical, categorical data (such as finding the most popular shoe size or vehicle type)[cite: 90, 92, 104], whereas the mean requires numerical values for calculation[cite: 101].

Uniqueness of Value

Median vs Mode

A dataset always possesses exactly one unique median[cite: 102], whereas a dataset can have one mode, multiple modes (bimodal/multimodal), or no mode at all if all values occur equally[cite: 104, 106, 107].

Graphic Visualisation

Bar Chart vs Pie Chart

Bar charts represent discrete categorical frequencies through bar height separated by spaces[cite: 91, 98], while pie charts display proportional parts of an entire whole using angular sectors of $360^\circ$[cite: 91, 92, 98].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Finding the median without arranging the numbers in order of size first[cite: 102].

Scientific Fact

You must arrange the raw data into an ascending or descending rank-ordered list before identifying the middle number[cite: 89, 102].

Common Mistake

Stating that a dataset has a mode of 0 when no numbers repeat[cite: 107].

Scientific Fact

If every value appears with equal frequency, the dataset has "no mode"; stating "mode = 0" implies that the number zero occurs most often[cite: 107].

Common Mistake

Dividing the sum of values by 2 instead of by the total number of items ($n$) when calculating the mean[cite: 101].

Scientific Fact

You divide by 2 only when averaging the two middle numbers for the median of an even set; the mean must be divided by $n$ (the total count)[cite: 101, 102].
