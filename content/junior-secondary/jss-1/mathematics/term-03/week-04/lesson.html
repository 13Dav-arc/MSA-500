Week 04: Three-Dimensional Shapes (Solids) — Properties, Nets, and Volume

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Three-Dimensional Shapes (Solids) — Properties, Nets, and Volume for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever unpacked a brand-new pair of shoes, unfolded a cardboard carton into a flat sheet, or wondered how much water a rectangular overhead tank can store? Unlike flat paper drawings, real-world objects like tins of milk, dice, chalk boxes, and footballs occupy physical space and possess length, width, and height. These solid figures are called three-dimensional (3D) shapes, and understanding their properties, flat patterns (nets), and volumes is the basis of manufacturing, packaging, and structural architecture.

Key Terms

Three-Dimensional (3D) Solid Any geometric figure that occupies physical space and has three measurable dimensions: length, width (breadth), and height (depth)[cite: 41, 48, 49].
Face Any flat or curved surface that forms part of the exterior boundary of a solid geometric shape[cite: 42, 43].
Edge The straight or curved line segment formed where two faces of a solid meet[cite: 42, 43].
Vertex A corner point where three or more edges intersect in a solid figure (plural: vertices)[cite: 42].
Net of a Solid A flat, two-dimensional pattern of joined polygons that can be folded along its edges to form a three-dimensional closed solid[cite: 46].

Core Concept

Identification and Properties of Prisms

A solid figure is anything that occupies space and has a definite shape. Geometric solids with uniform cross-sections along their length are called prisms:

A prism has identical, parallel base and top faces, with side faces whose opposite edges are equal and parallel.
For any prism with an $n$-sided base, it has $n + 2$ faces, $3n$ edges, and $2n$ vertices.

Common Prisms:

1.
Cube: A special regular cuboid where all edges are equal in length. It has $6$ congruent square faces, $12$ edges, and $8$ vertices. Examples include dice, sugar cubes, and seasoning cubes.
2.
Cuboid (Rectangular Prism): A solid bounded by $6$ rectangular faces where opposite faces are identical and parallel. It has $12$ edges and $8$ vertices. Examples include brick blocks, books, desks, and matchboxes.
3.
Cylinder: A circular prism with two parallel circular flat faces and one curved surface connecting them. It has $3$ faces, $2$ circular edges, and $0$ vertices. Examples include milk tins and water drums.

Properties of Pyramids, Cones, and Spheres

Pyramid: A solid figure whose base is a polygon and whose triangular lateral faces converge to a single common point called the apex or vertex. A pyramid is named after the shape of its base (e.g., a square-based pyramid has $5$ faces, $8$ edges, and $5$ vertices; a triangular-based pyramid or tetrahedron has $4$ faces, $6$ edges, and $4$ vertices).
Cone: A solid with a circular flat base and a curved lateral surface that tapers to a single pointed vertex (apex). A cone has $2$ faces (one flat circular, one curved), $1$ circular edge, and $1$ vertex. Examples include sharpened pencil tips, funnels, and ice-cream cones.
Sphere: A perfectly round three-dimensional solid in which every point on its surface is equidistant from its fixed centre. A sphere has $1$ continuous curved surface, $0$ edges, and $0$ vertices. Examples include tennis balls and globe models.
Faces, Edges, and Vertices of Common Three-Dimensional Geometrical Solids

Nets of Solids

The net of a solid is a flat 2D layout that folds into the 3D shape:

Cube Net: Consists of $6$ congruent squares arranged so that, when folded, opposite faces meet without overlapping.
Cuboid Net: Made of $3$ matching pairs of rectangles representing the top/base, front/back, and two side faces.
Cylinder Net: Formed by one central rectangle (which rolls into the curved body of height $h$ and length $2\pi r$) flanked by two identical circular bases.
Cone Net: Consists of a sector of a circle (curved slant surface) attached to one full circular base.
Pyramid Net: A central polygonal base surrounded by hinged triangular faces.

Volume of Cubes and Cuboids

Volume is the measure of internal three-dimensional space occupied by a solid body, measured in cubic units such as $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$.

Volume of a Cube: If the edge of a cube is $s$:

$$\text{Volume} = \text{side} \times \text{side} \times \text{side} = s^3$$

$$\text{Edge length } (s) = \sqrt[3]{\text{Volume}}$$

Volume of a Cuboid: Given length ($l$), width/breadth ($b$), and height ($h$):

$$\text{Volume} = l \times b \times h = \text{Base Area} \times \text{height}$$

$$\text{Height } (h) = \frac{\text{Volume}}{l \times b} = \frac{\text{Volume}}{\text{Base Area}}$$

Structural Properties of Core Three-Dimensional Solids

Prisms vs Pyramids

Uniform Cross-Section vs Converging Apex

Prisms have two congruent parallel bases connected by rectangular lateral faces[cite: 41, 48], whereas pyramids have a single polygonal base whose triangular faces taper upward to meet at a single apex[cite: 44, 48].

Vertex Count

Cylinder vs Cone

A cylinder has two circular edges and zero vertices[cite: 43], while a cone tapers to a single pointed vertex at its top[cite: 44].

Face Geometry

Cube vs Cuboid

A cube is bounded by 6 identical square faces with all edges equal[cite: 42], while a cuboid is bounded by 6 rectangular faces where opposite faces are identical in pairs[cite: 42].

Curvature

Polyhedra vs Curved Solids

Cubes and cuboids have completely flat planar faces[cite: 42], whereas cylinders, cones, and spheres incorporate smooth curved surfaces[cite: 43, 44, 45].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Stating that a cylinder has vertices where the circular faces meet the curved body[cite: 43].

Scientific Fact

A vertex requires three or more straight intersecting edges; a cylinder has 2 circular edges and 0 vertices[cite: 42, 43].

Common Mistake

Using square units ($\text{cm}^2$) instead of cubic units ($\text{cm}^3$) when stating volume[cite: 49].

Scientific Fact

Area measures two-dimensional flat surface in $\text{cm}^2$, while volume measures three-dimensional space in $\text{cm}^3$ or $\text{m}^3$[cite: 31, 49].

Common Mistake

Confusing the slant height of a cone or pyramid with its vertical perpendicular height in calculations[cite: 46, 56].

Scientific Fact

The perpendicular height is the vertical distance measured at $90^\circ$ from the base to the apex, whereas the slant height runs along the inclined outer slope[cite: 46, 56].
