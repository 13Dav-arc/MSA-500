Week 03: Area of Plane Figures — Circles, Triangles, Quadrilaterals, and Composite Shapes

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Area of Plane Figures — Circles, Triangles, Quadrilaterals, and Composite Shapes for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

If you want to tile a classroom floor, lay fresh carpet in a living room, or fertilize an agricultural plot, knowing the distance around the boundary fence is not enough. You need to measure the total amount of flat surface space enclosed inside the boundaries. That internal surface measurement is known as area, and calculating it accurately ensures that builders, farmers, and decorators purchase the exact amount of materials without expensive waste.

Key Terms

Area The measure of surface space enclosed within the boundary lines of a closed two-dimensional plane figure, expressed in square units such as $\text{cm}^2$ or $\text{m}^2$[cite: 31, 34].
Altitude (Height) The perpendicular distance measured at right angles ($90^\circ$) from the base of a geometric figure to its opposite vertex or parallel side[cite: 30, 33].
Radius The straight-line distance from the centre of a circle to its outer circumference, used in calculating circular area via $\pi r^2$[cite: 8, 29].
Composite Shape A complex or irregular plane figure formed by combining two or more basic geometric shapes, such as squares, rectangles, and triangles[cite: 34, 35].

Core Concept

Area of Circles

The area of a circle represents the measure of flat surface bounded by its circumference. It is derived using the constant ratio $\pi$ ($\approx \frac{22}{7}$ or $3.142$) and the radius $r$:

$$\text{Area of a Circle} = \pi r^2$$

Because the diameter $d$ is twice the radius ($d = 2r \implies r = \frac{d}{2}$), the formula can also be expressed as $\pi \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$.

When given the area of a circle, the radius is calculated by solving for $r$: $r = \sqrt{\frac{\text{Area}}{\pi}}$.
For example, if a circular flowerbed has a radius of $3.5\text{ m}$ ($\frac{7}{2}\text{ m}$), its area is $\frac{22}{7} \times \frac{7}{2} \times \frac{7}{2} = 38.5\text{ m}^2$.

Area of Triangles

Any rectangle can be divided along its diagonal into two congruent right-angled triangles. Therefore, the area of any triangle is half the area of a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height:

$$\text{Area of a Triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{b \times h}{2}$$

In a right-angled triangle, the base and perpendicular height are the two sides containing the $90^\circ$ angle.
When the area and base are known, the height is found by rearranging the formula: $h = \frac{2 \times \text{Area}}{\text{base}}$.
Formulas for Calculating the Area of Common Regular Plane Figures

Area of Quadrilaterals

1.
Square: All four sides are equal ($L$).

$$\text{Area of a Square} = L \times L = L^2 \quad \implies \quad \text{Length of side } (L) = \sqrt{\text{Area}}$$

1.
Rectangle: Formed by length ($l$) and breadth ($b$).

$$\text{Area of a Rectangle} = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} = l \times b$$

$$\text{Length} = \frac{\text{Area}}{\text{breadth}}, \quad \text{Breadth} = \frac{\text{Area}}{\text{length}}$$

1.
Parallelogram: A slanted quadrilateral where opposite sides are parallel. Its area equals the product of its base ($b$) and its perpendicular height ($h$):

$$\text{Area of a Parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{height} = b \times h$$

1.
Trapezium: A quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides ($a$ and $b$) separated by perpendicular height $h$. A diagonal splits the trapezium into two triangles of height $h$, giving:

$$\text{Area of a Trapezium} = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h$$

Area of Composite and Irregular Shapes

Irregular rectilinear shapes cannot be solved with a single standard formula. To determine their area:

1.
Divide the complex shape into familiar regular shapes (such as squares, rectangles, or triangles) by drawing dotted construction lines.
2.
Calculate the area of each individual sub-region using the appropriate formula.
3.
Sum the areas of all sub-regions to find the total composite area.

Comparing Formulas and Properties Across Plane Figures

Dimensional Dependency

Parallelogram vs Rectangle

Both multiply base by height ($b \times h$), but a rectangle uses its side length as height, whereas a parallelogram requires an internal perpendicular height measured at $90^\circ$ to the base[cite: 32, 33].

Fractional Multiplier

Triangle vs Parallelogram

A triangle is half of a parallelogram on the same base and height, meaning its area includes a half factor ($\frac{1}{2} b h$) while the parallelogram is simply $b \times h$[cite: 30, 33].

Boundary Curvature

Circle vs Square

A circle relies on the ratio $\pi$ multiplied by radius squared ($\pi r^2$)[cite: 29, 30], whereas a square squares its straight linear side ($L^2$)[cite: 31].

Parallel Base Summation

Trapezium vs Parallelogram

A parallelogram has equal opposite bases ($b \times h$)[cite: 13, 33], while a trapezium averages two unequal parallel bases before multiplying by height ($\frac{a + b}{2} \times h$)[cite: 12, 33].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using the slant side instead of the perpendicular height when calculating the area of a parallelogram or triangle[cite: 33].

Scientific Fact

Area calculations require the perpendicular height ($90^\circ$ altitude) dropped directly to the base line, never the slanted edge[cite: 30, 33].

Common Mistake

Confusing the formula for circumference ($2\pi r$) with the formula for area ($\pi r^2$)[cite: 17, 29].

Scientific Fact

Circumference is a one-dimensional linear measure ($2\pi r$ in $\text{cm}$), while area measures two-dimensional surface space ($\pi r^2$ in $\text{cm}^2$)[cite: 17, 29].

Common Mistake

Forgetting to square the radius in the circle area formula (e.g., computing $\pi \times r \times 2$).

Scientific Fact

$r^2$ means multiplying the radius by itself ($r \times r$), not multiplying by 2[cite: 29, 30].
