Week 06: Geometric Constructions — Lines and Angles

Foundational curriculum module in Mathematics covering Geometric Constructions — Lines and Angles for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever wondered how architects and civil engineers ensure that the walls of a skyscraper stand perfectly upright at $90^\circ$ without leaning, or how dual-carriage highways maintain the exact same distance apart for hundreds of kilometres? In technical drawing and architecture, guessing or drawing freehand is not allowed. Geometric construction teaches you how to create razor-sharp lines, exact right angles, and balanced bisectors using only a pencil, a straightedge ruler, and a pair of compasses.

Key Terms

Geometric Construction The accurate drawing of geometric figures, lines, and angles using only specialized drawing instruments such as a ruler, pencil, set-square, and compasses[cite: 57].
Parallel Lines Straight lines in the same plane that never meet or intersect, no matter how far they are extended, remaining equidistant from each other[cite: 57].
Perpendicular Lines Two intersecting straight lines that meet or cross each other at an exact right angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 60].
Bisection The geometric process of dividing a line segment or an angle into two exactly equal halves[cite: 62].
Perpendicular Bisector A straight line that cuts across a given line segment at its exact midpoint at an angle of $90^\circ$[cite: 62, 63].

Core Concept

Instruments and Principles of Construction

To construct a figure in geometry implies drawing it accurately using standard measuring and drawing instruments: ruler, pencil, set-squares, and compasses. Before beginning any construction, it is good practice to make a rough sketch of the expected figure.

Construction of Parallel Lines

Parallel lines lie in the same direction and remain the same distance apart without meeting. There are two primary methods to construct parallel lines:

1.
Using a Ruler and Set-Square (Sliding Method): Align one edge of a set-square along the given line $MN$. Place a straight ruler firmly along another edge of the set-square. Slide the set-square along the ruler until its edge reaches the desired point $O$, then draw the line with a sharp pencil.
2.
Using a Ruler and Compasses: Choose two points $A$ and $B$ on line $MN$. With the compass opened to radius $AB$, place the compass point at $O$ and draw an arc. With the compass opened to radius $AO$, place the point at $B$ and draw an arc intersecting the first arc at $P$. Draw a line through $O$ and $P$; line $OP$ is parallel to $MN$.
Methods for Constructing Parallel Lines

Construction of Perpendicular Lines

Two lines are perpendicular when they intersect at $90^\circ$.

From a Point on a Line: Given a point $B$ on line $AC$, place the compass point at $B$ and strike arcs on both sides to cut $AC$ at points $P$ and $Q$. With points $P$ and $Q$ as centres and equal radii greater than half $PQ$, strike intersecting arcs above the line at point $R$. Draw a line through $B$ and $R$; line $BR$ is perpendicular to $AC$ ($R\hat{B}A = R\hat{B}C = 90^\circ$).
From an External Point: Given a point $O$ outside line $XY$, place the compass point at $O$ and strike an arc cutting line $XY$ at two distinct points. From these two intersection points, strike intersecting arcs on the opposite side of the line. Draw a straight line from $O$ through this intersection to meet $XY$ at $90^\circ$.

Bisection of a Line Segment

A line segment $AB$ is the straight path between points $A$ and $B$. To bisect $AB$ into two equal parts:

1.
Open the compass to a radius greater than half the length of $AB$.
2.
Place the sharp compass point at $A$ and strike two arcs, one above and one below the line segment.
3.
Without changing the radius, place the compass point at $B$ and strike arcs intersecting the first two arcs at points $P$ and $Q$.
4.
Draw a straight line joining $P$ and $Q$. The line $PQ$ cuts $AB$ at its exact midpoint $M$ at right angles ($90^\circ$), forming the perpendicular bisector.

Construction of Special Angles ($90^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $30^\circ$)

Angle $90^\circ$: Constructed by erecting a perpendicular line on a straight line at a given point using compass arcs.
Angle $60^\circ$: With the compass point on the vertex $B$ of a straight line $BC$, draw a wide arc cutting $BC$ at point $X$. Without altering the compass radius, place the point at $X$ and strike an arc cutting the first arc at point $A$. Draw a straight line through $B$ and $A$. The angle $A\hat{B}C = 60^\circ$ (based on the properties of an equilateral triangle).
Angle $45^\circ$: Formed by constructing a $90^\circ$ right angle and bisecting it with compass arcs ($90^\circ \div 2 = 45^\circ$).
Angle $30^\circ$: Formed by constructing a $60^\circ$ angle and bisecting it with compass arcs ($60^\circ \div 2 = 30^\circ$).

Comparing Construction Methods and Geometric Angles

Instrument Requirements

Ruler & Set-Square vs Ruler & Compasses

A ruler and set-square rely on sliding along a straight reference edge to create parallel and perpendicular lines[cite: 58, 61], whereas compasses construct lines and angles using intersecting circular arcs of fixed radii[cite: 59, 63].

Angle Origin

Construction of $60^\circ$ vs $90^\circ$

An angle of $60^\circ$ is constructed from an equilateral triangle framework by preserving the initial arc radius[cite: 63, 64], while $90^\circ$ is constructed by erecting a perpendicular bisector across equidistant points on a straight line[cite: 63].

Bisected Offspring

Angle $30^\circ$ vs Angle $45^\circ$

An angle of $30^\circ$ is obtained by bisecting a $60^\circ$ angle[cite: 64, 67], whereas an angle of $45^\circ$ is obtained by bisecting a $90^\circ$ right angle[cite: 64, 65, 67].

Line Function

Line Bisection vs Perpendicular Line

Line bisection must pass through the exact midpoint of a segment[cite: 62], whereas a perpendicular line can meet a line at any specified point or from an external point at $90^\circ$[cite: 60, 61, 62].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Changing the compass radius between striking the first and second arcs when bisecting a line segment[cite: 62].

Scientific Fact

The compass radius must remain constant from both endpoints; changing the radius displaces the intersection and ruins the midpoint[cite: 62].

Common Mistake

Setting the compass radius to less than half the length of the line segment during bisection[cite: 62].

Scientific Fact

If the radius is less than half the length of the line segment, the arcs will never meet or cross each other[cite: 62].

Common Mistake

Using a dull, blunt pencil lead during geometric construction[cite: 58].

Scientific Fact

Thick pencil lines create measurement errors of several millimetres or degrees; constructions require a sharply pointed pencil[cite: 58].
