Week 02: Living Things (II) — Plant and Animal Diversity

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Living Things (II) — Plant and Animal Diversity for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever looked closely at a maize leaf and noticed how its lines run straight from top to bottom, while a bean leaf looks like a mini spiderweb? Or wondered why an earthworm wriggles smoothly through garden soil without any bones, while a tilapia fish has a stiff, sharp backbone? Nature is full of incredible variety. In this lesson, we will discover how scientists sort plants into monocots and dicots, and organize animals into vertebrates and invertebrates.

Key Terms

Vertebrate An animal that possesses an internal backbone or spinal column.
Invertebrate An animal that does not have an internal backbone or bony skeleton.
Monocotyledon (Monocot) A flowering plant whose seed contains only one seed-leaf (cotyledon).
Dicotyledon (Dicot) A flowering plant whose seed contains two seed-leaves (cotyledons).
Diversity The wide variety of different plant and animal species living within an environment.

Core Concept

How We Classify Plants

Plants make up the green world around us. Scientists divide all plants into two major groups:

1.
Flowering Plants (Angiosperms): These plants produce flowers, which later develop into fruits containing seeds. Examples include mango trees, maize, cowpea (beans), tomatoes, and hibiscus.
2.
Non-Flowering Plants: These plants never produce flowers. Instead of seeds inside fruits, many reproduce using microscopic dust-like grains called spores, or bear exposed seeds in cones. Examples include ferns, mosses, and pine trees.

Monocots vs. Dicots: The Two Families of Flowering Plants

Flowering plants are divided into two main classes based on how their seeds, leaves, and roots are built:

Monocotyledons (Monocots):
Seeds: Have only one cotyledon (seed-leaf) that cannot be split into two halves (e.g., maize, rice, guinea corn).
Leaves: Have long, narrow leaves with parallel venation (veins run straight side by side).
Roots: Have a fibrous root system (a bushy cluster of roots of equal thickness branching from the base of the stem).
Local Examples: Maize, oil palm, coconut, sugarcane, and common yard grasses.
Dicotyledons (Dicots):
Seeds: Have two cotyledons that easily split into two equal halves (e.g., beans, groundnuts).
Leaves: Have broad leaves with net-like (reticulate) venation (veins branch out like a net).
Roots: Have a taproot system (one thick main central root growing straight down, with smaller lateral roots branching off).
Local Examples: Cowpea (beans), groundnut, mango, orange, and pepper plants.

How We Classify Animals

The animal kingdom is divided into two primary divisions based on whether or not they have a backbone:

#### 1. Invertebrates (Animals Without Backbones)

Invertebrates make up over 90% of all animal species on Earth. Many have soft bodies, while others have a hard outer shell or protective armor called an exoskeleton.

Insects & Arthropods: Animals with jointed legs and segmented bodies (e.g., grasshoppers, houseflies, mosquitoes, spiders, crabs, millipedes).
Molluscs: Soft-bodied animals, often protected by hard spiral shells (e.g., land snails, periwinkles, octopuses).
Annelids (Segmented Worms): Animals with soft, ringed bodies and no legs (e.g., earthworms, leeches).
Nematodes & Flatworms: Simple parasitic or free-living worms (e.g., tapeworms, roundworms).

#### 2. Vertebrates (Animals With Backbones)

Vertebrates have an internal bony skeleton with a central spine running along their back. They are divided into five distinct classes:

1.
Pisces (Fish): Cold-blooded aquatic animals covered in slimy scales, swimming with fins and breathing through gills (e.g., tilapia, catfish, shark).
2.
Amphibia (Amphibians): Cold-blooded creatures with moist, scaleless skin. They lay jelly-like eggs in water, where their young live as tadpoles, while adults can live on land (e.g., toads, frogs).
3.
Reptilia (Reptiles): Cold-blooded animals with dry skin covered in waterproof horny scales. They breathe with lungs and lay tough, leathery eggs on land (e.g., lizards, snakes, crocodiles, tortoises).
4.
Aves (Birds): Warm-blooded animals covered in feathers. They have wings, toothless beaks, hollow bones, and lay hard-shelled eggs (e.g., domestic fowl, hawks, eagles, pigeons).
5.
Mammalia (Mammals): Warm-blooded animals with hair or fur on their skin. Females possess mammary glands that produce milk to nurse their young, and most give birth to live babies rather than laying eggs (e.g., goats, cattle, dogs, bats, humans).

Comparing Monocots vs. Dicots and Vertebrates vs. Invertebrates

Seed Leaf Structure

One Cotyledon vs Two Cotyledons

Monocots sprout with a single seed leaf (e.g., maize grain), whereas dicots store food in two cotyledon halves that open like a book (e.g., cowpea seed).

Root & Leaf Patterns

Parallel & Fibrous vs Net-veined & Taproot

Monocots have parallel leaf veins and clusters of fibrous roots, while dicots display net-like leaf veins and a deep central taproot.

Skeletal Architecture

Exoskeleton/Soft Body vs Bony Backbone

Invertebrates lack an internal spine and rely on shells or exoskeletons (e.g., snails, beetles), whereas vertebrates possess an internal vertebral column (e.g., goats, fish).

Body Temperature Control

Cold-Blooded vs Warm-Blooded Animals

Fish, amphibians, and reptiles rely on external sunshine to warm their bodies (cold-blooded), whereas birds and mammals maintain a steady internal body temperature (warm-blooded).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that bats are birds because they have wings and fly in the sky.

Scientific Fact

Bats are mammals. They have fur instead of feathers, give birth to live babies, and produce milk to feed their young.

Common Mistake

Thinking that all small animals are invertebrates and all big animals are vertebrates.

Scientific Fact

Size does not determine classification. Giant squids can grow larger than a human but are invertebrates; tiny tree frogs and minnow fish are small, yet they are vertebrates because they possess a backbone.

Common Mistake

Assuming all plant seeds can be easily split into two equal halves.

Scientific Fact

Only dicot seeds (like beans and peanuts) split into two halves; monocot seeds (like maize and rice grains) are solid single units that do not divide into two cotyledons.

Worked Examples

Example A: Identifying a Seedling in the School Farm

Context: A student pulls up a weed in the school garden and notices long narrow leaves with straight parallel veins, plus a bushy cluster of thin roots.

Problem: The student is unsure whether this weed is a monocot or a dicot.

Fix: Because the leaf veins run parallel side by side and the root system is fibrous without a main central taproot, the plant is classified as a monocotyledon.

Example B: Comparing an Earthworm to a Garden Snake

Context: After heavy rain, a student observes an earthworm and a small snake crawling on wet grass. Both look long, thin, and legless.

Problem: The student assumes both creatures belong to the exact same animal group.

Fix: An earthworm is an invertebrate (annelid) with a soft, boneless body. A snake is a vertebrate (reptile) with an internal backbone, ribs, and dry protective scales.

Example C: Classifying a Domestic Fowl

Context: A student watches a domestic chicken feeding on the compound and needs to place it into its correct vertebrate class.

Problem: Determining the proper biological class for the chicken based on visible features.

Fix: The chicken has feathers, wings, a beak without teeth, and lays hard-shelled eggs. These specific traits place it in the class Aves (Birds).
