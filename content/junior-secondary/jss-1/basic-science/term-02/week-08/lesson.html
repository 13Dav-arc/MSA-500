Week 08: Pure and Impure Substances (II) — Separation Techniques

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Pure and Impure Substances (II) — Separation Techniques for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever watched someone prepare akamu (pap) by squeezing ground corn paste through a fine white cloth, or seen your mother gently pour off excess water from boiled rice without letting a single grain fall into the sink? These everyday kitchen habits are real scientific separation techniques! Because substances in a mixture are only physically combined and not chemically bonded, we can easily separate them using smart physical methods.

Key Terms

Filtration A method used to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid using a porous barrier (such as filter paper) that traps the solid.
Evaporation A method used to recover a dissolved solid from a liquid solution by heating the mixture until the liquid boils away as vapour.
Decantation The careful pouring off of a clear top liquid after heavier solid particles have settled at the bottom of the container.
Distillation A process of separating a pure liquid from a solution by boiling the liquid into vapour and then cooling it back into liquid in a condenser.
Magnetic Separation A technique that uses a magnet to attract and remove magnetic materials (like iron) from non-magnetic substances.

Core Concept

Why Can Mixtures Be Separated?

In a mixture, different substances are blended together physically without forming chemical bonds. Each substance keeps its original physical properties, such as particle size, density, solubility, boiling point, and magnetic attraction. Scientists take advantage of these differences to separate mixtures cleanly.

Common Everyday Separation Methods

#### 1. Decantation

When insoluble solid particles are suspended in a liquid, you can leave the container still until the heavy particles settle to the bottom by gravity (sedimentation). You then gently pour off the clear liquid on top into another cup.

Everyday Example: Pouring water off washed rice grains or soaked beans.

#### 2. Filtration

Filtration is a more accurate way to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid using a filter paper placed inside a funnel:

Residue: The solid particles that are too large to pass through the tiny pores of the filter paper and remain trapped behind.
Filtrate: The clear liquid that passes freely through the pores into the collection beaker below.
Everyday Example: Squeezing ground maize through a fine cloth to make smooth pap (akamu/ogi), or using a tea strainer.

#### 3. Evaporation to Dryness

If a solid has completely dissolved in a liquid to form a clear solution (like salt in water), it will pass right through filter paper. To recover the solid, heat the solution in an evaporating dish until all the liquid evaporates into the air as steam, leaving behind dry salt crystals.

Everyday Example: Producing sea salt along coastal shores by letting ocean water dry under strong sunlight.

#### 4. Simple Distillation

If you want to recover both the dissolved solid and the liquid from a solution, evaporation is not enough because the steam escapes. In simple distillation, the solution is boiled in a flask:

The liquid turns into steam and enters a cold glass tube called a Liebig condenser.
Cold water circulating around the condenser cools the steam back into a pure liquid called the distillate.
The dissolved solid remains behind in the flask.
Everyday Example: Producing pure distilled water for car batteries and hospital medicines.

#### 5. Magnetic Separation

When a mixture contains a magnetic metal (such as iron or steel) mixed with non-magnetic materials (like wood shavings, sand, or rice), a magnet is moved over the mixture. The magnetic particles cling to the magnet, leaving the rest behind.

Everyday Example: Scrapyards using giant electromagnets to lift crushed motor bodies away from plastic rubbish.
Common Physical Methods for Separating Mixtures

Comparing Common Separation Techniques

Solid in Liquid Separation

Decantation vs Filtration

Decantation is a quick method of pouring off liquid after heavy solids settle, while filtration uses a porous paper or cloth to trap even fine, floating particles cleanly.

Liquid Recovery

Evaporation vs Distillation

Evaporation lets the liquid escape as vapour into the air to recover only the dry solid, whereas distillation cools and collects the liquid vapour so that both liquid and solid are saved.

Physical Principles

Particle Size vs Boiling Point

Filtration separates particles based on differences in physical size, while distillation and evaporation separate components based on differences in boiling temperatures.

Solid-Solid Separation

Magnetic Attraction vs Hand-Picking

Magnetic separation pulls out magnetic metals instantly with a magnet, whereas hand-picking requires manual sorting based on visible differences like colour and shape.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that filtration can remove dissolved table salt or sugar from water.

Scientific Fact

Dissolved salt breaks down into microscopic ions that easily pass through the pores of filter paper along with water molecules; you must use evaporation or distillation.

Common Mistake

Thinking the liquid is permanently lost during distillation.

Scientific Fact

The evaporated liquid vapour enters a cooled condenser, which turns it back into liquid water (distillate) for collection.

Example B: Separating Iron Nails from Sawdust

Context: A carpenter drops a packet of small iron nails into a large basin filled with wooden sawdust.

Problem: Picking hundreds of small sharp nails out of wood shavings by hand is slow and dangerous.

Fix: Wave a strong bar magnet over the basin. The iron nails are magnetic and jump onto the magnet, leaving the non-magnetic wood shavings behind in seconds.

Example C: Cleaning Muddy Pond Water for Drinking

Context: A village stream gets stirred up by rain, leaving the water filled with brown floating clay and dirt.

Problem: How can the muddy water be clarified step-by-step?

Fix: Allow the bucket of water to stand undisturbed for an hour so heavy dirt settles (sedimentation). Decant the top liquid, then pour it through a clean multi-layer cloth or sand filter (filtration) to trap fine suspended mud particles, producing clear water.
