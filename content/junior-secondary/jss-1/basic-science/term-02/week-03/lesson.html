Week 03: Non-Living Things & Matter

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Non-Living Things & Matter for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever dropped an ice block into a warm glass of water on a hot sunny afternoon and watched it vanish, or noticed white steam rising from a boiling pot of soup? That hard ice, running water, and rising steam are all the exact same substance in three completely different forms. Everything around us—from the wooden bench in your classroom to the invisible air filling your lungs—is made of matter, packed with tiny moving particles.

Key Terms

Matter Anything in nature that has mass (weight) and occupies space (volume).
Particle A tiny, microscopic building block, such as an atom or molecule, that makes up all matter.
Solid A state of matter with tightly packed particles, giving it a fixed shape, fixed volume, and fixed mass.
Liquid A state of matter with loosely packed particles that can flow, possessing a fixed volume but taking the shape of its container.
Gas A state of matter whose particles are very far apart and move rapidly in all directions, having neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume.

Core Concept

What is Matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. All objects you can see, touch, taste, or smell—such as sand, water, food, plastic, and trees—are matter. Even invisible things like the air we breathe are matter because they have weight and take up space. All matter is made up of billions of microscopic particles called atoms and molecules.

The Three States of Matter

Matter exists naturally in three main physical states: solid, liquid, and gas. How a substance behaves depends on how its particles are packed and how freely they can move:

1.
Solids:
Particle Arrangement: Particles are packed very tightly together in neat, orderly rows.
Forces & Movement: Held by very strong forces of attraction. Particles cannot move around; they can only vibrate back and forth in their fixed positions.
Properties: Solids have a definite shape, fixed volume, and fixed mass. They cannot be compressed (squeezed into a smaller space). Examples include stones, ice blocks, wood, salt, and iron rods.
1.
Liquids:
Particle Arrangement: Particles are close together but not in neat rows.
Forces & Movement: Held by moderate forces of attraction. Particles have enough energy to slide and roll over one another, allowing liquids to flow (fluidity).
Properties: Liquids have a fixed volume and fixed mass, but no fixed shape (they take the shape of whatever cup, bottle, or bowl holds them). Liquids cannot be easily compressed. Examples include water, palm oil, kerosene, and milk.
1.
Gases:
Particle Arrangement: Particles are spaced very far apart with large empty spaces between them.
Forces & Movement: Held by very weak forces of attraction. Particles move freely at great speed in all directions.
Properties: Gases have a fixed mass, but no fixed shape and no fixed volume (they spread out to fill any closed container). Gases can be easily compressed. Examples include air, oxygen, steam, and cooking gas.
The Three States of Matter and Their Particle Arrangements

Changes of State of Matter

Matter can change from one state to another when it is heated or cooled. Adding heat gives particles energy to break free and move apart, while cooling takes away energy so particles slow down and pull closer together:

Melting: A solid turns into a liquid when heated (e.g., an ice block melting into liquid water at 0°C).
Evaporation / Boiling: A liquid turns into a gas when heated (e.g., water boiling on a stove at 100°C to form steam).
Condensation: A gas cools down and turns back into a liquid (e.g., steam cooling into water droplets on the underside of a pot lid).
Freezing (Solidification): A liquid cools down and turns into a solid (e.g., water inside a freezer freezing into an ice block at 0°C).
Sublimation: A solid turns directly into a gas without melting into a liquid first (e.g., camphor / naphthalene mothballs placed in a school uniform box slowly shrinking into vapour).

Classification of Non-Living Matter: Metals and Non-Metals

Scientists also group non-living solids into two practical groups based on their physical properties:

Metals: Usually hard, shiny (metallic lustre), heavy, and can be hammered into flat sheets (malleable) or pulled into long wires (ductile). Metals conduct heat and electricity very well and generally have high melting points. Examples include iron, copper, aluminium, silver, and gold. (Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature).
Non-Metals: Usually dull, light, soft, and brittle (they snap or shatter when hammered). Non-metals are poor conductors of heat and electricity (insulators). Examples include wood, rubber, plastics, sulphur, charcoal (carbon), and oxygen gas.

Comparing the Three Physical States of Matter

Particle Arrangement

Packing of Particles

Solid particles are tightly packed in regular patterns, liquid particles are slightly looser, and gas particles are spread very far apart.

Particle Motion

Freedom of Movement

Solids only vibrate in fixed spots due to strong bonds, liquids slide and flow past one another, and gases fly freely in all directions.

Shape and Volume

Definite vs Variable Form

Solids keep a fixed shape and volume; liquids keep a fixed volume but match their container's shape; gases expand to fill any container size and shape.

Compressibility

Squeezing into Smaller Space

Solids and liquids cannot be squeezed smaller because their particles are already touching, whereas gases compress easily due to large empty spaces.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that invisible air is not matter because we cannot see or hold it.

Scientific Fact

Air is real matter; it has mass and occupies space. An inflated football or tyre is noticeably heavier and firmer than a completely flat, empty one.

Common Mistake

Thinking that camphor balls in a wardrobe dissolve or melt when they get smaller.

Scientific Fact

Camphor does not melt into a liquid; it sublimes directly from a solid into an invisible gas that mixes into the air.

Worked Examples

Example A: Weighing an Inflated Balloon

Context: A student hangs two identical unblown balloons on a simple ruler balance, and the ruler balances evenly. The student then blows air into one balloon and hangs it back up.

Problem: The ruler tilts downward toward the blown-up balloon, surprising the student.

Fix: Air is matter. Even though it is an invisible mixture of gases, it has mass. The air inside the blown-up balloon adds extra weight, causing that side of the balance to dip.

Example B: Water Forming on the Outside of a Cold Drink

Context: A cold glass bottle of soft drink is taken out of a refrigerator and placed on a dry table. Within five minutes, water droplets cover the outside of the glass.

Problem: The student thinks the glass bottle has tiny holes and is leaking drink from the inside.

Fix: The bottle is solid glass and is not leaking. Warm invisible water vapour (gas) floating in the surrounding room air touches the cold glass surface, loses heat, and condenses into liquid water droplets.

Example C: Heating Candle Wax

Context: A lighted candle melts solid wax into a pool of clear liquid near the wick, which runs down the side and turns back into a hard solid.

Problem: Explaining what happens to the wax particles as temperature changes.

Fix: Heat from the flame gives the solid wax particles energy to break their rigid pattern and flow as a liquid (melting). When the wax drips away from the flame, it loses heat, cools down, and packs tightly back into a hard solid (freezing).

Home Application & Guardian Sync

Take an ice block from your home freezer or refrigerator with your guardian. Place it in a small bowl on the kitchen table and watch it melt into water. If possible, observe water boiling in a covered pot to see steam condense into droplets on the lid, and explain the phase changes you see.
