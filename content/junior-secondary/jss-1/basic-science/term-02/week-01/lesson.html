Week 01: Living Things (I) — Characteristics and Classification

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Living Things (I) — Characteristics and Classification for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever wondered why weeds grow so aggressively next to your maize crops, or why a camel can easily survive in the hot, dry desert while a tilapia fish cannot? In nature, living things do not just eat and grow—they constantly struggle for sunlight, water, and space, and have special features that help them survive in their habitats. By understanding the complete signs of life, you will discover how plants and animals stay alive and adapt to our environment.

Key Terms

Living Thing An organism that has life and can carry out all fundamental life activities, including feeding, breathing, and reproducing.
Competition The struggle between living organisms for essential life necessities such as food, water, sunlight, and living space.
Adaptation The special features and behaviors that help a living organism survive comfortably in its specific environment.
Irritability The ability of a living organism to detect changes in its surroundings and respond to them.
Respiration The cellular breakdown of food substances using oxygen to release energy for bodily activities.

Core Concept

Meaning and Characteristics of Living Things (MR NIGERCLAD)

Living things are organisms that possess life and perform vital biological activities. We can easily remember all ten fundamental characteristics of living things using the memory word MR NIGERCLAD:

1.
Movement: Animals move from place to place (locomotion) to search for food and shelter. Plants remain anchored in one spot but show slow movements toward light or water.
2.
Respiration: Living cells take in oxygen to break down food molecules and release energy for daily activities.
3.
Nutrition: All living things feed. Green plants manufacture their own food through photosynthesis, while animals feed on plants and other animals.
4.
Irritability (Sensitivity): Living organisms detect changes in their surroundings (stimuli) and react to protect themselves.
5.
Growth: A permanent increase in size, height, and weight as new cells are produced.
6.
Excretion: The removal of poisonous metabolic waste products (like urine, sweat, and carbon dioxide) made by body cells.
7.
Reproduction: The ability of living things to produce new offspring of their own kind so their species continues to exist.
8.
Competition: Living things struggle with one another for limited resources such as food, water, sunlight, soil nutrients, and living space.
9.
Life Span / Death: Every living organism has a limited period of existence, after which life activities stop and the organism dies.
10.
Adaptation: Living things develop physical traits or behavioral habits that help them survive in their natural habitats.
The Ten Fundamental Characteristics of Living Organisms (MR NIGERCLAD)

The Classification of Living Things

Living things are organized into groups based on similarities in their body structures and life habits:

Kingdom Monera: Microscopic, single-celled organisms without a true nucleus (e.g., bacteria).
Kingdom Protista: Mostly single-celled organisms with a true nucleus (e.g., Amoeba, Paramecium).
Kingdom Fungi: Non-green organisms that absorb nutrients from decaying organic matter (e.g., mushrooms, yeast, bread mould).
Kingdom Plantae: Multicellular green organisms that produce their own food by photosynthesis (e.g., maize, beans, hibiscus, mango tree).
Kingdom Animalia: Multicellular organisms that move about freely and eat other organisms (e.g., fish, toads, lizards, birds, goats, humans).

Differences and Similarities Between Plants and Animals

Key Differences:

Nutrition: Plants make their own food using chloroplasts, sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide (photosynthesis). Animals cannot make food and must feed on plants or other animals.
Locomotion: Animals move freely from place to place using legs, wings, or fins. Plants stay rooted in the soil and only bend parts toward stimuli.
Cell Structure: Plant cells have a tough outer cellulose cell wall. Animal cells have only a soft cell membrane.
Growth Pattern: Plants grow mostly at the tips of their roots and shoots (apical growth). Animals grow evenly across all parts of their bodies.
Respiration Structures: Plants breathe through tiny leaf pores (stomata) and stem openings (lenticels). Animals breathe using lungs, gills, spiracles, or moist skin.

Similarities:

Both plants and animals feed, respire, excrete waste, grow, reproduce, compete for resources, and eventually die.

Importance and Uses of Plants and Animals

Uses of Plants: Provide food (grains, tubers, fruits, vegetables); supply timber for building houses and furniture; provide herbs for medicine; beautify surroundings; act as windbreaks and control soil erosion; absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen; generate personal and national income.
Uses of Animals: Provide food (meat, eggs, milk); serve as beasts of burden (donkeys, oxen); provide transportation (horses, camels); offer security (guard dogs); generate income through livestock farming.

Comparing Key Differences Between Plants and Animals

Food Production

Autotrophic vs Heterotrophic

Plants produce their own food inside green chloroplasts via photosynthesis, whereas animals must eat external food to get energy.

Body Movement

Stationary vs Locomotion

Plants are anchored by roots and show only slow localized bending, while animals actively move their whole bodies from place to place.

Growth Location

Apical vs Whole-Body Growth

Plant growth occurs primarily at the tips of shoots and roots throughout life, whereas animals grow evenly across their entire body.

Breathing Organs

Stomata/Lenticels vs Specialized Organs

Plants exchange respiratory gases through leaf stomata and stem lenticels, whereas animals use organs like lungs, gills, or skin.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Thinking that competition only occurs between fierce wild animals like lions and hyenas.

Scientific Fact

Plants also compete actively. Weeds and crops in the same farm bed compete vigorously for soil nutrients, moisture, and sunlight.

Common Mistake

Believing that non-living things like motor cars are alive because they move and consume fuel.

Scientific Fact

A car does not perform biological life functions; it cannot reproduce, adapt to its environment, compete biologically, or grow new cells.

Worked Examples

Example A: Competition on a Farmland

Context: A farmer plants maize seeds but fails to weed the farm for four weeks, resulting in stunted, yellow maize seedlings.

Problem: The wild weeds and the maize crops are competing for the exact same soil nitrogen, water, and sunlight.

Fix: Hand-weed or hoe the farm bed early to eliminate competition so the maize roots can absorb water and nutrients freely.

Example B: Observing Animal Adaptation

Context: A duck swims easily across a pond without sinking and paddles smoothly through the water.

Problem: A student wonders why a domestic chicken would struggle and drown in the same deep pond.

Fix: The duck possesses webbed feet that act as paddles and oily waterproof feathers that keep it buoyant—special physical adaptations for aquatic life that a chicken lacks.

Example C: Apical Growth in Garden Shrubs

Context: A gardener trims the top shoots of an overgrown hedge, causing the hedge to stop growing taller and instead grow bushy along the sides.

Problem: Explaining why trimming the tops changes the hedge's growth shape.

Fix: Plant growth is apical (concentrated at the tips). Cutting off the apical shoot tips removes the primary growth points, forcing the plant to activate side buds.
