Week 07: Pure and Impure Substances (I) — Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Pure and Impure Substances (I) — Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever stirred a spoonful of table salt into a cup of clean water and watched it vanish completely, or noticed the bright reddish-gold color of a pure copper wire? While the copper wire is made of only one single kind of building block (an element), the salt is made of two different elements locked tightly together (a compound), and the salty water is a mixture that you can easily separate simply by boiling away the water. Everything in our universe can be sorted into pure substances and mixtures!

Key Terms

Element A pure substance made of only one type of atom that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical method.
Chemical Symbol A short letter or pair of letters used by scientists worldwide to represent a chemical element.
Compound A pure substance formed when two or more different elements chemically combine in a fixed, definite proportion.
Mixture An impure substance made of two or more different substances physically mingled together without any chemical bond.
Pure Substance A material that consists of only one type of particle and has a definite, uniform chemical composition throughout.

Core Concept

Pure vs. Impure Substances

In science, matter is classified into two broad categories:

1.
Pure Substances: Matter that has a uniform chemical makeup and distinct physical properties (like a sharp melting and boiling point). Pure substances consist of either elements or compounds.
2.
Impure Substances (Mixtures): Matter containing two or more different substances physically mixed together in any proportion. Their properties depend on the individual substances present.

Elements and Chemical Symbols

An element is the simplest form of matter. It consists of only one kind of atom. Today, scientists recognize 118 known elements.

Classification of Matter: Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

Chemical Compounds

A compound is formed when two or more different elements join together through a chemical reaction. When elements form a compound, they lose their individual properties and form a brand-new substance with totally new characteristics. Compounds can only be broken down by chemical methods:

Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Made of hydrogen gas and oxygen gas chemically bonded in a fixed ratio of $2:1$. Even though hydrogen burns and oxygen supports fire, water puts out fires!
Common Salt ($\text{NaCl}$ - Sodium Chloride): Formed from dangerous sodium metal and toxic green chlorine gas. Once chemically joined, they form harmless white salt we eat every day.
Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$): Formed from black solid carbon and colourless oxygen gas.

Mixtures

A mixture contains two or more substances that are physically mingled together without any chemical bonding:

The substances inside a mixture keep their original properties.
They can be mixed in any ratio (for example, sweet tea can have one spoon of sugar or five spoons).
The components can be separated easily by simple physical methods like filtering, evaporating, or hand-picking.
Everyday Examples: Air (a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and water vapour), crude oil, muddy water, brass, and garri soaked with water and groundnuts.

Comparing Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

Chemical Composition

One Particle vs Joined Atoms vs Physical Blend

Elements contain only one kind of atom, compounds contain two or more elements chemically bonded in fixed proportions, and mixtures contain substances physically combined in any ratio.

Method of Separation

Cannot Separate vs Chemical vs Physical

Elements cannot be broken down by chemical means, compounds require chemical reactions or electricity to separate, and mixtures are easily separated by physical methods.

Retention of Properties

Unique Identity vs Loss of Traits vs Retained Traits

Elements possess their own unique atomic properties, compounds lose the original traits of their constituent elements, while mixtures retain the individual tastes, colours, and traits of their components.

Energy Changes During Formation

Atomic Creation vs Heat Exchange vs No Reaction

Elements are fundamental matter, forming a compound usually absorbs or releases significant heat/light, whereas mixing substances involves little to no energy exchange.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that dissolving sugar or salt in water forms a new chemical compound.

Scientific Fact

Dissolving forms a physical mixture (a solution), not a chemical compound. The water still tastes sweet or salty, and the water can be boiled away to leave the solid sugar or salt behind.

Common Mistake

Thinking all chemical symbols are just the first letter of their everyday English names.

Scientific Fact

When two elements share the same starting letter, a second lowercase letter is added (e.g., Carbon is $\text{C}$, Calcium is $\text{Ca}$). Other elements use Latin names (e.g., Sodium is $\text{Na}$ from *Natrium*, Potassium is $\text{K}$ from *Kalium*).

Worked Examples

Example A: Classifying Common Table Salt

Context: A student examines white table salt crystals in the kitchen and wants to classify the substance.

Problem: Is table salt an element, a compound, or a mixture?

Fix: Table salt is chemically known as sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$). It is made of two different elements—sodium and chlorine—chemically combined in a fixed $1:1$ ratio, forming a single pure substance. Therefore, table salt is a chemical compound.

Example B: Preparing Soaked Garri with Groundnuts

Context: A student mixes cold water, garri, sugar, and roasted groundnuts in a bowl for lunch.

Problem: What type of matter has the student prepared?

Fix: The garri, groundnuts, sugar, and water are not chemically bonded together. Each ingredient keeps its original taste and texture, and they can be mixed in any proportion. This makes soaked garri a physical mixture.

Example C: Decoding the Chemical Symbol for Sodium

Context: A test question asks for the chemical symbol of the element sodium, and a student writes "So".

Problem: Why is "So" incorrect for sodium?

Fix: The chemical symbol for sodium is derived from its historical Latin name, *Natrium*. Biologists and chemists worldwide use the first two letters of this Latin name: capital $\text{N}$ followed by lowercase $\text{a}$ ($\text{Na}$).
