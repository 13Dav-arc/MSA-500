Week 04: Refuse and Sewage Disposal

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Refuse and Sewage Disposal for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Every single day, your household discards food scraps, plastic bags, discarded cans, and wastewater from bathing, washing, and the toilet. In a busy city like Lagos, Kano, or Ibadan, millions of citizens generate tens of thousands of tonnes of waste each week. If this refuse is left in open street heaps and sewage is allowed to flow into gutters, disease-carrying pests multiply, toxic chemicals leach into drinking aquifers, and drainage channels choke, causing devastating city floods. Safe, scientific waste management is not simply about keeping a compound neat; it is the fundamental barrier that protects human civilization from environmental collapse and epidemic disease.

Key Terms

Refuse All solid waste materials discarded from residential households, schools, commercial markets, and industries, including vegetable peels, paper, broken glass, plastics, and metals.
Sewage Liquid waste and wastewater containing human feces, urine, and used soapy water conveyed from domestic bathrooms, kitchens, and industrial buildings through pipes and drains.
Biodegradable Waste Organic waste substances that can be naturally decomposed and broken down into simple harmless elements by bacteria and fungi in the soil.
Leachate A hazardous, highly toxic liquid formed when rainwater percolates through decomposing refuse heaps, dissolving chemicals and carrying pathogenic microbes into soil and groundwater.
Septic Tank An underground watertight settling chamber constructed of concrete or masonry where domestic sewage undergoes anaerobic bacterial decomposition and solid liquefaction.

Core Concept

1. Classification of Community Waste

In environmental science, discarded waste is divided into two primary categories based on physical state and chemical breakdown:

Refuse (Solid Waste):
Consists of solid, dry, or discarded packaging and food residues.
Biodegradable Refuse: Organic matter capable of natural microbial decomposition. Examples include yam peelings, food leftovers, garden leaves, dry grasses, wood shavings, and natural paper.
Non-Biodegradable Refuse: Synthetic or inorganic materials that cannot be decomposed by natural soil bacteria and persist in the environment for decades or centuries. Examples include polythene nylon bags, PET plastic bottles, tin cans, glass bottles, and discarded electronics.
Sewage (Liquid Waste):
Includes blackwater (water contaminated with human feces and urine from toilets) and greywater (used soapy wastewater from bathing, handwashing, and kitchen sinks).

2. Methods of Solid Waste (Refuse) Disposal

Improper refuse disposal creates offensive odors, pollutes groundwater, and breeds disease vectors like houseflies and rats:

1.
Open Dumping (Unsanitary & Dangerous):
Refuse is piled in uncovered heaps along roadsides, open fields, or empty plots.
Severe Hazards: Releases toxic leachate, blocks stormwater drainage causing street flooding, generates foul odors, and serves as a major breeding ground for flies, rodents, and mosquitoes.
1.
Sanitary Landfill:
An engineered disposal site excavated into the earth, lined with impermeable clay or synthetic geomembranes to catch toxic leachate.
Refuse is dumped in thin layers, mechanically compacted with heavy bulldozers, and covered daily with fresh soil to prevent flies and scavengers from accessing it.
1.
Composting:
A biological recycling method where biodegradable organic waste (food peels, leaves, manure) is piled in aerobic trenches and turned regularly.
Microorganisms convert the waste into dark, nutrient-rich organic fertilizer called humus or compost for farming.
1.
Incineration:
Thermal combustion of combustible waste at very high temperatures ($850^\circ\text{C}$ to $1100^\circ\text{C}$) in specialized industrial furnaces.
Greatly reduces waste volume by up to 90% and destroys hazardous hospital waste (syringes, contaminated dressings), though emissions must be scrubbed to prevent air pollution.
1.
The Modern Waste Hierarchy (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle):
Reduce: Minimizing waste generation by using durable goods.
Reuse: Finding secondary uses for containers (e.g., glass jars, plastic basins).
Recycle: Reprocessing discarded plastics, paper, and metal scrap into new raw industrial materials.

3. Methods of Liquid Waste (Sewage) Disposal

Safe sewage management isolates fecal pathogens from drinking water sources and human contact:

Pit Latrine:
A hole dug 3 to 5 metres deep into the ground, covered with a concrete slab fitted with a squat hole and drop lid.
Siting Rule: Must be located at least 15 to 30 metres down-slope from any water well or borehole to protect groundwater.
Ventilated Improved Pit (VIP) Latrine:
An advanced pit latrine fitted with an external vertical ventilation pipe covered with a fine wire mesh fly screen at the top.
Air currents pull foul odors out through the pipe, and flies entering the dark pit are attracted to light at the top of the vent pipe, where the mesh traps them until they die of dehydration.
Bucket Latrine (Decommissioned & Outlawed):
An unsanitary historical system where nightsoil was accumulated in buckets beneath a seat and emptied manually by human nightsoil scavengers. It is completely prohibited due to extreme disease transmission hazards.
Water Closet (WC) and Septic Tank System:
The gold standard for modern residential buildings.
A porcelain bowl with a water trap (U-bend) holds a water barrier that stops sewer gases from entering the bathroom. Flushing conveys waste into an underground septic tank.
Septic Tank & Soakaway: Anaerobic bacteria break down solid sludge at the bottom of the septic tank; clarified effluent flows into an adjacent gravel-filled soakaway pit to filter harmlessly into surrounding soil.
Central Municipal Sewage Treatment:
Urban sewer pipes transport wastewater to large public works where sewage undergoes screening, grit removal, primary settling, biological oxidation beds, and chemical chlorination before returning clean water into natural rivers.

Comparing Systems of Community Waste Disposal

Sanitary Landfill

Engineered Solid Containment

Uses lined pits, daily soil cover, and leachate collection to safely isolate solid refuse from community contact, but requires large tracts of municipal land.

Composting

Biological Resource Recovery

Decomposes organic biodegradable scraps into rich agricultural humus, but cannot process synthetic polymers, nylon, metals, or toxic chemicals.

VIP Latrine

Odor-Free On-Site Sanitation

Utilizes a screened vent pipe and dark superstructure to eliminate flies and odors without requiring running tap water or electricity.

Septic Tank & Soakaway

Waterborne Subterranean Digestion

Uses water flush to transport sewage to an underground anaerobic settling chamber, providing hygienic indoor sanitation but requiring continuous water supply and space.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Throwing plastic bags and discarded PET bottles into compound storm drainage ditches during heavy rainfall.

Scientific Fact

Rainwater does not destroy synthetic plastics; debris lodges under bridges and culverts, creating total blockages that trigger destructive flash floods.

Common Mistake

Believing that burning household garbage in open residential yards is an environmentally friendly disposal technique.

Scientific Fact

Open burning of plastics and synthetic rubbish releases highly carcinogenic dioxins, carbon monoxide, and toxic particulate smog that damages community respiratory health.

Common Mistake

Emptying raw, untreated septic tank sewage directly into community streams or gutters during midnight rainstorms.

Scientific Fact

Discharging raw sewage spreads billions of pathogenic Vibrio cholerae and Salmonella typhi bacteria directly into neighborhood waterways, triggering fatal community epidemics.

Example 2: Analyzing the Operating Physics of a Water Closet (WC) U-Bend

Context: A homeowner notices a foul, pungent smell of sewer gas in the bathroom after a guest toilet has been left unused for six dry months.

Problem: Why did the sewer smell begin entering the house, and what scientific remedy restores the barrier?

Fix: The curved U-bend beneath the toilet bowl maintains a standing column of water called a "water seal" that physically blocks sewer gases from flowing backward into the room. During six months of non-use, this standing water evaporated completely. Flushing the toilet refills the U-bend trap with water, immediately re-establishing the gas barrier.

Example 3: Siting a Household Soakaway Pit on a Residential Plot

Context: An architect is laying out the septic tank and soakaway pit for a new residential building on a plot that includes a deep borehole.

Problem: A contractor recommends placing the soakaway pit 4 metres uphill from the borehole to minimize drainage pipe excavation.

Fix: This layout poses extreme public health danger. Effluent from a soakaway pit carries bacteria and dissolved nitrates that leach downward into subterranean groundwater aquifers. Public health engineering rules dictate that soakaway pits must be placed strictly downhill and at least 15 to 30 metres away from any borehole or drinking well.
