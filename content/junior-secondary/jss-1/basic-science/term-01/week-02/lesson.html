Week 02: Nutrition & Balanced Diet

Understanding the six vital food classes, preventing clinical deficiency disorders, and planning balanced nutrition with accessible local foods.

Why This Matters to You

Have you ever wondered why eating only a large mound of plain garri leaves you feeling exhausted and unable to concentrate in class just two hours later? Your body does not simply need belly-filling bulk—it requires specific biological building blocks to function. Understanding the six food classes will allow you to build an energizing, balanced diet using affordable local foods.

Key Terms

Nutrition The biological process by which living organisms take in and utilize food nutrients for growth, energy, and cellular repair.
Balanced Diet A meal containing all six classes of food in the correct proportions required for daily metabolic needs.
Malnutrition A pathological physical condition resulting from an insufficient, excessive, or improperly balanced nutrient intake.
Deficiency Disease A specific illness caused by the prolonged lack of an essential dietary nutrient, such as Kwashiorkor or Scurvy.

Core Concept

The Six Classes of Food & Nigerian Sources

Every food item grown or consumed across Nigeria belongs to one or more of six vital chemical classes:

1.
Carbohydrates: The primary fuel source for cellular respiration, physical labor, and brain function. Local Sources: Cassava (garri, fufu), yam, maize, millet, rice, sweet potatoes.
2.
Proteins: Nitrogenous compounds essential for building new muscle cells, synthesizing metabolic enzymes, and repairing damaged tissues. Local Sources: Cowpeas (beans), soybean, fish, crayfish, eggs, groundnuts.
3.
Fats and Oils: Dense caloric reserves providing twice the energy of carbohydrates, cushioning internal organs, and dissolving fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Local Sources: Red palm oil, groundnut oil, melon seeds (egusi).
4.
Vitamins: Organic protective compounds required in minute quantities to regulate biological enzymes and resist infectious disease. Local Sources: Oranges, mangoes, carrots, pawpaw, green leafy vegetables (ugwu, waterleaf).
5.
Minerals: Inorganic elements necessary for structural bone density, blood hemoglobin production, and electrolyte balance. Local Sources: Ground crayfish, bone meal, iodized salt, dark green leaves.
6.
Water: The universal solvent making up 60% to 70% of human body weight, responsible for transporting nutrients, removing waste, and regulating temperature.
The Six Classes of Food and Local Dietary Sources

Malnutrition: Under-Nutrition vs. Over-Nutrition

Malnutrition includes both under-nutrition (deficiency diseases) and over-nutrition (excessive caloric intake leading to childhood obesity and vascular strain). When children consume almost purely carbohydrates without adequate protein, Kwashiorkor develops. When they face total caloric and protein starvation, Marasmus occurs.

Deficiency Disorders & Clinical Signs

Protein Deficiency

Kwashiorkor

Swollen fluid-filled abdomen (edema), cracked skin, thinning reddish-brown hair, and severe physical growth retardation.

Vitamin C Deficiency

Scurvy

Swollen and bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor skin wound healing, and fragile blood capillaries.

Vitamin A Deficiency

Night Blindness

Failure of light-sensitive pigments in the retina resulting in an inability to see clearly in low-light environments.

Iron Deficiency

Anemia

Reduced blood hemoglobin leading to pale inner eyelids, pale nail beds, chronic exhaustion, and shortness of breath.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that eating until your stomach is completely full guarantees that your body has received a balanced diet.

Scientific Fact

Physical fullness is usually achieved by carbohydrate volume alone; nutritional balance requires specific distributions of proteins, lipids, vitamins, and minerals.

Common Mistake

Assuming red palm oil is purely fattening and should be eliminated from a healthy diet.

Scientific Fact

Unadulterated Nigerian palm oil is rich in beta-carotene (provitamin A), which protects eyesight and supports the immune system.

Worked Examples

Example A: The Carbohydrate Imbalance Trap

Context: A student eats white bread for breakfast, white rice for lunch, and a large mound of garri for dinner.

Problem: The student's diet is over 85% carbohydrate with virtually zero protein, leading to constant afternoon fatigue and impaired mental concentration.

Fix: Replace half the rice portion with steamed cowpea beans or fish, and add crayfish powder and ugwu leaves to the evening soup.

Example B: Bleeding Gums in Boarding School

Context: A student living in a hostel consumes only dry biscuits and instant noodles for several weeks, refusing fresh vegetables.

Problem: Complete lack of ascorbic acid (Vitamin C), leading to impaired collagen production and early scurvy characterized by bleeding gums.

Fix: Introduce fresh local citrus fruits like oranges, grapefruits, or raw garden eggs into the student's daily food supply.

Example C: Low-Cost Correction of Iron Deficiency

Context: A student frequently experiences dizziness and pale nail beds during physical education classes.

Problem: Inadequate dietary iron intake, reducing hemoglobin levels and the blood's capacity to transport oxygen.

Fix: Add ground dried crayfish, cowpeas, and dark green leafy vegetables to weekly family stews.
