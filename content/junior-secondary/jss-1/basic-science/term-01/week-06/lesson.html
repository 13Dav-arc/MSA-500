Week 06: Disease Vectors & Prevention

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Disease Vectors & Prevention for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

A single insect weighing less than three milligrams lands quietly on your forearm, pierces your skin with microscopic mouthparts, and withdraws a tiny droplet of blood. In that fleeting moment, it injects hundreds of microscopic parasites directly into your bloodstream, initiating an infection that can cause high fever, chills, and life-threatening anemia. This insect is a disease vector. In tropical Africa, insects like mosquitoes, houseflies, tsetse flies, and blackflies transmit deadly diseases responsible for millions of hospital admissions each year. By understanding the life cycles, breeding habitats, and transmission routes of these vectors, you gain the scientific power to break their reproductive chains and safeguard your community.

Key Terms

Disease Vector A living carrier, most commonly an arthropod or rodent, that transports an infectious pathogenic microorganism from an infected host to a susceptible human or animal.
Complete Metamorphosis A four-stage developmental life cycle in insects comprising Egg, Larva (wriggler/maggot), Pupa (tumbler), and Adult (imago), where the immature forms differ completely in structure and habit from the adult.
Mechanical Vector A vector that physically transports pathogens on the external surface of its body, feet, or mouthparts without the pathogen multiplying or undergoing biological development inside the insect's tissues.
Biological Vector A vector inside whose body the pathogen must actively multiply, develop, or complete a mandatory phase of its life cycle before being transmitted to another host.
Larvicide An insecticide or chemical substance specifically formulated to target and kill the immature larval stage of insects before they can pupate into adults.

Core Concept

1. Definition and Classification of Vectors

In infectious disease epidemiology, a vector is any living organism that conveys pathogens from a reservoir to a victim:

Biological Vectors: The pathogen depends on the vector for its survival and life cycle. For example, the Plasmodium malaria parasite must undergo sexual reproduction inside the female Anopheles mosquito before entering human salivary glands.
Mechanical Vectors: The vector acts simply as a passive transporter. For example, the common housefly lands on open human excreta, picks up cholera bacteria on its hairy foot pads and sticky proboscis, and walks over exposed dinner food.

2. Major Tropical Disease Vectors and Their Pathogens

In Nigeria and broader West Africa, four primary insect vectors represent the greatest public health challenges:

1.
*Mosquitoes (Family Culicidae):*
Female Anopheles Mosquito: Transmits the protozoan parasite Plasmodium causing Malaria. Breeds in clean, unpolluted stagnant surface water (rain puddles, blocked gutters, discarded containers). Only the female bites because she requires blood protein to develop her eggs.
Aedes aegypti Mosquito: Transmits arboviruses responsible for Yellow Fever and Dengue Fever. It is a daytime biter distinguished by black-and-white striped legs, breeding predominantly in domestic water containers like tin cans, flower vases, and discarded vehicle tires.
Culex Mosquito: Transmits the microscopic nematode worm Wuchereria bancrofti, which blocks human lymphatic vessels to cause Filariasis (Elephantiasis). Breeds in heavily polluted, foul stagnant water such as overflowing septic soakaways and unwashed drains.
1.
*The Common Housefly (Musca domestica):*
Transmits bacterial and amoebic infections, notably Cholera (Vibrio cholerae), Typhoid Fever (Salmonella typhi), and Amoebic Dysentery.
Breeds in decaying organic garbage, wet animal dung, and exposed human feces.
Pathogens are transferred through three mechanisms: adherence to hairy legs, regurgitation of digestive vomitus drops onto solid food, and frequent defecation during feeding.
1.
*The Tsetse Fly (Genus Glossina):*
Transmits the flagellated protozoan parasite Trypanosoma, which causes African Sleeping Sickness (Human African Trypanosomiasis) in humans and Nagana in cattle.
Breeds in warm, shady riverine vegetation and dense savannah woodlands; deposits mature larvae directly into moist, shady soil.
1.
*The Blackfly (Simulium damnosum):*
Transmits the filarial nematode worm Onchocerca volvulus, which causes River Blindness (Onchocerciasis).
Breeds exclusively in fast-flowing, turbulent, well-oxygenated rivers and rocky waterfalls, where its aquatic larvae anchor to submerged rocks.
Morphology and Complete Four-Stage Metamorphosis of Tropical Disease Vectors

3. Life Cycles of Disease Vectors (Complete Metamorphosis)

Mosquitoes and houseflies undergo complete metamorphosis through four distinct structural stages:

The Mosquito Life Cycle:
1.
Egg: Female deposits 100–300 raft-like or floating eggs with lateral air floats directly on the surface of standing water.
2.
Larva (Wriggler): Hatches within 24–48 hours; lives suspended just below the water surface, feeding on microscopic algae and breathing atmospheric oxygen through a posterior respiratory siphon.
3.
Pupa (Tumbler): A comma-shaped, non-feeding aquatic transformative stage that breathes through paired thoracic trumpets.
4.
Adult (Imago): The pupal skin splits at the water surface, and the fully formed adult emerges, dries its wings, and takes flight to mate.
The Housefly Life Cycle:
1.
Egg: Laid in moist decaying organic matter.
2.
Larva (Maggot): Legless, cylindrical white larvae that feed voraciously on decomposing waste.
3.
Pupa (Puparium): Dark brown, barrel-shaped capsule inside which the maggot undergoes transformation into an adult fly.
4.
Adult: Emerges within several days to start feeding and reproducing.

4. Integrated Vector Management (Control Strategies)

Eradicating vector-borne disease requires an integrated, multi-barrier scientific approach:

Environmental Management (Habitat Destruction):
Clearing bushes and tall grasses around compounds (eliminates adult resting sites).
Emptying, puncturing, or burying water-holding tins, broken bottles, and discarded tires.
De-silting and sweeping compound drainage ditches to maintain continuous water flow and eliminate standing pools.
Physical / Mechanical Protection:
Sleeping under Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs / ITNs).
Installing fine wire mesh gauze across all windows, doors, and ventilation bricks.
Keeping all food items and drinking water reserves in tightly covered containers to block flies.
Chemical Control:
Applying oil films onto stagnant water: petroleum or paraffin forms an impermeable surface film that suffocates mosquito larvae by blocking their respiratory siphons.
Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) of walls with approved insecticides to kill resting adult mosquitoes.
Applying selective chemical larvicides to water reserves that cannot be drained.
Biological Control:
Introducing small larvivorous fish (such as Gambusia affinis, the mosquito fish) into decorative ponds and irrigation canals to feed on mosquito larvae.
Applying natural bacterial spores (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis / BTI) that selectively kill mosquito and blackfly larvae without harming other aquatic wildlife.

Comparing Four Major Tropical Disease Vectors

Female Anopheles Mosquito

Biological Vector of Malaria

Breeds in clean stagnant water pools, bites predominantly at night, and transmits Plasmodium protozoa through direct salivary inoculation during blood-feeding.

Aedes aegypti Mosquito

Vector of Yellow Fever & Dengue

Easily recognized by striped black-and-white legs, bites during daytime hours, and breeds in clean domestic artificial containers like vases, drums, and old tyres.

Common Housefly (Musca)

Mechanical Vector of Enteric Infections

Breeds in decaying manure and refuse, feeds by regurgitating digestive enzymes onto solid food, and transfers cholera and typhoid bacteria mechanically.

Blackfly (Simulium)

Vector of River Blindness

Breeds exclusively in turbulent, fast-flowing river rapids, and transmits Onchocerca filarial worms that migrate to the eyes to cause permanent blindness.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that all mosquitoes transmit malaria to human beings.

Scientific Fact

Only female mosquitoes of the genus Anopheles transmit malaria; male mosquitoes feed exclusively on plant nectar and lack mouthparts to pierce skin, while Culex and Aedes transmit other illnesses.

Common Mistake

Assuming that spraying insecticide aerosol in the bedroom once a month is sufficient to permanently eliminate mosquitoes.

Scientific Fact

Aerosols only kill adult mosquitoes present at that moment; if outdoor stagnant water gutters are left full of larvae, hundreds of newly hatched adults will replace them within days.

Common Mistake

Believing that houseflies transmit diseases through sharp biting stings like mosquitoes.

Scientific Fact

The common housefly possesses sponge-like, non-biting lapping mouthparts; it transmits disease mechanically by walking on feces and transferring germs via its feet, vomitus, and droppings onto exposed human food.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identifying the Metamorphic Stage Targeted by Paraffin Oil Films

Context: A community health team observes thousands of mosquito wrigglers suspended below the surface of an undrainable construction water pool.

Problem: The team pours a thin film of biodegradable paraffin oil across the water surface. Which developmental stage does this intervention target, and what is the biological mechanism of extermination?

Fix: The oil film targets the **larval (wriggler) and pupal (tumbler)** stages. Mosquito larvae and pupae breathe atmospheric oxygen through specialized respiratory siphons and trumpets projected through the water-air interface. The oil film forms an impenetrable surface layer with reduced surface tension, blocking the siphons and suffocating the larvae within hours.

Example 3: Designing Environmental Vector Control for a Residential Compound

Context: A student notes frequent swarms of mosquitoes in their bedroom despite keeping windows closed at dusk.

Problem: What outdoor environmental breeding sites around the residential compound should be inspected and eliminated to permanently stop mosquito reproduction?

Fix: The student must inspect: (1) blocked storm drainage gutters containing silt and standing water, (2) discarded plastic containers, tin cans, and vehicle tyres collecting rainwater, (3) uncovered overhead rainwater storage tanks, and (4) broken septic tank vent pipes lacking intact wire fly screens.
