Week 08: Drug and Substance Abuse

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Science covering Drug and Substance Abuse for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When you suffer from a severe malarial fever or bacterial infection, a doctor prescribes an antibiotic or antimalarial tablet that targets pathogens, restores cellular balance, and saves your life. Yet the very same biochemical pathways that allow pharmaceutical medicines to heal can also be hijacked by chemical substances that shatter human health, derail academic dreams, and tear families apart. Across secondary schools and communities, millions of young people encounter dangerous offers of unauthorized pills, volatile solvents, alcohol, and illicit drugs. Understanding the chemistry of drugs, the difference between medical therapy and chemical abuse, and the destructive trap of addiction empowers you to make life-preserving decisions and build an unshakeable future.

Key Terms

Drug Any chemical substance (other than food or water) that, when absorbed into the human body, alters the physiological, biochemical, or psychological functioning of the central nervous system and bodily organs.
Drug Misuse The incorrect, unauthorized, or improper usage of a legitimate prescribed or over-the-counter medicine, such as taking the wrong dosage, using another person's prescription, or failing to finish an antibiotic course.
Drug Abuse The intentional, excessive, and self-directed consumption of psychoactive chemical substances for mood alteration, intoxication, or non-medical reasons, resulting in physical, mental, and social harm.
Addiction (Dependence) A chronic, relapsing brain disease characterized by compulsive drug-seeking and usage despite devastating health, legal, and social consequences, accompanied by physical and psychological dependence.
Tolerance A physiological state resulting from regular drug consumption where the body adapts to the substance, requiring progressively larger doses to produce the original desired effect.

Core Concept

1. What is a Drug? Medicine vs. Illicit Substance

In biological science, a drug is any chemical agent that alters biological mechanisms:

Medicine: A drug manufactured, tested, and administered specifically to diagnose, treat, relieve, cure, or prevent disease. All medicines are drugs, but not all drugs are medicines.
Drug Use: Taking pharmaceutical drugs in exact compliance with a licensed medical practitioner's prescription or verified manufacturer guidelines for a diagnosed illness.
Drug Misuse: The improper or careless use of legitimate medicines. Common examples include:
Doubling an antimalarial dose to "cure the sickness faster."
Stopping prescribed antibiotics halfway through the course because symptoms have temporarily vanished, which breeds drug-resistant superbugs.
Using an elder sibling's prescription glasses or medication without an individual medical examination.
Buying prescription-only antibiotics from unauthorized street hawkers.
Drug Abuse: Taking chemical substances for recreational excitement, euphoria, escape from reality, or social pressure.

2. Classification of Commonly Abused Substances

Drugs of abuse are grouped by their primary legal status and pharmacological effects on the central nervous system (CNS):

1.
Legal and Socially Regulated Substances:
Alcohol (Ethanol): A potent central nervous system depressant found in beers, wines, and spirits. Impairs judgment, slows motor reflexes, damages the liver, and causes behavioral disinhibition.
Tobacco (Nicotine): Contains the highly addictive alkaloid nicotine. Inhaling cigarette smoke coats the lungs with toxic tar, destroying respiratory cilia and causing chronic bronchitis, emphysema, and lethal lung cancer.
Caffeine: A mild central nervous system stimulant found in coffee, tea, energy drinks, and cola nuts.
1.
Prescription Drugs Frequently Abused:
Codeine and Tramadol: Potent opioid pain relievers and cough suppressants. Abused in unauthorized mixtures ("syrup cocktails") to induce drowsiness and euphoria, rapidly leading to respiratory arrest and fatal addiction.
Benzodiazepines and Barbiturates: Sedatives and sleeping pills that slow brain activity, causing dangerous coma when combined with alcohol.
1.
Illicit (Prohibited) Narcotic Drugs:
Cannabis (Indian Hemp / Marijuana): Contains tetrahydrocannabinol (THC); alters sensory perception, impairs short-term memory, accelerates heart rate, and can trigger psychotic episodes in adolescents.
Cocaine and Methamphetamine: Powerful synthetic stimulants that induce dangerous euphoria, extreme paranoia, elevated blood pressure, and catastrophic cardiac failure.
Heroin: An illegal, highly addictive semi-synthetic opiate injected into veins; causes intense euphoria followed by severe physical withdrawal sickness and risk of HIV transmission via shared needles.
1.
Volatile Solvents and Inhalants:
Chemical vapors inhaled directly from industrial glue, petrol, kerosene, correction fluid, or paint thinners.
These toxic hydrocarbons pass rapidly from lungs into the brain, dissolving the fatty myelin sheaths of nerve cells, causing permanent brain damage and sudden sniffing death syndrome.

3. Causes and Risk Factors of Drug Abuse

Adolescents and secondary school students frequently fall into substance abuse due to specific social and psychological triggers:

Peer Pressure: The overwhelming desire to fit in with older students or belong to a popular social group.
Curiosity and Experimentation: A naive desire to "experience what getting high feels like" without understanding that chemical addiction can begin after single exposures.
Academic and Emotional Stress: Escaping family conflict, academic anxiety, loneliness, or untreated depression.
False Beliefs: Believing dangerous myths that certain pills increase examination memory, enable all-night study marathons, or bestow athletic superpowers.
Unregulated Accessibility: Ease of purchasing prescription medicines from unlicensed patent medicine stores and street hawkers.

4. Health, Social, and Economic Consequences

The destructive effects of substance abuse ripple across every sphere of life:

Physical Health:
Liver Cirrhosis: Chronic alcohol abuse destroys hepatocytes, replacing healthy liver tissue with non-functional scar tissue, culminating in liver failure.
Respiratory Disease: Smoking damages alveoli, causing lung cancer, heart attacks, and severe emphysema.
Brain Damage & Cognitive Decline: Inhalants and stimulants destroy neurons, eroding memory, concentration, and emotional control.
Overdose and Death: Excessive depressants (like codeine or tramadol) shut down the brain's respiratory centre, halting breathing entirely.
Psychological Harm:
Severe chemical dependence, paranoia, hallucinations, chronic anxiety, depression, and permanent drug-induced psychosis.
Socio-Economic Destruction:
Academic failure, chronic truancy, suspension, and complete school dropout.
Family devastation, domestic violence, broken trust, and emotional trauma.
Involvement in theft, robbery, gang violence, and prostitution to finance escalating drug costs.

5. Regulatory Agencies and Prevention Frameworks in Nigeria

Nigeria maintains statutory governmental agencies to combat the drug menace:

NAFDAC (National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control):
Mandated to regulate and control the manufacture, importation, exportation, distribution, advertisement, sale, and use of food, drugs, cosmetics, medical devices, chemicals, and packaged water.
Ensures all medicines sold in pharmacies bear genuine NAFDAC registration numbers, confiscating and incinerating fake, expired, and substandard drugs.
NDLEA (National Drug Law Enforcement Agency):
Nigeria's premier counter-narcotics law enforcement agency.
Mandated to eliminate the cultivation, processing, manufacturing, trafficking, and commercial sale of prohibited narcotic substances (cannabis, cocaine, heroin, methamphetamine).
Inspects seaports, airports, and border crossings, prosecutes drug traffickers in federal courts, and operates drug rehabilitation and counseling facilities across all 36 states.

Comparing Forms of Drug Involvement and Chemical Categories

Drug Use

Medically Supervised Therapy

Taking genuine prescribed pharmaceuticals in exact adherence to medical instructions to diagnose, treat, or cure a confirmed physical or psychological disease.

Drug Misuse

Careless or Inappropriate Administration

Taking legitimate medications incorrectly, such as altering dosages, sharing prescriptions, or prematurely stopping antibiotic treatments.

Drug Abuse

Recreational Psychoactive Consumption

Intentionally ingesting chemical substances for euphoria, intoxication, or non-medical escapism, leading to addiction, organ damage, and social destruction.

NAFDAC vs NDLEA

Product Quality vs Counter-Narcotics Enforcement

NAFDAC regulates the quality, safety, and labeling of legal foods and medicines; NDLEA arrests drug traffickers and eradicates illegal narcotics trade.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that stopping an antibiotic treatment early because you feel better after two days is harmless and saves money.

Scientific Fact

Prematurely discontinuing antibiotics leaves the strongest bacteria alive, allowing them to mutate into lethal, multidrug-resistant bacterial strains.

Common Mistake

Thinking that abusing prescription cough syrup with codeine or tramadol is safe simply because it is manufactured in pharmaceutical factories.

Scientific Fact

Pharmaceuticals are dangerous chemicals; when abused in non-medical doses, codeine and tramadol cause severe physical addiction and fatal respiratory arrest.

Common Mistake

Believing that you can experiment with addictive drugs like heroin, cocaine, or cannabis once or twice and stop whenever you want.

Scientific Fact

Psychoactive drugs alter the brain's dopamine reward circuitry; physical tolerance and compulsive dependence can develop rapidly, trapping users in chronic addiction.

Example 2: Analyzing the Danger of Inhaling Volatile Hydrocarbon Solvents

Context: A mechanic's apprentice is observed secretly sniffing fumes from open petrol drums and contact glue containers in an unventilated workshop.

Problem: What biochemical mechanism explains why volatile solvent sniffing causes rapid, irreversible brain damage?

Fix: Volatile solvents contain lipophilic (fat-soluble) hydrocarbons like toluene, benzene, and hexane. These chemicals dissolve rapidly into the lipid-rich myelin sheaths that insulate nerve cell axons in the brain. Stripping away myelin disrupts nerve signal conduction, killing brain cells, destroying memory and motor coordination, and causing sudden fatal cardiac arrest.

Example 3: Verifying the Authenticity of a Purchased Pharmaceutical Medicine

Context: A parent sends a JSS 1 student to a local pharmacy to purchase a pack of prescribed antimalarial tablets.

Problem: What three regulatory and scientific details printed on the medicine packaging must the student verify before paying for the medication?

Fix: The student must verify: (1) an authentic, printed **NAFDAC Registration Number** (e.g., NAFDAC Reg. No. XX-XXXX), (2) a clearly printed **Manufacture Date and Expiry Date** confirming the medicine is not expired, and (3) an intact, untampered **Manufacturer's Holographic Scratch Seal** revealing an authentication PIN for SMS verification.
