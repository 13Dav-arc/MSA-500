Week 07: Generations of Computers II (Third, Fourth, and Fifth Generations)

Foundational curriculum module in Information Technology covering Generations of Computers II (Third, Fourth, and Fifth Generations) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Hold up a modern smartphone or look at a laptop in your classroom. That slim, lightweight device weighs less than a notebook, yet it has more computing power than all the room-sized computers NASA used combined to land the Apollo astronauts on the Moon in 1969! It can instantly translate spoken Yoruba, Hausa, or Igbo into English, recognize your face in a fraction of a second, and search through billions of websites across the globe while resting on the palm of your hand. How did computers evolve from room-sized cabinets requiring technical teams into personal devices that fit inside our pockets? The secret is the microscopic marvel of Integrated Circuits, the invention of the Microprocessor, and the thrilling dawn of Artificial Intelligence across the Third, Fourth, and Fifth Generations.

Key Terms

Integrated Circuit (IC) A tiny wafer or microchip of semiconductor material (usually silicon) that combines thousands to billions of microscopic transistors, resistors, and capacitors etched onto a single miniature surface.
Microprocessor A complete Central Processing Unit (CPU) contained on a single integrated circuit chip, functioning as the primary computational engine of modern computers.
VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) An advanced semiconductor manufacturing technique that combines tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of electronic components onto a single microchip.
ULSI (Ultra Large Scale Integration) A state-of-the-art semiconductor fabrication process that packs millions, hundreds of millions, or billions of transistors onto a single silicon chip die.
Graphical User Interface (GUI) A visual computer environment that allows users to interact with software using visual elements such as windows, icons, menus, and pointer devices like a mouse or touchscreen.
Artificial Intelligence (AI) The branch of computer science focused on creating intelligent computing systems capable of performing tasks that traditionally require human cognitive intelligence, such as visual perception, voice recognition, learning, and reasoning.

Core Concept

The Evolution of Modern Computing Generations

The historical progression of computing accelerated exponentially in the 1960s with the invention of the microchip. Rather than soldering individual transistors onto circuit boards, engineers learned how to print and etch entire complex circuits onto microscopic slices of silicon crystal.

The Silicon Miracle: Modern Microprocessors pack billions of transistors onto a thumbnail-sized silicon wafer

---

Third Generation of Computers (Approx. 1964 – 1971): The Integrated Circuit Era

In 1958 and 1959, American engineers Jack Kilby (at Texas Instruments) and Robert Noyce (at Fairchild Semiconductor) independently invented the Integrated Circuit (IC) or silicon microchip:

Core Hardware Component: Integrated Circuits (ICs). Hundreds and thousands of miniature transistors, diodes, and resistors were etched into a single flat silicon chip.
Operating Speed: Measured in nanoseconds ($10^{-9}$ seconds—one billionth of a second).
User Interface Revolution:
Punched cards were replaced by keyboards for data entry and video display terminal monitors (VDTs) for viewing output.
The introduction of modern Operating Systems (OS) allowed computers to run multiple programs simultaneously (multiprogramming) and permitted multiple users to share a central system at the same time (time-sharing).
Physical Footprint & Cost: Machines became substantially smaller, cheaper, and more energy-efficient, allowing universities, hospitals, and medium-sized businesses to own their own computers (called minicomputers).
Notable Third-Generation Computers:
IBM System/360: A historic family of computers that shared the same architecture, allowing businesses to upgrade hardware without rewriting their software.
DEC PDP-8: The world's first commercially successful minicomputer, priced affordably for university science laboratories.

---

Fourth Generation of Computers (Approx. 1971 – Present): The Microprocessor Era

In 1971, a team of engineers at Intel led by Ted Hoff, Federico Faggin, and Stan Mazor achieved a monumental breakthrough: they placed an entire Central Processing Unit onto a single silicon chip known as the Intel 4004. This birthed the Microprocessor:

Core Hardware Component: VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) and later VLSIC, placing hundreds of thousands to millions of transistors on a single microchip.
Operating Speed: Measured in picoseconds ($10^{-12}$ seconds) with processor clock speeds rated in megahertz (MHz) and gigahertz (GHz).
Birth of the Personal Computer (PC):
Because a microprocessor was tiny and inexpensive, manufacturers could build computers specifically for individuals to place on their office desks or study tables at home.
In 1977, Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs introduced the Apple II. In 1981, IBM launched the IBM PC, cementing the personal computer as a worldwide business necessity.
Graphical User Interfaces (GUI) & The Mouse:
In 1984, the Apple Macintosh introduced the mass market to the Graphical User Interface (GUI), replacing complex command-line prompts (like MS-DOS) with windows, desktop folders, icons, and a rolling computer mouse.
Microsoft released Windows, bringing GUI computing to millions of IBM-compatible computers.
Networking & The Internet:
The fourth generation saw the expansion of Local Area Networks (LANs) and the global Internet.
In 1989, British computer scientist Sir Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web (WWW), transforming computers into global communications tools.
Modern Form Factors: Laptops, notebook computers, tablets, and smartphones are all direct products of fourth-generation microprocessor engineering.

---

Fifth Generation of Computers (Present and Beyond): Artificial Intelligence & ULSI

The Fifth Generation represents the frontier of modern computing, driven by Ultra Large Scale Integration (ULSI) and Artificial Intelligence (AI):

Core Hardware Component: ULSI chips containing billions of transistors, specialized Neural Processing Units (NPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs), massively parallel computing architectures, and quantum computing prototypes.
Defining Capabilities & Technologies:
Natural Language Processing (NLP): Computers that understand and process human speech and text in natural languages such as English, Hausa, Yoruba, and French.
Voice Assistants & Smart Recognition: Systems like Apple Siri, Google Assistant, and automated call centers that respond intelligently to voice commands.
Biometrics & Computer Vision: Facial recognition unlocking phones, automated license plate readers, and medical imaging systems that detect diseases in X-rays.
Autonomous Machines: Self-driving automobiles, agricultural drones that spray crops autonomously, and surgical robots in modern operating theatres.
Generative AI & Machine Learning: Software models that can learn from massive data patterns, write essays, compose music, generate computer code, and solve advanced scientific problems.

Home Application & Guardian Sync

With your parent or guardian, take a look around your living room and kitchen to conduct a "Microprocessor Hunt." Identify five household appliances that contain microprocessors (such as your DSTV/cable decoder, microwave oven, washing machine, digital electric prepaid meter, or smart television). Explain to them that before the Fourth Generation of computers, each of these appliances would have required room-sized cabinets of vacuum tubes or expensive discrete circuits to operate. Discuss how the microprocessor turned computing into an invisible, indispensable helper in daily family life.
