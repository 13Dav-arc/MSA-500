Week 02: Ancient Methods of Transmitting Information

Foundational curriculum module in Information Technology covering Ancient Methods of Transmitting Information for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Imagine standing in a peaceful Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa village in the 16th century at dusk. Suddenly, a sharp, resonant sound cuts through the evening air: Kpom! Kpom! Kpom! Every conversation halts, mothers hush their babies, and elders look toward the village square. A man wearing woven cloth and holding an iron gong clears his throat and shouts: "Hear ye! Hear ye! The King commands all young men to gather at the palace at dawn with their machetes for communal farm clearing!" That man was the Town Crier—the living, breathing newspaper and television station of traditional Africa! Before cell towers, satellite dishes, and fiber optic cables were invented, human communities relied on clever acoustic and visual inventions to send news across valleys, rivers, and forests. How did our ancestors transmit information using only drums, smoke, whistles, and spoken words? Let us journey into the fascinating world of Ancient Methods of Information Transmission!

Key Terms

Ancient Methods of Information Transmission Traditional, non-electronic techniques developed by early societies to communicate messages across short or moderate distances using physical human effort, acoustic sounds, or optical signals.
Oral Method The direct verbal transmission of information, folklore, histories, and news by word of mouth from one person to another or from generation to generation.
Town Crier An official community messenger appointed by traditional rulers or village councils to broadcast public royal proclamations and community news using an iron gong or bell.
Talking Drum A traditional acoustic percussion instrument (such as the Yoruba *Gangan* or Igbo *Ikoro*) engineered to mimic the tonal pitch, accents, and rhythms of human speech to send messages across distances.
Smoke Signals (Fire Lighting) An ancient visual communication technique where smoke puffs or sequential hilltop bonfires were used to transmit prearranged alerts of danger, invasion, or victory across long sightlines.
Physical Representations The use of tangible cultural symbols, objects, or marks (such as fresh palm fronds or tied grass knots) placed in physical locations to convey specific legal, spiritual, or territorial warnings.

Key Ancient Methods of Information Transmission

1. The Oral Method (Word of Mouth & Messengers)

Mechanism: The oldest form of communication. Information is spoken directly using human vocal cords.
Applications: Storytelling, family genealogies, moral folklore passed to children around evening fires, and trained royal foot messengers sent between kingdoms.
Limitation: Highly vulnerable to the "broken telephone" effect—details, dates, and names easily become distorted or exaggerated as rumors pass from person to person.

2. Town Crying (The Royal Proclamation)

Mechanism: An appointed official walks through village quarters beating a resonant iron bell or double-iron gong (ogbene or koge in Igbo, agogo in Yoruba). The metallic clang echoes through the village to command immediate silence before the crier delivers the king’s announcement.
Applications: Announcing royal decrees, community tax collections, market day changes, burial announcements, and communal labor tasks.
Limitation: The town crier had to walk long distances on foot; bad weather or heavy rain prevented villagers from hearing announcements clearly.

3. Beating Drums (The Talking Drum)

Mechanism: African languages are tonal—meaning the pitch (high, mid, low) of a spoken syllable determines its meaning. Traditional drum makers developed hourglass-shaped drums with leather tension cords (such as the Yoruba Gangan) or massive hollowed slit-logs (such as the Igbo Ikoro). By squeezing the drum strings under the arm, the drummer varied pitch to mimic spoken phrases precisely.
Applications: Warning neighboring settlements of approaching raiders, announcing the death of a king, celebrating harvest festivals, and rallying warriors for defense.
Range: Large slit-drums could be heard echoing through forest canopies up to 10 kilometers away.

4. Fire Lighting & Smoke Signals

Mechanism: Used since ancient times across Africa, ancient Greece (the Trojan War), and Native America. By building bonfires on sequential hilltops and covering the flames with damp grass, users created distinct puffs of dark smoke visible across vast distances during the day, or bright firelight at night.
Applications: Prearranged emergency warnings: enemy invasion, approaching storm, or military victory.
Limitation: Extremely low data capacity; could only transmit a single prearranged binary message (e.g., Smoke = Danger). Useless in thick fog, heavy rainstorms, or heavy overcast skies.

5. Whistling & Flute Blowing

Mechanism: Using fingers, hollow wooden whistles, or animal horns (such as elephant tusk ivory horns) to produce high-pitched acoustic signals that pierce through dense forest canopies.
Applications: Used by hunters in the bush to coordinate tracking without scaring game, or to alert villagers across deep river ravines.

6. Drawing Diagrams & Making Symbolic Representations

Mechanism: Before written alphabet scripts emerged, humans drew pictorial diagrams on cave walls, tree barks, and clay tablets to record territorial boundaries, astronomical events, and dangerous hunting paths.
Physical Representations in Nigeria:
*Fresh Yellow Palm Fronds (Omu):* In traditional Igbo and Yoruba societies, tying fresh palm fronds across a farm boundary or around a vehicle meant the land was under sacred legal dispute or the vehicle was carrying a corpse. Everyone understood the non-verbal warning immediately without a single word being spoken!
Stones & Sticks on Paths: Arranged in directional arrows to guide traveling hunting parties.

Home Application & Guardian Sync

Together with your parent or guardian, discuss the traditional communication methods of your ancestral culture: 1. Ask About Local Instruments: Ask your guardian: "What was the name of the traditional drum or royal horn used to summon people in our ancestral village?" (e.g., the Gangan, Bata, or Dundun in Yoruba; the Ikoro or Ogene in Igbo; the Kakaki or Tambari in Hausa). 2. Explore Traditional Symbols: Ask them to describe traditional symbolic markers used in their youth—such as tying leaves across a door or placing palm fronds on a broken-down lorry on the highway. Discuss why drivers in Nigeria still use tree branches on the road today as an ancient warning symbol!
