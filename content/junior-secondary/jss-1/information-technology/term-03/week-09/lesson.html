Key Terms

Digital Citizenship The norms of appropriate, responsible, ethical, legal, and empowered behavior regarding technology use, digital communication, and online community participation.
Cyber Safety (Online Safety) The collection of habits, protective practices, and technical measures used by individuals to protect their personal privacy, digital devices, and emotional well-being from online hazards.
Phishing A deceptive social engineering cyber-attack where criminals impersonate trustworthy institutions (such as banks, schools, or email providers) via fraudulent emails, SMS, or fake websites to steal passwords, PINs, or financial details.
Malware (Malicious Software) Any destructive software program developed to intentionally infiltrate, damage, disrupt, spy on, or gain unauthorized control over a computer system (e.g., viruses, worms, trojans, ransomware).
Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) / Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) A cybersecurity verification process that requires a user to provide two separate forms of identification before gaining account access (e.g., a secret password plus an instant OTP code sent to a mobile phone).
Personally Identifiable Information (PII) Any sensitive personal data that can be used to distinguish, trace, or identify an individual’s identity (such as full name, home address, National Identification Number, date of birth, and biometric data).

Cyber Defense and Digital Safety: Safeguarding personal identity, data privacy, and online security

---

Core Rules of Cyber Safety

1. Protecting Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

Never publicly broadcast sensitive personal details on public social media platforms, gaming chat rooms, or unverified websites:

Private Data to Guard: Full legal name, residential home address, current physical location, school name and uniform pictures, personal telephone number, parent bank details, and your 11-digit National Identification Number (NIN).
Why It Matters: Cybercriminals compile scattered public details to execute identity theft, answer security questions, or track a student's physical location.

2. The Golden Rules of Password Security

Your password is the digital key to your online life:

Length & Complexity: Make passwords at least 10 to 12 characters long, combining uppercase letters (`A–Z`), lowercase letters (`a–z`), numbers (`0–9`), and symbols (`! @ # $ % ^ & *`).
Avoid Guessable Information: Never use birthdays, telephone numbers, your pet's name, or sequential numbers (e.g., `password123` or `ade12345`).
Never Reuse Passwords: Use unique passwords for different accounts so that if one website suffers a data breach, your other accounts remain secure.
Activate Two-Factor Authentication (2FA): Always enable 2FA on email, school portals, and messaging apps. Even if a thief steals your password, they cannot log in without the second secret code sent to your phone.

3. Recognizing Phishing Scams

Phishing is the most widespread cybercrime targeting everyday citizens:

How It Works: You receive an urgent email or SMS stating: "URGENT: Your bank account will be blocked within 24 hours due to BVN failure! Click this link immediately to verify your details!"
The Red Flags:
Creates artificial panic and urgency ("Act within 10 minutes or lose your account!").
Comes from suspicious, misspelled email addresses (e.g., `support@zen1th-bank-alert.com` instead of the genuine official bank domain).
Asks for secret credentials (banks NEVER ask for your ATM PIN or password via email or phone!).
Contains poor spelling, awkward grammar, and suspicious clickable links.

4. Understanding Malware Types

Computer Virus: Malicious code that attaches itself to legitimate program files and replicates when the program is run.
Worm: Standalone malware that self-replicates across computer networks automatically without requiring user intervention.
Trojan Horse: Deceptive malware disguised as a harmless, attractive file (such as a free video game or screen saver) that secretly installs backdoors or spyware once opened.
Ransomware: Highly dangerous malware that encrypts a victim’s personal files, locking them until the victim pays extortion money to the cybercriminals.
