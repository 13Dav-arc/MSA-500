Week 04: First Aid and Rescue Operations

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering First Aid and Rescue Operations for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Even in the most disciplined workshop where safety rules are strictly enforced, an unexpected slip of a chisel, a sudden splash of hot solder, or an accidental contact with a frayed electric wire can cause sudden injury. In those frantic first moments, waiting thirty minutes for an ambulance or a doctor without taking action can turn a simple cut into severe blood loss or allow minor shock to deteriorate into respiratory failure. Knowing how to open the workshop first aid box, stop arterial bleeding, soothe a chemical burn, or assist in a coordinated building evacuation is not merely academic knowledge—it is the direct, life-saving difference between rapid recovery and permanent disability.

Key Terms

First Aid The immediate, temporary care and treatment administered to an injured or suddenly ill person before the arrival of a qualified physician or professional emergency medical services.
First Aid Box A designated, clearly labeled container or wall cabinet containing sterile medical supplies, antiseptics, and instruments needed to deliver emergency first aid.
Rescue Operations Organized emergency procedures and physical actions executed to free, protect, and evacuate individuals trapped in hazardous situations such as fires, building collapses, or toxic gas leaks.
Antiseptic A chemical substance (such as iodine, hydrogen peroxide, or methylated spirit) applied to living tissue and open wounds to kill pathogens and prevent septic infection.
Shock (Physiological) A critical bodily state resulting from trauma, severe burns, blood loss, or electric contact, characterized by a sudden drop in blood pressure and inadequate oxygen delivery to vital organs.

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Definition, Principles, and Objectives of First Aid

First Aid is the initial assistance given to a casualty at the scene of an injury before full medical care is accessed:

Primary Objectives of First Aid (The Three Ps):
1.
Preserve Life: Carry out urgent, life-saving interventions such as clearing an obstructed airway, restoring breathing, and arresting massive hemorrhage (bleeding).
2.
Prevent Deterioration: Prevent the victim's physical condition from worsening by dressing open wounds, stabilizing bone fractures, and keeping the casualty warm and calm.
3.
Promote Recovery: Relieve acute pain, reassure the victim, comfort their shock, and prepare them for a safe transition to a hospital.
The Golden Rules of the First Aider:
Check for danger first; never become a second casualty by rushing into live electrical currents or poisonous fumes.
Act with speed, calm confidence, and quiet reassurance.
Prioritize injuries systematically: treat breathing difficulties first, severe bleeding second, burns third, and fractures fourth.

Aspect 2: The Workshop First Aid Box (Contents and Uses)

Every school technology workshop must have an accessible, clearly marked white or green box marked with a red or green cross. The standard contents include:

Dressings and Bandages:
Sterile Gauze Pads & Cotton Wool: Used for cleaning wound margins, soaking up blood, and padding open wounds.
Adhesive Plasters (Band-Aids): Pre-medicated strips used to cover and protect small punctures, cuts, and scrapes.
Roller Bandages: Used to bind dressings over bleeding wounds and provide pressure.
Triangular Bandage: Used to create arm slings to immobilize fractured forearms, wrists, or collarbones.
Antiseptics and Lotions:
Methylated Spirit / Hydrogen Peroxide: Used to disinfect skin around wounds and sterilize surgical scissors.
Gentian Violet / Iodine Tincture: Antiseptic solutions applied to open abrasions to prevent microbial growth.
Calamine Lotion / Burn Gel: Soothing topicals applied to soothe heat rashes, insect bites, or minor first-degree burns.
Embrocation / Methyl Salicylate Liniment: Rubbed on closed muscular strains and sprains to relieve aches.
Instruments and Medication:
Blunt-ended Surgical Scissors: Used to cut cloth, adhesive tape, and gauze without puncturing skin.
Forceps / Tweezers: Used to extract wood splinters or small glass chips from superficial skin.
Safety Pins: Used to secure loose ends of roller and triangular bandages.
Mild Analgesics (Paracetamol / Aspirin): Pain-relieving tablets administered for headaches, toothaches, or mild trauma.

Aspect 3: First Aid Protocols for Common Workshop Injuries

Technicians must know the immediate sequential treatment for specific workshop accidents:

1.
Cuts and Bleeding (Lacerations):
Wash hands and apply firm, direct pressure over the wound using a clean gauze pad to stop blood flow.
Elevate the bleeding limb above the level of the casualty’s heart (if no fracture is suspected) to reduce blood pressure at the wound.
Clean the surrounding skin outward with an antiseptic-soaked cotton swab.
Secure a sterile pressure dressing with a roller bandage. Seek medical attention if bleeding persists or the cut is deep.
1.
Burns and Scalds:
First-degree Heat Burn (Red, unbroken skin): Immediately immerse or flood the affected area under clean, cold running water for at least $10$ to $15$ minutes. Do not apply grease, oil, butter, or engine paste.
Chemical Burn: Flood the affected area with large volumes of clean water for $20$ minutes to dilute and flush the acid or solvent. Cover loosely with a dry, clean dressing.
1.
Electric Shock:
Step 1: Do NOT touch the victim directly with bare hands while they remain in contact with the live current.
Step 2: Switch off the main electrical circuit breaker immediately. If the switch cannot be reached, separate the victim from the live conductor using a dry wooden broom handle, dry timber board, or rubber bar.
Step 3: Check for breathing and pulse. If breathing has stopped, commence mouth-to-mouth resuscitation (CPR). Treat for burns and shock, and call for emergency medical services.
1.
Fainting (Syncope):
Lay the casualty flat on their back on the floor.
Elevate their feet approximately $30\text{ cm}$ above heart level to encourage blood flow back to the brain.
Loosen tight collars, neckties, belts, and waistbands.
Ensure adequate ventilation by opening doors and dispersing crowded onlookers. Do not pour water over the victim’s face.

Aspect 4: The Concept and Organization of Rescue Operations

A rescue operation is an organized emergency intervention conducted to extract people from life-threatening environments:

Typical Workshop Rescue Scenarios:
Fire outbreaks and smoke inhalation trapped inside rooms.
Structural collapse of walls or heavy workshop shelving onto workers.
Entanglement of clothing or limbs inside heavy motorized machinery.
Hazardous chemical spills releasing toxic or asphyxiating fumes.
Key Objectives of a Rescue Team:
Remove victims safely from the source of danger with minimal physical trauma.
Stabilize life-threatening injuries on site prior to relocation.
Coordinate rapid evacuation to medical care facilities.

Aspect 5: Sequential Steps in a Rescue Operation

A successful rescue follows a strict four-phase procedure:

1.
Phase 1: Scene Assessment and Hazard Control:
The rescuer must survey the area to identify active hazards (live wires, falling debris, explosive gases).
Eliminate or isolate the hazard (e.g., cut off the main electrical power, shut the gas cylinder valve).
1.
Phase 2: Triage and Casualty Access:
Reach the victim safely using specialized access techniques.
Assess responsiveness, airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs of resuscitation).
1.
Phase 3: Safe Extraction and Stabilization:
Free trapped limbs carefully without aggravating spinal or skeletal injuries.
Use stretchers, firemen's lifts, or blanket drags to evacuate casualties away from smoke and structural collapse.
1.
Phase 4: Medical Handover:
Transfer the rescued victims systematically to emergency medical technicians (EMTs), providing a clear report of the hazard, vital signs, and treatments applied.

Comparing Emergency Treatments and Medical Responses

Thermal Reaction

Cold Water Treatment vs Ointment / Butter Application

Running cold water cools the burn, stops thermal tissue destruction, and relieves pain, whereas applying greasy ointments, butter, or paste traps heat within the skin and promotes bacterial infection.

Hemorrhage Control

Direct Pressure vs Elevation

Direct pressure compresses ruptured blood vessels to form clots at the wound site, while limb elevation reduces hydrostatic blood pressure feeding the injured limb.

Conductor Isolation

Dry Wooden Handle vs Bare Hand Contact

A dry wooden handle is a non-conductive electrical insulator used to push away live wires safely, while bare hands conduct electricity and transfer the lethal shock to the rescuer.

Fainting vs Shock Management

Flat Recumbent Position with Elevated Feet vs Sitting Upright

Laying a fainting victim flat with elevated legs directs oxygenated blood directly to the brain, whereas forcing them to sit upright starves the brain of blood and prolongs unconsciousness.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Pouring cold water over a person who is still in contact with a live electrical current.

Scientific Fact

Water conducts electricity; pouring water on an energized victim electrocutes everyone standing nearby. Turn off the mains first.

Common Mistake

Rubbing raw butter, palm oil, or motor grease onto a fresh workshop burn.

Scientific Fact

Greases and oils insulate the burn, holding heat in the flesh and breeding bacterial infections; flush exclusively with clean cold water.

Common Mistake

Forcing an unconscious or semi-conscious casualty to drink water or soda.

Scientific Fact

Liquids can enter the trachea (windpipe) and choke an unconscious person; never give oral fluids to an unconscious casualty.

Common Mistake

Removing large, deeply embedded foreign objects (like a deep metal shard) from a wound on the workshop floor.

Scientific Fact

Pulling out an embedded object can unseal severed blood vessels and cause fatal hemorrhage; pad around the object to stabilize it and let surgeons remove it in a hospital.
