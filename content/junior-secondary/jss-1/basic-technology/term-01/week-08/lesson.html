Week 08: Materials and Processing (Ceramics and Glass)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Materials and Processing (Ceramics and Glass) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Every time you drink from a smooth porcelain mug, step onto glazed bathroom floor tiles, switch on a wall lamp, or look out through a transparent window, you are interacting with materials that began as simple mud, sand, and clay dug straight from the earth. Unlike metals that bend or timber that rots and burns, ceramics and glass can withstand blistering furnace temperatures, resist corrosive acids, and block dangerous high-voltage electrical currents. Yet, drop a ceramic teacup or strike a glass pane with a pebble, and it shatters into razor-sharp shards in an instant. Understanding the raw materials, manufacturing stages, thermal properties, and mechanical brittleness of ceramics and glass reveals why these ancient earthen materials remain vital in high-tech aerospace, civil construction, and electrical engineering.

Key Terms

Ceramics Non-metallic, inorganic engineering solids manufactured by shaping moist clay and earthy minerals, followed by permanent hardening through high-temperature baking in a kiln.
Glass A hard, brittle, non-crystalline (amorphous) transparent or translucent solid produced by fusing silica sand, soda ash, and limestone together at intense temperatures.
Clay A fine-grained, earthy natural soil rich in hydrous aluminum silicates that becomes soft and plastic when mixed with water, allowing it to be molded into shapes that retain their form when baked.
Kiln A specialized, thermally insulated high-temperature industrial furnace or oven used for drying, baking, and firing ceramic wares and bricks.
Glazing The application of an impervious, glassy chemical coating onto the surface of a ceramic object, which melts during firing to make the product smooth, waterproof, and decorative.

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Origin, Raw Materials, and Meaning of Ceramics

The word ceramics originates from the Greek word keramos, meaning "potter’s clay" or "burnt earth":

In engineering and materials technology, ceramics are defined as inorganic, non-metallic solids formed through the action of intense heat.
Primary Raw Materials:
Clay (Hydrous Aluminum Silicate): The fundamental raw material. Common varieties include kaolin (pure white china clay used for fine porcelain) and terracotta/ball clay (iron-rich red or brown clay used for bricks and pottery).
Feldspar: A mineral that melts during firing to act as a natural flux and binder.
Silica / Quartz Sand: Provides structural rigidity and reduces shrinkage during firing.
Cement and Mud: Used for structural bonding and traditional building blocks.

Aspect 2: Manufacturing Stages of Ceramic Products

Converting raw natural clay into a durable, functional ceramic object follows a strict sequence:

1.
Clay Winning and Excavation: Digging suitable raw clay deposits from riverbanks, valleys, and quarries, followed by crushing and screening to remove pebbles, roots, and debris.
2.
Kneading and Wedging (Preparation): Mixing the clay powder with water and working it thoroughly by hand or in a pug mill to remove trapped air bubbles and achieve uniform plasticity.
3.
Forming and Shaping (Molding): Shaping the plastic clay into the desired component using methods such as:
Pinch and Coil Method: Traditional hand-building techniques.
Potter's Wheel (Throwing): Rotating clay on a revolving turntable to form symmetrical hollow cylindrical items (vases, cups, bowls).
Press Molding / Slip Casting: Pouring liquid clay slip into plaster-of-Paris molds for mass-producing complex sanitary wares (toilet bowls, sinks).
1.
Drying (Greenware Stage): Allowing the wet molded clay to dry slowly in the shade to remove free moisture. Rapid drying under hot sun causes severe cracking and warping.
2.
Firing (Baking in a Kiln): Heating the dried clay inside a kiln to temperatures ranging from $900^\circ\text{C}$ to over $1300^\circ\text{C}$. Firing chemically fuses the mineral particles together, permanently transforming soft clay into hard, rock-like biscuit ware.
3.
Glazing and Refiring: Dipping or spraying the biscuit ware with a liquid mineral glaze formulation and firing it a second time. The glaze melts into a glassy, impermeable, vitreous skin that seals pores and makes the product completely waterproof and hygienic.
The Ceramic Manufacturing Cycle: From Raw Clay to Glazed Ceramic Products

Aspect 3: Properties of Ceramic Materials

Engineers deploy ceramics in harsh environments because of several distinct physical and chemical characteristics:

High Compressive Strength: Capable of supporting enormous downward crushing loads without deforming; ideal for building foundation bricks and load-bearing walls.
Extreme Brittleness (Low Tensile Strength): Ceramics lack ductility and cannot bend. When subjected to sudden shock, tension, or impact, they fracture and shatter immediately without plastic deformation.
High Heat Resistance (Refractory Quality): Possess exceptionally high melting points (often exceeding $1600^\circ\text{C}$), allowing them to retain physical shape and strength at temperatures that melt steel.
Electrical and Thermal Insulation: The rigid ionic and covalent atomic bonds do not allow electrons or heat to move freely, making ceramics outstanding non-conductors.
Chemical Inertness and Corrosion Resistance: Highly resistant to acids, alkalis, moisture, and atmospheric oxidation; ceramics do not rust, tarnish, or rot.

Aspect 4: Glass: Composition, Manufacturing, and Types

Glass is a special sub-class of ceramic materials characterized by an amorphous (non-crystalline) atomic structure:

Raw Materials of Glass:
Silica Sand ($\text{SiO}_2$): The primary network-forming ingredient (accounts for roughly $70\%$ to $75\%$).
Soda Ash (Sodium Carbonate, $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$): Acts as a flux to lower the melting point of silica sand from over $2000^\circ\text{C}$ down to approximately $1400^\circ\text{C}$.
Limestone (Calcium Carbonate, $\text{CaCO}_3$): Added to make the resulting glass water-insoluble and chemically durable.
Manufacturing Process: The raw dry batch is melted inside a regenerative glass furnace at temperatures between $1400^\circ\text{C}$ and $1600^\circ\text{C}$. The viscous, molten liquid is then shaped by floating on molten tin (float process for flat sheet glass), blowing into molds (bottles), or drawing through rollers. When cooled rapidly, the atoms freeze in an irregular arrangement, producing clear transparency.
Types of Engineering Glass:
Sheet / Float Glass: Ordinary flat glass used for domestic window panes, picture frames, and glass tabletops.
Tempered (Toughened) Glass: Heated to near-melting temperature and rapidly cooled with blasts of cold air (quenched). This induces compressive surface stresses that make the glass four to five times stronger than ordinary glass. When shattered, it breaks safely into tiny, blunt granular pebbles rather than jagged dagger-like shards.
Laminated Safety Glass: Formed by sandwiching a tough, clear polymer interlayer (polyvinyl butyral [PVB]) between two sheets of glass under heat and pressure. Used for automobile windscreens; when struck, broken glass adheres firmly to the plastic interlayer without spraying inward.

Aspect 5: Practical Applications of Ceramics and Glass

The unique thermal, optical, and electrical properties of ceramics and glass make them irreplaceable across various industries:

1.
Domestic and Sanitary Ware: Glazed porcelain plates, bowls, drinking mugs, bathtubs, washbasins, and ceramic water-closet (WC) toilet bowls.
2.
Civil Building and Construction: Burnt clay structural bricks, roofing tiles, ceramic floor and wall tiles, sanitary drainage pipes, and window glazing.
3.
Electrical and Power Engineering:
High-Voltage Overhead Insulators: Corrugated porcelain disc insulators mounted on high-voltage national grid transmission towers to isolate live cables from steel pylons.
Automotive Spark Plugs: High-grade alumina ceramic casings designed to insulate $25,000\text{ V}$ electrical surges inside firing engine cylinders.
1.
Refractory and Heavy Industrial Linings: Firebricks and alumina-silica refractory blocks used to line the internal walls of steel blast furnaces, cement rotary kilns, and glass melting tanks.
2.
Optics, Communication, and Safety: Corrective eyeglass lenses, microscope optics, optical fiber cables (transmitting digital data at the speed of light across oceans), and bullet-resistant laminated vehicle glass.

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Traditional Ceramics (Pottery/Brick) | Red clay, water, mud, sand | High compressive strength, porous before glaze, brittle | Building bricks, flower pots, cooking pots |

| Porcelain / White Ceramics | Kaolin (white clay), feldspar, quartz | Non-porous, smooth, high electrical resistance | High-voltage power insulators, sanitary sinks, fine dishes |

| Laminated Safety Glass | Glass panes + PVB polymer interlayer | Holds together upon impact, shatter-proof | Automobile windscreens, bullet-resistant security booths |

Comparing Ceramics and Glass Across Engineering Properties

Atomic Structure

Crystalline Structure vs Amorphous (Non-Crystalline) Structure

Traditional ceramics possess a crystalline atomic lattice formed during kiln firing, whereas glass possesses an amorphous, disordered liquid-like atomic structure that allows light to pass through without scattering.

Optical Transmission

Opaque / Translucent vs Completely Transparent

Most ceramic wares are opaque or faintly translucent due to crystalline boundaries and mineral impurities, whereas silica-based glasses are manufactured to be optically transparent.

Thermal Resistance

Refractory Firebricks vs Commercial Soda-Lime Glass

Refractory ceramics withstand continuous temperatures exceeding $1500^\circ\text{C}$ without melting or softening, whereas ordinary soda-lime glass softens and loses shape between $500^\circ\text{C}$ and $700^\circ\text{C}$.

Safety Fracture

Tempered Glass vs Laminated Glass

Tempered glass shatters completely into hundreds of tiny, blunt granular cubes when breached, whereas laminated glass retains its structural sheet form because cracked pieces remain bonded to the internal plastic interlayer.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that because ceramics shatter easily, they possess low strength.

Scientific Fact

Ceramics possess exceptionally high compressive strength (supporting massive downward crushing loads), but very low tensile and shear strength, causing them to fracture under sudden impacts or bending forces.

Common Mistake

Thinking that glazing is applied purely for decorative painting and color.

Scientific Fact

Unglazed fired clay (biscuit ware) is porous and absorbs liquids; the primary engineering purpose of glazing is to seal microscopic surface pores, making the ceramic waterproof, stain-proof, and hygienic.

Common Mistake

Assuming ordinary window sheet glass can be installed as an automobile windscreen.

Scientific Fact

Ordinary sheet glass shatters into razor-sharp, dagger-like shards that can decapitate or severely injure vehicle passengers; windscreens must strictly be made of laminated safety glass.

Common Mistake

Drying wet molded clay rapidly in hot midday sun to save time.

Scientific Fact

Rapid surface drying causes uneven shrinkage between the dry exterior and moist interior, leading to catastrophic warping and cracking before the product ever reaches the kiln.
