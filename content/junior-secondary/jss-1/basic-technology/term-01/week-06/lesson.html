Week 06: Materials and Processing (Wood)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Materials and Processing (Wood) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Walk into any home, school, or office, and wood is everywhere: the roof trusses supporting heavy concrete tiles, the desks and chairs in your classroom, polished doors, dining tables, and even the paper in your notebooks. Wood has served humanity as an indispensable construction material for thousands of years because it is strong, workable, renewable, and aesthetically warm. However, wood is not a uniform, manufactured synthetic block; it is an organic material harvested from living trees. Understanding how a tree grows, how its cross-section is structured, and the critical differences between broad-leaved hardwoods and needle-leaved softwoods is essential before any carpenter or engineer picks up a saw.

Key Terms

Timber The wood of felled and seasoned trees prepared for use in carpentry, joinery, furniture making, and structural building construction.
Hardwood Wood obtained from broad-leaved, angiosperm deciduous trees whose seeds are enclosed within a fruit or flower (e.g., Iroko, Mahogany, Obeche).
Softwood Wood obtained from cone-bearing, needle-leaved gymnosperm evergreen trees whose naked seeds are held in cones (e.g., Pine, Cedar, Fir).
Heartwood The dark, inactive central core of a tree trunk that provides mechanical support, rigidity, and natural resistance against insect and fungal attacks.
Cambium Layer The microscopic layer of living, dividing cells situated between the inner bark and sapwood responsible for the secondary growth and diameter expansion of the tree.

Core Concept

Aspect 1: The Tree as the Primary Source of Timber

Wood is an organic tissue obtained from the stems, branches, and trunks of living trees. A growing tree performs three vital physiological functions: its root system anchors the plant and absorbs water with mineral salts; its trunk (stem) elevates the branches and transports sap; and its leaves synthesize food through photosynthesis. When a mature tree is felled, stripped of its branches, and converted into commercial sizes, the resulting material is called timber or lumber.

Aspect 2: Internal Structure and Cross-Section of a Tree Trunk

Examining a transverse cross-section cut across a mature tree trunk reveals several concentric circular zones, each serving a distinct physiological purpose:

1.
The Outer Bark (Cortex):
The dry, rough, fibrous outer covering of the tree.
Functions as an external protective skin shielding the living inner tissues from physical damage, weather extremes, pest infestations, and excessive water evaporation.
1.
The Inner Bark (Phloem):
A soft, moist layer located immediately beneath the outer bark.
Transports synthesized liquid food (sugars) downward from the green leaves to nourish the growing roots, trunk, and cambium cells.
1.
The Cambium Layer:
A microscopic, delicate layer of active, rapidly dividing reproductive cells situated between the inner bark and the sapwood.
Produces new phloem cells outward and new sapwood xylem cells inward, driving the tree's diameter growth.
1.
The Sapwood (Xylem):
The lighter-colored outer zone of the wood surrounding the heartwood.
Contains active, living cells that conduct water and dissolved minerals upward from the roots to the leaves.
It has high moisture content, is less dense, and is susceptible to decay and insect attack if not treated with preservatives.
1.
The Heartwood (Duramen):
The mature, darker, and denser core located inside the center of the trunk.
Formed as older inner sapwood cells become clogged with resins, tannins, gums, and chemical extracts, halting sap conduction.
Heartwood represents the most valuable portion of the tree for construction and furniture because of its exceptional strength, hardness, and durability.
1.
The Pith (Medulla):
The small, soft, spongy central core of the tree trunk formed during the sapling's initial primary growth.
It has no commercial timber value and is often prone to rot.
1.
Annual Growth Rings:
Concentric rings formed by the seasonal activity of the cambium layer.
In temperate regions, rapid spring growth produces light, porous early wood, while dry summer growth produces dense, dark late wood.
Counting these concentric rings provides an estimate of the tree's age in years.
1.
Medullary Rays:
Thin cellular ribbons radiating outward like wheel spokes from the central pith to the bark.
Store nutrients and carry food horizontally across the trunk diameter while binding annual rings together.

Aspect 3: Classification of Wood: Hardwoods vs. Softwoods

In materials science and technical education, wood is classified botanically into two major divisions: Hardwoods and Softwoods. This classification is based on botanical taxonomy, seed structure, and foliage, rather than physical hardness alone:

1.
Hardwoods (Deciduous / Angiosperms):
Sourced from broad-leaved trees that usually shed their leaves annually in dry or cold seasons (deciduous trees).
Botanically classified as angiosperms, producing seeds that are enclosed in a seed vessel, nut, or fruit.
Characterized by complex, compact cellular structures featuring specialized hollow pores or vessels for sap transport.
Generally heavier, denser, and darker in color, requiring longer growth cycles ($60$ to $100+$ years to mature).
Prominent Nigerian Hardwood Species:
Iroko (African Teak): Highly durable, golden-brown, resistant to termites and rot; used for exterior joinery, marine decking, and structural beams.
Mahogany: Reddish-brown, fine grain, easy to work and polish; used for high-end furniture, cabinetry, and interior paneling.
Obeche: Light straw-colored, soft, light-weight, easy to carve; used for model making, ceiling boards, and plywood core veneers.
Afara, Teak, Ebony, and Mansonia.
1.
Softwoods (Coniferous / Gymnosperms):
Sourced from evergreen trees with thin, needle-like or scale-like leaves that remain green year-round.
Botanically classified as gymnosperms (meaning "naked seeds"), bearing seeds exposed on the woody scales of cones.
Composed of simpler, uniform cell tissues called tracheids, lacking the distinct open pores found in hardwoods.
Generally faster-growing ($20$ to $40$ years), lighter in weight, lighter in color (yellowish to pale cream), and easier to saw, nail, and screw.
Common Softwood Species:
Pine, Cedar, Spruce, Fir, and Larch.
Widely used for paper pulp, building scaffolding, roof trusses, packing crates, matchsticks, and fiberboard.

Aspect 4: Physical and Mechanical Properties of Wood

Engineers evaluate specific properties when selecting timber for engineering tasks:

Density and Weight: The ratio of mass to volume. Dense timbers (such as Iroko and Ebony) resist denting and wear, whereas low-density timbers (such as Obeche and Balsa) are favored for lightweight interior fixtures.
Grain Pattern and Texture: The directional arrangement, size, and alignment of wood fibers. Straight grains are easy to plane, while interlocked or wavy grains yield decorative patterns on veneer surfaces.
Moisture Content and Shrinkage: Freshly felled green timber contains over $50\%$ water by weight. As wood dries (seasons), it shrinks across its width and thickness, which can cause warping, splitting, or twisting if unmonitored.
Tensile and Compressive Strength: Wood exhibits high tensile strength along its grain (resisting pulling loads) and high compressive strength parallel to fibers (acting as load-bearing structural columns).
Durability and Permeability: The ability of heartwood extractives to resist fungal dry-rot, wood-boring beetles, and subterranean termites.

Comparing Hardwoods and Softwoods Across Key Engineering Metrics

Botanical Foliage

Broad Leaves vs Needle-Like Leaves

Hardwoods originate from broad-leaved deciduous trees that drop leaves seasonally, while softwoods originate from evergreen trees bearing needle-like or scale-like leaves.

Seed Formation

Enclosed Fruit Seeds (Angiosperms) vs Naked Cone Seeds (Gymnosperms)

Hardwood seeds are enclosed in fleshy fruits, pods, or hard shells, whereas softwood seeds are naked and borne exposed on the scales of cones.

Cellular Anatomy

Pores and Vessels vs Uniform Tracheids

Hardwoods contain specialized vascular pores and vessels that give them distinct surface grain rings, whereas softwoods consist of uniform, tubular tracheid cells without true pores.

Growth Rate & Density

Slow Growth High Density vs Fast Growth Lower Density

Hardwoods mature slowly over many decades producing dense, durable, and dark timber, while softwoods grow rapidly, yielding lighter, paler, and more resinous lumber.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Assuming that every hardwood is physically hard and every softwood is physically soft.

Scientific Fact

The classification is botanical, not mechanical. For example, Balsa is botanically a hardwood (broad-leaved angiosperm) but is softer than nearly all softwoods, while Yew is a softwood that is harder than many hardwoods.

Common Mistake

Believing that sapwood is stronger and more durable than heartwood.

Scientific Fact

Sapwood contains live cells and active sugars that attract fungi and termites; heartwood is denser, chemically fortified with tannins, and much more durable for construction.

Common Mistake

Using unseasoned, wet green timber directly for domestic furniture.

Scientific Fact

Freshly cut wood shrinks as it loses moisture, causing furniture joints to crack, twist, and pull apart; wood must be properly seasoned before fabrication.

Common Mistake

Confusing annual growth rings with medullary rays.

Scientific Fact

Annual growth rings form concentric circular bands around the center, whereas medullary rays run radially outward across the rings like bicycle wheel spokes.
