Week 07: Materials and Processing (Metals)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Materials and Processing (Metals) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

From the structural steel skeleton of modern skyscrapers and the copper wiring pulsing electricity through our walls, to the lightweight aluminum wings of commercial airliners, metals form the physical foundation of modern technological civilization. Long before plastics or synthetic resins were synthesized, humanity’s mastery over fire and earth enabled the extraction of molten metals from solid rock. Metals possess a unique combination of mechanical toughness, heat resistance, electrical conductivity, and deformability that no piece of timber can match. Understanding how metals are extracted, how they are classified into ferrous, non-ferrous, and alloyed categories, and how their mechanical properties dictate their use is a fundamental requirement for every aspiring engineer and technician.

Key Terms

Metal An inorganic, mineral-based engineering material characterized by high thermal and electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, luster, and malleability.
Ferrous Metal Any metal or metal alloy that contains iron as its principal constituent element and exhibits magnetic properties (e.g., cast iron, mild steel).
Non-Ferrous Metal Any pure metal or alloy that contains no iron as a base constituent, is non-magnetic, and provides high natural resistance to atmospheric corrosion (e.g., copper, aluminum).
Alloy An intimate metallic mixture or solid solution composed of two or more metals, or a metal fused with a non-metal, designed to achieve enhanced physical or mechanical properties.
Malleability The mechanical property of a metal that allows it to be hammered, rolled, or pressed into thin flat sheets without cracking or shattering.

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Origin and Extraction of Metals

Metals are natural mineral elements found beneath the earth's crust, rarely occurring in their pure metallic state (with the exception of unreactive noble metals like gold and platinum):

Ores: Most commercial metals are found combined with oxygen, sulfur, carbon, or silica in rocks known as mineral ores (e.g., iron ore such as hematite and magnetite; aluminum ore such as bauxite; copper ore such as chalcopyrite).
Smelting and Refining:
Mined mineral ore is crushed, washed, and chemically reduced inside high-temperature metallurgical furnaces (such as the Blast Furnace for iron ore) using coke (carbon) as a reducing agent and limestone as a flux.
The intense heat separates the molten metal from stony impurities (slag).
The crude extracted metal (such as pig iron) is then refined in specialized converter furnaces to remove excess carbon and impurities before being poured into molds or rolling mills as commercial stock.

Aspect 2: Ferrous Metals (Characteristics and Types)

Ferrous metals are defined by the presence of iron ($\text{Fe}$) as their primary chemical constituent:

General Characteristics:
Strongly attracted to permanent magnets (magnetic response).
Prone to rusting (oxidation) when exposed to moisture and atmospheric oxygen unless coated or painted.
Heavy, strong, and capable of being heat-treated to achieve varied levels of hardness and toughness.
Common Types of Ferrous Metals:
1.
Cast Iron:
Contains a high carbon content ($2\%$ to $4\%$).
Extremely hard, highly rigid, and possesses high compressive strength, but is brittle and shatters under sharp hammer blows.
Applications: Machine tool beds (lathe beds), engine cylinder blocks, heavy vice bodies, and manhole covers.
1.
Wrought Iron:
Almost pure elemental iron with less than $0.1\%$ carbon.
Highly ductile, malleable, fibrous, and resists corrosion better than other unalloyed irons.
Applications: Decorative gates, outdoor ornamental railings, crane chains, and agricultural hooks.
1.
Mild Steel (Low-Carbon Steel):
Contains $0.15\%$ to $0.3\%$ carbon.
Ductile, malleable, easy to bend, weld, and machine, but cannot be hardened by direct quenching.
Applications: Structural steel beams (I-beams), automobile body panels, steel pipes, nails, nuts, and bolts.
1.
High-Carbon Steel (Tool Steel):
Contains $0.7\%$ to $1.5\%$ carbon.
Extremely hard and wear-resistant, capable of being tempered to hold a keen cutting edge.
Applications: Cold chisels, hacksaw blades, files, drill bits, shear blades, and hammer heads.

Aspect 3: Non-Ferrous Metals (Characteristics and Types)

Non-ferrous metals are metals that contain no iron:

General Characteristics:
Non-magnetic (not attracted by magnets).
Do not form iron oxide (rust), giving them excellent resistance to atmospheric corrosion.
Generally lighter in weight, more ductile, softer, and possess superior electrical and thermal conductivity compared to ferrous metals.
Common Non-Ferrous Metals:
1.
Copper ($\text{Cu}$):
Distinctive reddish-brown color; exceptional electrical and thermal conductor; highly malleable and ductile.
Applications: Electrical wiring cables, printed circuit board traces, refrigerator cooling coils, and domestic plumbing pipes.
1.
Aluminum ($\text{Al}$):
Silvery-white, lightweight metal (approximately one-third the density of steel); forms a self-protecting oxide film that stops corrosion.
Applications: Aircraft fuselages, overhead high-voltage transmission cables, beverage cans, cooking utensils, and window profiles.
1.
Lead ($\text{Pb}$):
Bluish-grey, very heavy, dense, soft, and flexible with a low melting point ($327^\circ\text{C}$).
Applications: Car battery plates, radiation shielding in X-ray rooms, and chemical tank linings.
1.
Zinc ($\text{Zn}$):
Bluish-white metal used primarily to coat steel sheets (a process called galvanizing) to prevent rusting.
Applications: Galvanized roofing sheets, water buckets, and battery casings.
1.
Tin ($\text{Sn}$):
Soft, silvery, non-toxic metal with a low melting point.
Applications: Coating steel food cans (tin plating) and formulating soft soldering wire.
Classification of Engineering Metals: Ferrous, Non-Ferrous, and Alloys

Aspect 4: Metallic Alloys

An alloy is produced by melting and fusing two or more metals (or a metal and a non-metal like carbon) together to create a material with superior properties that neither pure component possesses alone:

1.
Ferrous Alloys:
Stainless Steel: Steel alloyed with Chromium ($18\%$) and Nickel ($8\%$). The chromium forms an invisible oxide layer that makes the steel immune to rust and staining.
Applications: Surgical scalpels, kitchen sinks, cutlery, and chemical processing vats.
High-Speed Steel (HSS): Steel alloyed with Tungsten, Chromium, and Vanadium; retains cutting hardness even when glowing red from high friction.
Applications: High-speed lathe cutting tools and twist drill bits.
1.
Non-Ferrous Alloys:
Brass: A non-ferrous alloy composed of Copper ($60\%–70\%$) + Zinc ($30\%–40\%$). Golden-yellow, corrosion-resistant, low-friction, and easy to machine.
Applications: Water taps, musical wind instruments (trumpets), electrical plug pins, and marine fittings.
Bronze: An ancient alloy composed of Copper ($90\%$) + Tin ($10\%$). Harder and tougher than brass, with superior resistance to seawater corrosion.
Applications: Ship propellers, church bells, medals, statues, and heavy bearing bushings.
Duralumin: An aerospace alloy composed of Aluminum ($95\%$) + Copper ($4\%$) + trace amounts of Magnesium and Manganese. Combines the light weight of aluminum with the structural strength of mild steel.
Applications: Aircraft structural frames, racing bicycle components, and automotive pistons.
Soft Solder: Formulated from Lead ($50\%$) + Tin ($50\%$) with a low melting point used to fuse electrical wiring and copper plumbing joints.

Aspect 5: Mechanical Properties of Metals

Engineers test and select metals based on distinct mechanical behaviors under applied physical forces:

Ductility: The ability of a metal to be drawn out or stretched into long, thin wires under tensile tension without snapping (e.g., copper and mild steel).
Malleability: The ability of a metal to be hammered, rolled, or shaped in all directions into thin sheets under compression without fracturing (e.g., aluminum, gold, lead).
Hardness: The ability of a metal's surface to resist scratching, abrasion, indentation, or wear by another hard material (e.g., high-carbon steel, cast iron).
Tensile Strength: The capacity of a metal to withstand heavy pulling or stretching loads along its length without tearing apart.
Brittleness: The tendency of a material to fracture, shatter, or snap cleanly under sudden shock or bending without showing prior plastic deformation (e.g., cast iron).
Conductivity: The efficiency with which a metal permits the free transmission of electrical current (electrical conductivity) or thermal energy (thermal conductivity).

Comparing Categories and Properties of Engineering Metals

Chemical Composition

Ferrous Metals vs Non-Ferrous Metals

Ferrous metals contain iron as their principal element and are magnetic, while non-ferrous metals contain no iron, are non-magnetic, and naturally resist atmospheric rust.

Mechanical Behavior

Ductility vs Malleability

Ductility is the capacity of a metal to be drawn out into thin wires under tensile pulling, whereas malleability is the capacity to be beaten into thin flat sheets under compression.

Material Architecture

Pure Elemental Metal vs Metallic Alloy

A pure metal consists of a single metallic chemical element, whereas an alloy is an engineered solid mixture of two or more metals (or a metal and carbon) formulated for superior properties.

Non-Ferrous Alloys

Brass vs Bronze

Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc used for low-friction fittings and musical instruments, while bronze is an alloy of copper and tin used for ship propellers and durable bearing bushings.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Believing that all metals rust.

Scientific Fact

Only ferrous metals (iron and steel) rust, producing reddish-brown iron oxide; non-ferrous metals (like aluminum, copper, and zinc) oxidize on their surface but do not rust.

Common Mistake

Assuming that brass and bronze are identical pure metals.

Scientific Fact

Both are copper-based alloys, but brass is alloyed with zinc, while bronze is alloyed with tin.

Common Mistake

Confusing the mechanical properties of hardness and toughness.

Scientific Fact

Hardness measures resistance to surface scratching and wear, while toughness measures resistance to fracturing under heavy impacts. A file is very hard but brittle; a rubber mallet is tough but soft.

Common Mistake

Thinking that a magnet will stick to all metals.

Scientific Fact

Magnets only attract ferromagnetic metals (chiefly iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel); non-ferrous metals like copper, brass, and aluminum exhibit no magnetic attraction.
