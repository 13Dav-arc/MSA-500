Week 06: Board Practice: Types of Lines and Their Uses

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Board Practice: Types of Lines and Their Uses for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Every line in a technical drawing carries a specific meaning, functioning like words and grammar in a written language. A thick, solid line tells an engineer that an edge is clearly visible, while a line of short dashes reveals hidden chambers inside a machine part that the eye cannot see directly from the outside. Without standardized lines, an artisan would confuse a dimension mark with a solid steel wall or mistake an imaginary centerline for a physical cut. Mastering the standard technical lines—from thick continuous outlines to thin zig-zag break lines and arrowheads—allows you to draft plans that can be read and interpreted across any engineering workshop in the world.

Key Terms

Technical Line Convention The standardized system of varying line thicknesses, patterns, and forms used in technical drawing to convey specific geometric features, boundaries, and measurements[cite: 1].
Thick Continuous Line A bold, solid line used to depict visible outlines and prominent physical edges of an object[cite: 1].
Thin Continuous Line A light, unbroken line used for dimension lines, projection lines, and construction lines[cite: 1].
Thin Long Chain Line A line consisting of alternating long lines and short dashes or dots, used to indicate centerlines, path lines of motion, and cutting planes[cite: 1].
Hidden Detail Line (Thin Short Dashes) A line composed of short, evenly spaced dashes used to represent edges, outlines, and surfaces that are hidden from direct view[cite: 1].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Using the Tee-Square and Set-Squares for Board Practice

Combining the tee-square and set-squares on the drawing board allows draftsmen to produce lines in exact orientations:

1.
Drawing Horizontal Lines:
The stock of the tee-square is held firmly against the left working edge of the board with the non-drawing hand.
Horizontal lines are drawn smoothly along the top edge of the tee-square blade from left to right.
1.
Drawing Vertical Lines:
The set-square sits squarely on top of the horizontal blade of the tee-square.
Vertical lines are drawn upward along the vertical edge of the set-square, producing true perpendicular lines at an angle of $90^\circ$.
1.
Drawing Inclined Lines and Angles:
By sliding a $45^\circ$ or $30^\circ/60^\circ$ set-square along the tee-square blade, inclined lines can be drawn directly at angles of $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$.
Combining both set-squares upon the tee-square enables the drafting of angles such as $15^\circ$ ($45^\circ - 30^\circ$) and $75^\circ$ ($45^\circ + 30^\circ$).
1.
Measuring Dimensions:
Linear lengths along vertical and diagonal lines can be measured and marked directly using the millimeter graduations on the set-square while it rests on the tee-square blade.

Aspect 2: Visible Outlines vs. Construction and Dimension Lines

The contrast between line thicknesses gives technical drawings depth and structural hierarchy:

1.
Thick Continuous Line:
Form: A solid, continuous dark line drawn with an HB or medium pencil.
Uses: Strictly designated for visible outlines and edges of the object being drawn. It defines the physical boundaries of the component that are directly visible to an observer.
1.
Thin Continuous Line:
Form: A faint, sharp, unbroken line drawn using a hard pencil such as 2H.
Uses:
Dimension lines: Lines terminating with arrowheads that indicate the length, width, or diameter of an object.
Projection lines: Lines extending outward from an object feature to show where a dimension begins and ends.
Construction lines: Initial faint layout lines used to position and construct geometric shapes before darkening final outlines.

Aspect 3: Centerlines and Hidden Detail Lines

Lines that represent non-visible or abstract geometric properties follow strict pattern conventions:

1.
Thin Long Chain Line:
Form: Alternating long lines and short dashes or dots (e.g., long line, short gap, short dash, short gap, long line).
Uses:
Indicating centre lines of symmetric features, cylinders, holes, and circles.
Marking path lines for indicating movement of mechanical links or rotating gears.
Representing cutting and viewing planes when an object is sliced open to reveal internal views.
1.
Thin Short Dashes:
Form: A series of short, uniform dashes (approximately $2\text{ mm}$ to $3\text{ mm}$ long with $1\text{ mm}$ spaces between them).
Uses: Strictly used for hidden outlines and edges. When a hole, interior recess, or rear edge is blocked from view by a foreground solid surface, it is drawn using short dashes.

Aspect 4: Break Lines and Boundary Conventions

When drafting long uniform shafts or partial component views, special break lines are employed to save space on the paper sheet:

1.
Thick Continuous Wavy Line:
Form: A thick, freehand wavy line drawn continuously.
Uses: Used for short break lines and boundary lines where an object is broken off irregularly to reveal a cross-section.
1.
Thin Continuous Wavy Line:
Form: A light, thin continuous wavy line.
Uses: Used for limits of partial views when only a portion of a larger component is drawn.
1.
Thin Ruled Line with Short Zig-Zags:
Form: A straight, ruled line interrupted at intervals by small sharp zig-zag strokes.
Uses: Designated for long break lines across lengthy continuous parts (such as long structural pipes or timber beams) to condense their representation within the drawing sheet.

Aspect 5: Dimensioning Conventions and Arrowheads

Dimensioning provides the manufacturing team with exact physical measurements:

Arrowheads:
Form: Pointed triangular terminations constructed with a length approximately three times their width, filled in solid black.
Uses: Strictly used at the end of dimension lines to touch the projection lines and establish the precise boundaries of the measurement.
Dimension Placement:
The thin continuous dimension line is placed parallel to the edge being measured.
Projection lines extend perpendicularly from the feature, leaving a small gap of $1\text{ mm}$ to $2\text{ mm}$ from the outline.
Dimension figures (numbers in millimeters) are lettered either centrally above the dimension line or into an opening in the line.

Comparing Standard Technical Lines and Applications

Line Weight

Thick Continuous vs Thin Continuous Line

Thick continuous lines represent visible outlines and outer boundaries of an object[cite: 1], whereas thin continuous lines represent dimensions, projection guides, and faint construction lines[cite: 1].

Visibility Status

Thick Continuous Line vs Thin Short Dashes

Thick continuous lines show edges that are directly visible to the observer[cite: 1], while thin short dashes indicate hidden interior outlines and obscured edges[cite: 1].

Break Length

Thick Wavy Line vs Thin Ruled Line with Zig-Zags

A thick continuous wavy line is used for short break lines and boundaries[cite: 1], whereas a thin ruled line with short zig-zags is used for long break lines across extended components[cite: 1].

Feature Geometry

Centerline (Chain Line) vs Dimension Line

A thin long chain line marks axes of symmetry and motion paths[cite: 1], while a thin continuous line paired with arrowheads specifies physical numerical distances[cite: 1].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using a thick continuous line for dimension or construction lines[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Dimension and construction lines must always be thin and faint so they do not overpower the actual physical shape of the object[cite: 1].

Common Mistake

Drawing hidden details using solid continuous lines[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Solid continuous lines indicate visible surfaces; concealed features must strictly be drawn with thin short dashes[cite: 1].

Common Mistake

Constructing arrowheads with wide, blunt, or unproportioned shapes[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

Arrowheads should be sharp, elongated points (length roughly three times width) pointing cleanly to projection lines[cite: 1].

Common Mistake

Drawing vertical lines with a set-square placed directly on the board without resting on the tee-square[cite: 1].

Scientific Fact

The set-square must sit squarely on the horizontal blade of the tee-square to maintain true $90^\circ$ perpendicularity[cite: 1].
