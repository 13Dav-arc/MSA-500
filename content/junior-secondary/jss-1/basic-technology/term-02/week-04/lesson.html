Week 04: Basic Board Practice (Paper Setting, Pencil Sharpening, and Border Lines)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Basic Board Practice (Paper Setting, Pencil Sharpening, and Border Lines) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Before a draftsman draws a single line of an architectural plan, the precision of the entire project is determined by how the paper is mounted and how the pencil is sharpened. If your drawing paper is tilted by half a millimeter, every horizontal and vertical line drawn across the sheet will be crooked. Similarly, using a dull pencil produces thick, inaccurate lines that accumulate errors across dimensions. Mastering basic board practice—aligning your paper with the tee-square, dressing pencil leads into conical and chisel points, and laying down uniform border margins—builds the disciplined foundation required for all professional technical drafting.

Key Terms

Board Practice The systematic hands-on procedure of preparing, mounting, aligning, and executing technical drawing tasks on a drawing board using standard drafting instruments[cite: 2].
Conical Point A pencil lead sharpened into a smooth, symmetrical circular cone, used primarily for freehand sketching, general writing, and technical lettering[cite: 2].
Chisel Point (Knife Edge) A pencil lead sharpened into a flat, wedge-shaped chisel edge, used for drawing uniform, continuous lines in geometrical and engineering constructions[cite: 2].
Border Line (Margin) A continuous frame drawn $10\text{ mm}$ to $15\text{ mm}$ inward from the outer edges of the drawing paper to protect and enclose the drawing area[cite: 2].
Stock Alignment Holding the head or stock of the tee-square firmly against the working edge of the drawing board to guarantee true horizontal orientation[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Setting Drawing Paper on the Board (Step-by-Step Procedure)

Mounting drawing paper squarely and securely on the board is the first step in technical drawing. The operation follows a four-step sequence:

1.
Step 1 (Positioning the Board and Paper): Place the drawing board flat and convenient to your reach on the workstation table. Lay the drawing paper on the surface of the board, leaving equal margins all around, with the true working edge of the board positioned to your left-hand side.
2.
Step 2 (Alignment with the Tee-Square): Place the tee-square over the paper. Press the stock of the tee-square firmly against the left-hand working edge of the board with your left hand, and gently slide the blade up toward the top edge of the paper. Align the top edge of the drawing sheet parallel to the top working edge of the tee-square blade.
3.
Step 3 (Fastening the Sheet): While holding the tee-square firmly against the paper and board edge, fasten the top two corners of the paper with short strips of drafting masking tape placed diagonally, or clamp them with metal drawing clips. Smooth the paper downward flat against the timber and secure the bottom two corners.
4.
Step 4 (Verification): Slide the tee-square blade gently down the board to verify that the paper has remained stationary and perfectly parallel without buckling or shifting.
Standard Four-Step Procedure for Setting Drawing Paper on a Drawing Board

Aspect 2: Pencil Sharpening Techniques (Conical Point vs. Chisel Point)

A properly sharpened pencil is essential to technical drawing. Draftsmen prepare their pencil leads into two distinct profiles depending on the specific drafting task:

1.
The Conical Point:
The wooden casing is peeled back by approximately $15\text{ mm}$ to $20\text{ mm}$, and the exposed graphite core ($8\text{ mm}$ to $10\text{ mm}$) is ground symmetrically into a pointed cone using a pencil sharpener or fine sandpaper block.
Primary Applications: General writing, freehand sketching, and standard technical lettering.
Handling Technique: The pencil is rotated between the thumb and forefinger while lettering to maintain a uniform point.
1.
The Chisel Point (Knife Edge):
The exposed graphite core is shaved flat on two opposing sides to form a thin, sharp wedge resembling a wood chisel or knife edge.
Primary Applications: Geometrical constructions, mechanical drawings, and drawing long, uniform continuous lines.
Advantage: The flat edge wears down much slower than a point, maintaining a consistent line thickness from the beginning to the end of a long line.

Aspect 3: Instruments Required for Good Board Practice

Executing accurate technical drawings requires an assembled kit of standardized instruments working together:

Drawing Board and Tee-Square: Provide the flat working surface and the horizontal datum guide.
Set-Squares ($45^\circ$ and $30^\circ/60^\circ$): Rest on the tee-square to draw vertical, perpendicular, and angled lines.
Compasses and Dividers: Used for drawing circles, arcs, and transferring measurements from scales.
Protractor and French Curves: Used for measuring arbitrary angles and constructing smooth irregular curves.
Drawing Pencils (2H and HB): A 2H hard pencil for layout and construction lines; an HB pencil for border lines, outlines, and lettering.
Eraser and Drawing Paper: Clean drafting eraser and standard ISO paper ($\text{A3}$ or $\text{A4}$).

Aspect 4: Drawing Border Lines (Margins)

Once the paper is mounted, the draftsman must construct border lines (also called margins):

Dimension Standard: A uniform space of $10\text{ mm}$ to $15\text{ mm}$ is measured inward from all four outer edges of the drawing paper.
Construction Method:
Using a metric rule, mark points $10\text{ mm}$ (or $15\text{ mm}$) from the left, right, top, and bottom edges of the sheet.
Use the tee-square to draw horizontal border lines across the top and bottom marks.
Use a set-square resting squarely on the tee-square to draw vertical border lines along the left and right marks, connecting the corners cleanly.
Purpose of Border Lines:
Protection: Shields the interior drawing area from thumb smudges, handling tears, and perimeter damage.
Presentation: Encloses the technical work in a neat, balanced, and professional frame.

Aspect 5: Line Generation Using the Tee-Square and Set-Squares

Combining the tee-square and set-squares allows the draftsman to produce clean horizontal, vertical, and inclined lines:

Horizontal Lines: Always drawn with the aid of the tee-square. The draftsman holds the stock firmly against the left edge with the non-dominant hand and draws the line from left to right along the top edge of the blade, tilting the pencil slightly in the direction of the stroke.
Vertical Lines: The set-square must sit squarely on the horizontal blade of the tee-square. Vertical lines are drawn from the bottom upward along the vertical edge of the set-square.
Perpendicularity: Because the tee-square blade is horizontal and the set-square vertical edge forms a $90^\circ$ angle with its base, lines drawn using this combined setup are automatically perpendicular.

Comparing Basic Board Practice Tools and Techniques

Lead Geometry

Conical Point vs Chisel Point

A conical point is round and pointed for freehand sketching and lettering[cite: 2], whereas a chisel point is a flat wedge designed to draw long, uniform lines in geometric constructions without rapid blunting[cite: 2].

Line Orientation

Horizontal Lines vs Vertical Lines

Horizontal lines are drawn directly along the top edge of the tee-square blade from left to right[cite: 2], while vertical lines are drawn upward using a set-square resting on the tee-square blade[cite: 2].

Sheet Boundaries

Paper Trim Edge vs Border Line

The paper trim edge is the physical cut border of the sheet[cite: 2], while the border line is an internal margin drawn $10\text{ mm}$ to $15\text{ mm}$ inward to frame and protect the drawing[cite: 2].

Pencil Hardness

2H Pencil vs HB Pencil

The 2H pencil produces faint, sharp construction lines for initial layouts[cite: 2], while the HB pencil produces dark, dense lines for finished borders, outlines, and lettering[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Setting the drawing paper by aligning it with the edge of the drawing board instead of the tee-square blade[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Board edges may have small variations; paper must always be aligned directly to the blade of the tee-square while its stock is held against the working edge[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Drawing horizontal lines along the bottom edge of the tee-square blade[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Lines must only be drawn along the top edge of the tee-square blade to maintain stability and sight of the line[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Drawing vertical lines by placing the set-square directly on the paper without supporting it on the tee-square[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

A set-square must always sit squarely on the blade of the tee-square to ensure true vertical ($90^\circ$) alignment[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Leaving uneven margins when constructing border lines around the drawing sheet[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Border lines must maintain an equal, uniform distance ($10\text{ mm}$ or $15\text{ mm}$) all around the paper[cite: 2].
