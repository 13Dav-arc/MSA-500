Week 04: Woodwork Hand Tools (Holding Devices, Cutting and Paring Tools)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Woodwork Hand Tools (Holding Devices, Cutting and Paring Tools) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When a carpenter attempts to saw a tenon joint or plane a rough board, timber cannot simply be held in place with one bare hand while operating a sharp tool with the other. The forward push of a plane or the rapid stroke of a saw demands that timber be anchored firmly using specialized holding devices like vices, bench hooks, and sash clamps. Once secured, shaping timber down to exact dimensions requires choosing between specialized bench saws for straight grain cutting, intricate curve-cutting saws, and precision bench planes designed to produce flat, smooth surfaces. Mastering holding devices alongside cutting and paring tools ensures that timber is shaped safely, cleanly, and with dimensional accuracy.

Key Terms

Holding Devices Workshop tools and fixtures mounted on the workbench or clamped to workpieces to hold timber securely during cutting, planing, chiseling, and assembly[cite: 2].
Bench Vice (Fitter's Vice) A heavy clamping device equipped with broad jaws and a torsion bar used to hold timber firmly on the workbench during operations such as filing, bending, cutting, and assembly[cite: 2].
Sash Clamp A large, elongated bar clamp used for holding and drawing wooden boards and frames tightly together during gluing and assembly[cite: 2].
Bench Saws Straight-bladed hand saws used for linear cutting along or across the grain of wood (such as rip saws, cross-cut saws, panel saws, and tenon saws)[cite: 2].
Bench Planes Woodworking paring and smoothing tools equipped with a cutting iron, used to remove tool marks, dress timber surfaces, and produce flat surfaces and straight edges[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Workbench Holding Devices and Fittings

Woodworkers perform their constructions on sturdy workbenches fitted with specialized work-holding fixtures:

1.
The Bench Vice (Fitter's Vice):
Mounted to the side of the workbench to clamp and hold jobs during operations such as filing, bending, tapping, cutting, and assembling parts.
Features a screw mechanism driven by a torsion bar that opens and closes the clamping jaws.
1.
Other Types of Vices:
Hand Vice: A compact, portable vice held in the hand, used for gripping small workpieces during drilling, riveting, and light bench tasks.
Machine Vice: Clamped or bolted directly to the table of machine tools (such as drill presses) to secure workpieces beneath cutting spindles.
1.
Care and Maintenance of the Vice:
Always keep the vice clean and free of sawdust and metal chips.
Oil the internal screw threads regularly to ensure smooth movement.
Never use the vice jaws as an anvil for hammering.
Tighten the vice using hand force only on the torsion bar; never strike it with a hammer.
1.
Bench Stop and Bench Hook:
Bench Stop: A strip of wood or metal fixed on top of the workbench that prevents wood from slipping off the bench top during planing.
Bench Hook: A portable wooden device hooked over the bench edge used to hold workpieces down during sawing or chiseling while protecting the bench surface from saw cuts.
Essential Woodworking Holding Devices and Workbench Fittings

Aspect 2: Workshop Clamps (G-Clamp, F-Clamp, and Sash Clamp)

Clamps are portable holding tools used to hold timber components together temporarily during fabrication or permanently while adhesive cures:

1.
G-Clamp:
A rigid metal frame shaped like the letter "G" fitted with a threaded clamping screw.
Ideal for holding small pieces of wood together or securing small jobs to the workbench.
1.
F-Clamp:
Shaped like the letter "F" with a long sliding bar that provides an adjustable clamping capacity wider than a standard G-clamp.
Used like the G-clamp for holding workpieces securely during assembly.
1.
Sash Clamp:
A heavy, elongated clamp consisting of a long steel bar, an adjustable tail stop, and a screw head.
Used for holding and drawing large wooden assemblies, wide table panels, door frames, and casework tightly together during gluing and assembly.

Aspect 3: Straight Cutting: Bench Saws

Saws are cutting tools used to part timber stock. Saws are broadly divided into bench saws (for straight cutting) and curve cutting saws:

Rip Saw: Designed specifically for cutting timber along the grain (lengthwise). Its teeth act like a row of small chisels that rip through wood fibers.
Cross-cut Saw: Designed specifically for cutting timber across the grain. Its teeth are shaped like knife points that sever fibers cleanly.
Panel Saw: A saw with finer teeth used for sawing thin timber across the wood.
Tenon Saw: Features a thin blade reinforced with a heavy brass or steel spine along the back to keep the blade rigid. It is used for cutting shoulders to tenons and sawing precise recesses in boards.
Dovetail Saw: Similar in design to the tenon saw but significantly shorter with finer teeth. It is used for cutting fine joints (like dovetails) and light, delicate sawing tasks.

Aspect 4: Curved Cutting: Curve Cutting Saws

When timber designs require circular openings, hollow cutouts, or contoured edges, specialized curve cutting saws are used:

1.
Coping Saw:
Features a thin, flexible steel blade mounted inside a deep C-shaped metal frame.
Used for cutting curve marked lines in wood.
The blade can be rotated and adjusted to cut in any direction.
1.
Fret Saw:
Features a deep metal frame and a very fine blade.
Used to cut intricate curves in thin wood of $8\text{ mm}$ thickness or less, and is commonly used for cutting plywood.
1.
Bow Saw:
Features a wooden frame tensioned with a twisted cord or rod.
Used to cut along curve marked lines in heavier timber up to $50\text{ mm}$ thick.
1.
Compass Saw:
Features a narrow, tapering blade attached to an open pistol-grip handle.
Used for cutting large interior curves and openings where a framed saw cannot reach.
1.
Keyhole Saw:
Features a very slender, pointed blade designed to enter small pre-drilled holes.
Used mainly for cutting internal curves and narrow slots where the bow saw cannot be used.

Aspect 5: Paring and Smoothing Tools: Types and Uses of Planes

Bench planes are shaving tools used to obtain a smooth surface, square true edges, and bring timber to required finished dimensions:

1.
Jack Plane:
The general-purpose workhorse of the wood shop, measuring roughly $350\text{ mm}$ to $400\text{ mm}$ in length.
Used to smoothen rough wood, remove initial saw marks from timber, and dress surfaces and edges down to approximate size.
1.
Trying Plane:
The longest bench plane (typically $550\text{ mm}$ to $600\text{ mm}$ long).
Used to produce true flat surfaces and perfectly straight edges on long boards.
1.
Smoothing Plane:
A short, compact bench plane (measuring roughly $200\text{ mm}$ to $250\text{ mm}$).
Used for the final cleaning and smoothing of timber surfaces and edges ready for assembling.
1.
Block Plane:
A small, compact plane operated with one hand, featuring a low-angle cutting iron.
Used for planing end grain and small workpieces that are not easily accessible with large bench planes.
1.
Spoke Shave:
A specialized curve-cutting plane featuring a narrow blade mounted between two lateral handles.
Used to dress and produce smooth curved surfaces and rounded edges on timber components like tool handles and chair legs.

Comparing Woodwork Holding, Sawing, and Planing Tools

Grain Orientation

Rip Saw vs Cross-cut Saw

A rip saw is designed specifically for cutting along the wood grain[cite: 2], whereas a cross-cut saw is designed for cutting across the wood grain[cite: 2].

Clamping Reach

G-Clamp vs Sash Clamp

A G-clamp holds small pieces of wood within its fixed G-frame[cite: 2], whereas a sash clamp uses a long steel bar to hold and draw large frames together during gluing[cite: 2].

Surface Truing

Jack Plane vs Trying Plane

A jack plane is used for rough dressing and removing saw marks[cite: 2], while a trying plane produces perfectly flat surfaces and straight edges on long boards[cite: 2].

Curve Cutting Capacity

Fret Saw vs Bow Saw

A fret saw cuts curves in thin wood of $8\text{ mm}$ or less and plywood[cite: 2], whereas a bow saw cuts curves in timber up to $50\text{ mm}$ thick[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using a rip saw to cut timber across the grain[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Rip saw teeth are shaped to cut along the grain; using them across grain tears the wood fibers and leaves splintered edges[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using a bench vice as an anvil for heavy hammering[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Vice jaws are made of cast metal that cracks or fractures under hammering impacts; use an anvil for hammering and tighten vices with hand force only[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Attempting to smooth an inside curve with a trying plane or smoothing plane[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Flat bench planes cannot ride inside curved surfaces; smooth curved edges using a spoke shave[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using a tenon saw without supporting timber in a bench hook or vice[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Unsupported sawing allows the timber to shift, causing binding and inaccurate joint shoulders; always use a bench hook or bench vice[cite: 2].
