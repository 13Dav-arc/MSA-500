Week 01: Woodwork Hand Tools (Classes of Hand Tools, The Workbench, and Measuring Tools)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Woodwork Hand Tools (Classes of Hand Tools, The Workbench, and Measuring Tools) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Before a carpenter can cut a timber beam, smooth a tabletop with a plane, or assemble a sturdy chair, two foundational requirements must be met: a solid work surface and exact dimensions. If a timber board is measured inaccurately by even a fraction of a centimeter, the entire wooden structure will fit poorly or fail completely. In a professional woodworking workshop, operations like sawing, planing, chiseling, and boring depend on the specialized fittings of the woodworking workbench and precision measuring tools. Understanding the six core classes of woodwork tools, mastering the parts of the workbench, and selecting the right tool for linear and circular measurements form the essential first steps of woodwork technology.

Key Terms

Woodwork Hand Tools Manual implements guided and operated by hand without electrical power to measure, mark, hold, cut, shape, and assemble timber workpieces[cite: 2].
The Workbench A heavy, rigid wooden bench in the woodworking workshop upon which operations such as sawing, planing, chiseling, drilling, and boring are carried out[cite: 2].
The Well A longitudinal hollow trough recessed along the center of the workbench used to store bench tools safely so they do not roll onto the floor[cite: 2].
Linear Measurement The measurement of the straight-line distance between two points, including length, breadth, and height[cite: 2].
Calipers Specialized measuring tools equipped with curved or stepped legs used to measure the internal and external diameters of circular and cylindrical shapes[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: The Six Classes of Woodwork Hand Tools

Woodworking operations require different hand tools designed for specific workshop tasks. Hand tools in the woodworking trade are organized into six primary functional classes:

1.
Measuring Tools: Used to determine, check, and record physical distances and dimensions on timber stock (e.g., metric rules, folding rules, measuring tapes, and calipers).
2.
Marking Out / Setting Out Tools: Used to mark lines, transfer dimensions, check angles, and lay out the boundaries of measured lengths (e.g., try-squares, mitre squares, sliding bevels, and compasses).
3.
Work Holding Tools (Holding Devices): Used to clamp and hold workpieces firmly on the workbench during workshop operations (e.g., bench vices, bench stops, bench hooks, G-clamps, and sash clamps).
4.
Boring Tools: Used to bore or drill narrow pilot holes and deep cylindrical holes in wood (e.g., ratchet braces, twist drills, gimlets, and bradawls).
5.
Cutting and Paring Tools: Used to cut, shape, and smooth timber down to required sizes (e.g., rip saws, cross-cut saws, tenon saws, coping saws, bench planes, and chisels).
6.
Driving Tools: Used to drive, push, or fix fasteners like nails and screws into wood, or strike chisels (e.g., ball-peen hammers, cross-peen hammers, screwdrivers, and mallets).

Aspect 2: The Woodworking Workbench and Its Fittings

The woodworker's workbench is a primary workstation in the wood shop upon which operations such as sawing, drilling, planing, chiseling, and boring are executed. To support these varied tasks safely, the workbench is fitted with specialized built-in fixtures:

The Bench Stop: A wood or metal fixture mounted on top of the workbench that prevents the timber workpiece from slipping forward off the bench surface during planing operations.
The Bench Hook: A portable wooden appliance hooked over the edge of the workbench to hold a workpiece securely during light sawing or chiseling, while shielding the bench top from saw cuts.
The Bench Vice (Woodworker's / Fitter's Vice): A heavy clamping device equipped with broad jaws and a torsion bar or screw mechanism that opens and closes to hold timber stock securely during cutting, filing, chiseling, and assembly. More than one bench vice is commonly mounted on a workshop bench.
The Well: A longitudinal hollow recess running down the center of the workbench, positioned lower than the outer work surfaces. It accommodates bench tools brought from the tool cupboard, preventing them from rolling off the bench onto the workshop floor or striking someone's feet.

Aspect 3: Fundamentals of Measurement in Woodwork

Measurement is the foundational first operation in woodwork. It involves taking, checking, and recording the distance between two points (or dimensions) of an object before any cutting or construction can begin. Timber must be measured accurately into required lengths to avoid wasting valuable wood.

Linear Measurement: Carried out when the two measured points lie along a straight line, representing length, breadth (width), or height/thickness.
Angular Measurement: Carried out when there is an inclination, slope, curve, or angular bend in a size or joint face.

Aspect 4: Linear Measuring Tools

Woodworkers use several linear measuring devices depending on the length of the timber and the working environment:

1.
The Metric Rule:
The simplest measuring instrument in the woodwork workshop.
Manufactured from steel, seasoned wood, or plastic, graduated in millimeters and centimeters.
Used for general bench measuring, short layout work, and straightedge guidance.
1.
The Measuring Tape:
Consists of a flexible, curved metal blade coiled inside a protective housing with an automatic spring-return mechanism.
Commonly ranges in length from $1.8\text{ m}$ to $5.0\text{ m}$.
Equipped with a hooked metal end to grip board edges during long measurements.
1.
The Folding Rule:
A rigid wooden or plastic rule measuring $1.8\text{ m}$ to $2.4\text{ m}$ in total length when fully unfolded.
Consists of multiple short rule segments connected by spring joints that fold compactly for transport.
Certain models feature a sliding brass extension rule for taking internal measurements inside tight spaces such as door frames and window openings.

Aspect 5: Circular Measurement: Calipers and Dividers

Measuring the diameters of curved and cylindrical timber pieces requires specialized contact measuring tools called calipers:

Inside Caliper: Features outward-pointing bent legs designed to measure the internal diameter of holes, hollow mortises, and recesses in circular objects.
Outside Caliper: Features inward-curving legs designed to span and measure the external diameter of round bars, cylindrical dowels, and turned spindles.
Odd-Leg Caliper (Jenny's Caliper): Features one straight pointed leg (or scriber leg) and one curved caliper leg. It is specifically used to scribe lines parallel to an edge and locate the center lines of round wooden bars.
Vernier Caliper: A precision measuring instrument carrying a main scale and a sliding vernier scale, capable of measuring internal diameters, external diameters, and depths accurately.
Dividers: An instrument featuring two straight, pointed steel legs used to step off equal measurements and transfer dimensions from a metric rule to the timber workpiece.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| Metric Rule | Metal, wood, plastic | Simplest linear measurement of length, breadth, and height |

| Measuring Tape | Flexible coiled metal blade ($1.8\text{ m}$ to $5.0\text{ m}$) | Long linear measurements of timber boards and assemblies |

Comparing Core Measuring and Workshop Tools

Dimensional Form

Linear Measurement vs Angular Measurement

Linear measurement determines straight-line distances between two points (length, breadth, height)[cite: 2], while angular measurement measures the inclination, slope, or bend between intersecting lines[cite: 2].

Contact Measurement

Inside Caliper vs Outside Caliper

Inside calipers measure internal bore diameters using outward-pointing legs[cite: 2], whereas outside calipers measure external cylinder diameters using inward-curving legs[cite: 2].

Bench Function

Bench Stop vs Bench Hook

A bench stop prevents timber from slipping off the bench during planing[cite: 2], while a bench hook holds workpieces steady during sawing and chiseling operations[cite: 2].

Rule Construction

Folding Rule vs Flexible Tape Rule

A folding rule uses rigid spring-jointed sections ($1.8\text{ m}$ to $2.4\text{ m}$) ideal for framing[cite: 2], whereas a tape rule uses a coiled metal blade ($1.8\text{ m}$ to $5.0\text{ m}$) inside a spring-loaded housing[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Attempting to cut timber stock before measuring and marking out[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Measurement is strictly the first operation in woodwork; unmeasured cuts lead to wasted timber and ruined joints[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using an outside caliper to measure the internal diameter of a circular bore[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Outside calipers have inward-curving legs for external diameters; internal diameters require inside calipers with outward-pointing legs[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Planing timber on the workbench without engaging the bench stop[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Planing creates forward pushing force; without a bench stop, the wood slips off the bench surface, creating safety hazards and uneven surfaces[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Leaving hand tools scattered on the outer raised surface of the workbench during work[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Tools left on the flat bench surface can roll onto the floor or strike feet; store tools in the central recessed well during operations[cite: 2].
