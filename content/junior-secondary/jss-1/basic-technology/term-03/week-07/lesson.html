Week 07: Metalwork Hand Tools (Driving Tools and Cutting Tools)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Metalwork Hand Tools (Driving Tools and Cutting Tools) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When shaping a solid block of mild steel or forming a curved sheet of aluminum, standard cutting tools from the carpentry shop are completely ineffective. A wood saw would have its teeth stripped instantly against steel, and a wooden mallet cannot provide the focused impact required to peen a solid copper rivet. In metalworking, craftsmen rely on cold chisels forged from hardened tool steel to shear tough stock, high-carbon steel files to shape contours, hacksaws with hardened blades to part thick rods, and planishing hammers to smooth out body panels. Understanding the specific applications of metalwork driving tools and cutting tools ensures that heavy metals can be severed, chipped, filed, and assembled cleanly, accurately, and safely.

Key Terms

Metalwork Driving Tools Workshop hand tools used for pushing, striking, and driving fasteners such as screws, rivets, and pins into structural metal members, or striking punches[cite: 2].
Metalwork Cutting Tools Hardened workshop tools used for severing, chipping, shaping, and removing material from pieces of metal in the workshop[cite: 2].
Cold Chisel A hardened chipping tool made of high-carbon steel used for cutting and shaping metals where finishing by filing cannot be carried out[cite: 2].
Scraper A hand tool that resembles a file but has no cutting teeth, used for removing slight surface irregularities and high spots from flat metal surfaces[cite: 2].
Hacksaw A frame-mounted saw specifically designed for cutting metals, featuring an adjustable blade that can be turned to cut in a sideways direction[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Metalwork Driving Tools: Hammers and Mallets

Driving tools in the metal workshop provide the kinetic striking force needed to shape sheet metal, seat pins, and drive mechanical fasteners:

1.
Metalworking Hammers:
A hammer consists of two distinct parts: a handle (made of seasoned wood or metallic material) and a head (always forged from high-carbon steel).
Hammers are classified according to the specific shape and contour of their head:
Ball-peen Hammer: Features a flat face and a hemispherical peen; the primary general-purpose striking tool in metalwork.
Straight-peen Hammer: The wedge-shaped peen is aligned parallel to the handle; used for riveting and shaping sheet metals.
Cross-peen Hammer: The peen is aligned across (perpendicular to) the handle; used for drawing down metal bars and riveting in narrow channels.
Planishing Hammer: Has highly polished, smooth striking faces; used by panel beaters for sheet metal finishing and smoothing out dings.
1.
Mallets:
A soft hammer whose head is made of soft materials such as synthetic rubber, wood, or rawhide.
Primary Function: Used to shape and bend thin sheet metal without denting, scratching, or damaging the surface of the workpiece.
Metalwork Striking and Driving Tools

Aspect 2: Metal Punches and Screwdrivers

Securing components and preparing hole locations in metal structures requires dedicated pin and driver tools:

1.
Punches:
Striking tools made of hardened steel used for producing holes on thin sheets of metal, marking holes for drilling, and removing fasteners.
Centre Punch & Dot Punch: Used to mark drill points and witness dots along scribed lines.
Pin Punch: A slender, flat-ended punch used specifically to drive out and remove rivets, pins, and dowels from mechanical assemblies.
1.
Screwdrivers in Metal Assemblies:
Hand tools used for driving screws into or out of structural metal members.
Flat Screwdriver: Engages screws with a single straight line slot.
Star Screwdriver (Phillips): Engages screws with a four-point star opening.
Allen Key (Allen Screwdriver): An L-shaped hexagonal bar used for screws and socket bolts with hexagonal or square sockets in their head.
Offset Screwdriver: Used in tight clearance spaces where an inline straight screwdriver cannot be turned.

Aspect 3: Metal Cutting Tools: Cold Chisels

Chisels in metalwork are chipping tools used for rough-shaping metals and parting sheet stock where finishing by other methods (such as filing or sawing) cannot be carried out:

1.
Flat Chisel:
The most common chisel, featuring a wide, slightly convex cutting edge.
Used for general chipping of flat surfaces, cutting sheet metal, and cutting off rivet heads.
1.
Cross-cut Chisel:
Features a narrow cutting edge designed to cut grooves, keyways, and narrow channels into metal.
1.
Round-Nose Chisel:
Features a rounded cutting nose used for chipping curved oil grooves in machine bearings and clearing concave corners.
1.
Diamond Chisel (Diamond-Point):
Features a sharp diamond-shaped cutting point used for cutting V-shaped grooves and cleaning out square interior corners.
Types of Cold Chisels Used in Metal Chipping Operations

Aspect 4: Metal Shaping Tools: Files and Scrapers

Refining chipped or cut metal surfaces down to finished dimensions requires abrasive tooth action or precision scraping:

1.
Workshop Files:
Hardened steel tools covered with cutting teeth, used for shaping, deburring, and smoothing metals.
Classified by their cross-sectional profile:
Flat File: Rectangular cross-section; used for general filing of flat exterior surfaces.
Square File: Square cross-section; used for filing rectangular slots, keyways, and splines.
Half-Round File: One flat face and one curved face; used for dressing flat surfaces and concave curves.
Round File: Circular cross-section (often called a rat-tail file); used for enlarging circular holes and filing small radii.
1.
Scrapers:
Hand tools that look like files in profile but have no teeth along their faces.
Possess ground cutting edges used for removing slight irregularities and high spots from flat metal surfaces to create true bearing contacts.

Aspect 5: Metal Sawing Tools (Hacksaw, Piercing Saw, and Brass-Back Saw)

Parting metal rods, pipes, and sheets is performed using metal-cutting saws equipped with hardened, replaceable blades:

1.
The Hacksaw:
The primary metal-cutting saw consisting of a rigid C-frame, a handle, and an adjustable blade held under tension.
Sideways Adjustment: The blade pins can be turned $90^\circ$ by the user so that the blade cuts in a sideways direction, allowing long continuous cuts without the frame striking the workpiece.
1.
The Piercing Saw:
Features a deep, lightweight frame holding a very fine, flexible jeweller's blade.
Ideal for cutting intricate curves, sharp contours, and fine patterns in thin sheet metal.
1.
The Brass-Back Saw:
Features a fine-toothed blade stiffened with a solid brass spine along the top.
Useful when performing fine, straight cutting operations on soft metals such as brass, copper, and thin aluminum.

Comparing Metalwork Driving and Cutting Tools

Striking Hardness

Ball-peen Hammer vs Rubber Mallet

A ball-peen hammer delivers high-impact force with its high-carbon steel head[cite: 2], whereas a rubber mallet delivers a soft blow that prevents surface damage to sheet metal[cite: 2].

Cutting Mode

Workshop File vs Metal Scraper

A file uses thousands of sharp teeth to abrade and shape metal stock[cite: 2], whereas a scraper has no teeth and shaves away minor surface high spots using a ground edge[cite: 2].

Chisel Geometry

Flat Chisel vs Cross-cut Chisel

A flat chisel has a broad cutting edge for general surface chipping[cite: 2], while a cross-cut chisel has a narrow edge specifically for cutting slots and keyways[cite: 2].

Metal Sawing Scope

Hacksaw vs Piercing Saw

A hacksaw cuts thick metal bars, pipes, and structural plates[cite: 2], whereas a piercing saw has a fine blade designed for cutting intricate curves in thin sheet metal[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using a carpenter's wood chisel to chip or cut metal bars[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Wood chisels are tempered for soft wood fibers; striking steel immediately shatters or dulls the edge[cite: 2]. Always use cold chisels forged from high-carbon steel[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Striking finished sheet metal directly with a steel hammer face[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Steel hammer faces leave permanent dents and stretch sheet metal unevenly; use a soft-faced mallet made of synthetic rubber[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Mistaking a scraper for a toothless, worn-out file[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

A scraper is intentionally manufactured without teeth so it can shave off high spots without scratching the metal surface[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Attempting to cut continuous long strips of sheet metal with a hacksaw in its standard blade position[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

The hacksaw frame will collide with the sheet; turn the blade pins $90^\circ$ to cut in the sideways direction[cite: 2].
