Week 03: Woodwork Hand Tools (Driving Tools and Boring Tools)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Woodwork Hand Tools (Driving Tools and Boring Tools) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

When assembling a wooden cabinet, driving a screw directly into dry hardwood without first creating a pathway will either split the timber or snap the screw's head clean off. Similarly, striking a finished polished tabletop with a steel hammer leaves permanent dents that destroy hours of careful planing. In carpentry, assembling components securely and cleanly requires two complementary families of tools: boring tools to create clean pilot holes and recesses, and driving tools to set nails, screws, and wooden pins firmly into place. Understanding the specific applications of different hammer heads, screwdriver profiles, drill bits, and braces ensures that your woodwork projects remain strong, durable, and free from surface damage.

Key Terms

Driving Tools Hand tools used to push, strike, and fix fasteners such as nails and screws into wooden or metallic materials, or to strike cutting tools[cite: 2].
Boring Tools Workshop tools and cutting bits used to make narrow cylindrical holes or pilot openings in wood or metal[cite: 2].
Ratchet Brace A crank-shaped hand tool equipped with a chuck mechanism used to hold and turn various boring bits to drill holes in timber[cite: 2].
Mallet A soft-faced striking tool with a head made of wood or synthetic rubber, used to drive chisels or strike wooden joints without damaging the workpiece surface[cite: 2].
Countersink Bit A specialized conical boring bit used to bevel and enlarge the rim of an existing hole so that flathead screws sit flush with or below the timber surface[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: Fasteners and Driving Principles: Nails vs. Screws

Woodworking components are frequently joined using mechanical fasteners that require dedicated driving tools:

1.
Nails:
Manufactured from iron or steel featuring a flat head, a smooth stem (shank), and a sharp pointed end.
Nails are driven directly into wood or sheet metal using the impact force of hammers.
1.
Screws:
Fasteners that resemble nails but feature a slotted head and a turned or twisted helical stem (thread).
Screws provide superior holding power compared to nails because their threads grip the timber fibers.
Screws are driven into and withdrawn from wood or metal using screwdrivers.

Aspect 2: Workshop Hammers and Mallets

Hammers and mallets provide the striking force needed for assembly and shaping operations:

Structure of a Hammer: A hammer consists of two main parts: a head made of forged high-carbon iron or steel, and a wooden or composite handle designed to absorb shock.
Types of Hammers (Named by Head Shape):
1.
Ball-peen Hammer: Features a flat striking face on one side and a hemispherical ball on the other; used for general workshop purposes.
2.
Straight-peen Hammer: The peen is shaped like a blunt wedge parallel to the handle; used for riveting and shaping sheet metals.
3.
Cross-peen Hammer: The wedge-shaped peen runs across (perpendicular to) the handle; used for drawing down metal and riveting in narrow spaces.
4.
Planishing Hammer: Has flat, highly polished faces used by panel beaters for surface finishing and smoothing.
5.
Blocking Head Hammer: Features polished, rounded faces used specifically for shaping and dishing sheet metal.
6.
Upholstery Hammer: A lightweight hammer with a slender head, featuring one magnetic face used to pick up and drive furniture tacks (short, light nails).
Mallets:
Soft-faced hammers with heads made of hardwood, synthetic rubber, or rawhide.
Used to strike wooden chisel handles and assemble jointed timber parts without marring, denting, or fracturing the wood fibers.
Workshop Striking Tools: Hammers and Soft-Faced Mallets

Aspect 3: Types of Screwdrivers and Head Matching

A screwdriver consists of a steel blade, an operating tip, and a wooden or molded plastic handle designed for rotational grip. The tip must match the corresponding slot in the screw head:

1.
Flat Screwdriver: Features a flat, wedge-shaped tip used for driving screws that have a single straight slot across the head.
2.
Star Screwdriver (Phillips): Features a cross-shaped, pointed tip used for driving screws that have a star or cross-shaped opening in the head, providing slip resistance.
3.
Offset Screwdriver: An L-shaped or Z-shaped bent steel bar with screwdriver blades at both ends; used in tight, confined corners where a straight screwdriver cannot be turned.
4.
Allen Screwdriver (Allen Key): An L-shaped hexagonal steel bar used for driving in or removing screws and bolts that have a hexagonal or square recessed socket in their head.

Aspect 4: Boring Tools: The Ratchet Brace and Boring Bits

Boring is the technical act of making narrow cylindrical holes in materials like wood or metal. In woodwork, holes are bored using cutting tools called bits held in a ratchet brace:

The Ratchet Brace: A crank-shaped hand tool that incorporates a chuck mechanism to grip square or round bit shanks, and a ratchet system allowing the user to drill holes in tight quarters where a full $360^\circ$ sweep of the handle is impossible.
Common Boring Bits and Their Functions:
Centre Bit: Fitted with a central guide point, a spur, and a flat cutting lip; used for boring shallow, flat-bottomed holes in wood.
Dowel Bit: Used to bore accurate, clean holes where two wooden components are to be joined and pinned together by wooden dowel pins.
Countersink Bit: Conical in shape; used to enlarge and bevel the mouth of existing pilot holes so that countersunk screw heads rest flush with the surface.
Double Twist Drill Bit (Twist Drills): Features helical flutes that lift wood shavings out of the bore; used for boring deep, clean holes in timber.

Aspect 5: Light Hand Boring Tools (Gimlet, Bradawl, and Hand Drill)

For small pilot holes, fine joinery, or rapid drilling, light manual boring tools are preferred:

1.
The Gimlet:
A small T-shaped hand tool featuring a threaded, screw-like pointed tip and a fluted shank.
Turned by hand to bore small pilot holes in wood before inserting small nails or screws, preventing the timber from splitting.
1.
The Bradawl:
Resembles a small screwdriver with a sharp chisel-like or pointed edge and a bulbous wooden handle.
Can be used in place of a gimlet to form small starter holes before using screws or nails, or as a light driver to insert small screws.
1.
The Hand Drill:
Differs from the ratchet brace by using a beveled gear mechanism to rotate the drill chuck rapidly when the side handle is turned.
Designed for drilling small, accurate holes in wood, plastic, or sheet metal using straight-shank twist drill bits.

Comparing Driving and Boring Tools Across Form and Function

Fastener Drive

Hammer vs Screwdriver

Hammers drive smooth-stemmed nails into wood through linear impact force[cite: 2], while screwdrivers drive threaded screws through rotational torque applied to the head slots[cite: 2].

Striking Surface

Steel Hammer vs Soft Mallet

Steel hammers deliver hard impacts for nails and metalwork[cite: 2], whereas soft-faced mallets protect wooden workpieces and chisel handles from bruising or splitting[cite: 2].

Hole Depth

Centre Bit vs Double Twist Drill

Centre bits are designed for boring shallow holes in timber[cite: 2], whereas double twist drills have helical flutes designed for boring deep holes[cite: 2].

Drive Mechanism

Ratchet Brace vs Hand Drill

A ratchet brace uses a manual crank sweep for heavy bits[cite: 2], while a hand drill uses beveled gears to rotate small drill bits at high speed[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Using a steel hammer to strike the wooden handle of a wood chisel[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Steel hammer heads fracture and split wooden chisel handles; always use a wooden or rubber mallet to strike chisels[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Driving a flat-head wood screw without countersinking the pilot hole[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Without using a countersink bit, the conical head of the screw will protrude above the timber or crush surrounding wood fibers[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using an incorrect screwdriver tip that does not fit the screw head slot[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

A loose or oversized tip damages the slot (stripping the screw) and causes slipping; always pair flat screwdrivers with single slots and star screwdrivers with star openings[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Attempting to drive nails directly near the edge of dry hardwood without pre-boring[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Nailing close to edges splits timber; always bore a small starter hole using a gimlet or bradawl first[cite: 2].
