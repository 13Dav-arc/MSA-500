Week 02: Woodwork Hand Tools (Setting and Marking Out Tools)

Foundational curriculum module in Basic Technology covering Woodwork Hand Tools (Setting and Marking Out Tools) for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Once a craftsman measures a length of timber, cutting straight into the wood without marking clear reference lines invites errors that ruin entire assemblies. A saw blade cannot follow an invisible boundary, and a table leg cut at an improper angle leaves the furniture rocking on the workshop floor. Setting and marking out tools translate raw numerical measurements into precise physical lines, right angles, and joint bevels directly on the grain of the wood. Mastering tools like the try-square, mitre square, sliding bevel, and compass ensures that every saw cut, mortise, and chamfer aligns with millimeter accuracy.

Key Terms

Marking Out The essential workshop operation that immediately follows measurement, involving the scribing or drawing of boundary lines, joint locations, and centers on a workpiece before cutting begins[cite: 2].
Try-Square An L-shaped woodworking tool consisting of a thick wooden or cast-iron stock and a steel blade fixed at an angle of $90^\circ$, used to mark right-angled lines and test the squareness of planed timber edges[cite: 2].
Mitre Square A marking tool with a blade permanently set into its stock at an angle of $45^\circ$, used specifically for marking and checking mitre angles for frames and corner joints[cite: 2].
Sliding Bevel An adjustable marking tool with an adjustable blade held by a locking screw, used for duplicating, transferring, and setting out non-standard angles and bevels[cite: 2].
Compass An instrument with two legs joined by a friction pivot, fitted with a pencil lead, used to scribe circular arcs, circles, and curved profiles on timber[cite: 2].

Core Concept

Aspect 1: The Role and Sequence of Marking Out in Woodwork

In both woodwork and metalwork, marking out is the direct operational step that immediately follows measurement:

Measurement determines and records the physical distance between two points, while marking out clearly establishes the physical boundaries, cut lines, shoulders, and centers directly onto the timber workpiece.
Attempting to saw, plane, or chisel without marking out leads to dimension errors, uneven joints, and wasted timber stock.
Marking out tools include the try-square, sliding bevel, mitre square, compass, and pairs of dividers.

Aspect 2: The Try-Square (Structure and Functions)

The try-square is one of the most frequently used setting and testing tools in the wood shop:

Construction: It consists of two primary components joined at an angle of $90^\circ$: a thick stock (traditionally made of wood or cast iron with an internal brass face plate) and a thin steel blade.
Core Functions:
1.
Marking Lines at Right Angles ($90^\circ$): The stock is pressed against the planed face or edge of the timber while a pencil or marking knife draws a line along the blade perpendicular to the edge.
2.
Testing Squareness: It is placed across timber faces and edges to verify that they are planed flat and meet at an angle of $90^\circ$. If light shines under the blade when resting against the wood, the edge is not square and requires further planing.
Using the Try-Square to Mark Right Angles and Test Edge Squareness

Aspect 3: The Mitre Square (Marking $45^\circ$ Angles)

When joining two pieces of wood to form corner frames (such as picture frames, window architraves, or door moldings), right angles are often formed by joining two $45^\circ$ cuts called mitres:

Design: The blade of the mitre square is fixed into the stock at an angle of $45^\circ$ (or $135^\circ$ internally).
Function: It is specifically used to mark and verify lines at an angle of $45^\circ$ across the face or edge of a board before sawing.
Unlike the sliding bevel, the angle on a mitre square is fixed permanently during manufacturing to maintain $45^\circ$ accuracy.

Aspect 4: The Sliding Bevel (Duplicating and Transferring Angles)

Not all woodwork joints meet at standard angles of $90^\circ$ or $45^\circ$. Structures like roof trusses, splayed leg stools, dovetail pins, and decorative chamfers require variable angles:

Construction: The sliding bevel features a slotted steel blade that pivots inside a wooden or metal stock and is clamped at any chosen angle by a thumbscrew or locking lever.
Function: It is used for duplicating angles from an existing template or drawing and for setting out bevels across timber stock.
Angular Constraint: The lines and angles set by a sliding bevel should not be $45^\circ$ (for which a mitre square is dedicated) or $90^\circ$ (for which a try-square is dedicated); it is reserved for irregular, adjustable inclinations.
Comparison of Angular Setting Tools: Try-Square, Mitre Square, and Sliding Bevel

Aspect 5: Compasses and Setting Out Arcs

In woodwork, curved lines, rounded corners, and circular recesses are set out using compasses:

Function: A pencil compass is used to mark and scribe the arcs of a circle, curved profiles, and rounded shoulders directly onto the wood surface.
Setting the Radius: The compass legs are opened, and the sharp steel pivot point and pencil tip are adjusted to the required radius against a metric rule before scribing the arc.
Dividers Integration: While a compass carries a pencil lead to mark visible lines, dividers feature two steel points used to step off equal spaces and transfer layout points from drawing plans to timber faces.

Comparing Woodwork Setting and Marking Out Tools

Angular Characteristic

Try-Square vs Mitre Square

The try-square has a blade fixed at $90^\circ$ to mark right angles and test squareness[cite: 2], while the mitre square has a blade set at $45^\circ$ to lay out mitre joints[cite: 2].

Adjustability

Mitre Square vs Sliding Bevel

A mitre square is locked at $45^\circ$[cite: 2], whereas a sliding bevel has an adjustable blade that pivots and clamps to duplicate variable non-standard angles[cite: 2].

Operation Sequence

Measurement vs Marking Out

Measurement involves determining and checking dimensions with rules or tapes[cite: 2], while marking out is the subsequent step of drawing physical lines and boundaries on the workpiece[cite: 2].

Curved Geometry

Compass vs Dividers

A compass scribes visible pencil arcs and circles on timber[cite: 2], whereas dividers step off equal linear intervals using two sharp steel points[cite: 2].

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common Mistake

Sawing timber immediately after taking measurements without marking out guidelines[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Marking out is the required intermediate step after measurement; sawing without lines produces inaccurate, uneven cuts[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Using a sliding bevel to mark standard $45^\circ$ mitre cuts[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

Standard $45^\circ$ angles should be marked with a dedicated mitre square to ensure consistent accuracy without slippage[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Letting the stock of the try-square tilt away from the timber edge while drawing lines[cite: 2].

Scientific Fact

The stock must be pressed firmly flush against the face edge of the wood; if the stock tilts, the line will not be a true $90^\circ$ right angle[cite: 2].

Common Mistake

Dropping try-squares or using them to pry open paint cans.

Scientific Fact

Impact or prying loosens the rivets between the stock and blade, ruining the $90^\circ$ alignment of the tool[cite: 2].
