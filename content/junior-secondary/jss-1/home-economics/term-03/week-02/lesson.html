Why This Matters to You

Imagine sitting down to a formal dinner with school principals, community leaders, or potential scholarship benefactors. Across from you, someone slouches heavily over their plate, stuffs their mouth until their cheeks bulge, talks loudly while chewing—spraying grains of rice across the table—and uses a knife to shovel soup into their mouth! How would you feel? You would likely feel embarrassed, uncomfortable, and repulsed. Eating is not merely a biological act to fill an empty belly; it is a profound social event that reveals a person's character, self-discipline, respect for others, and cultural upbringing. Furthermore, during adolescence, your body is undergoing the fastest physical growth spurt of your lifetime, requiring specialized nutrients like iron and calcium to build strong bones and vibrant blood cells. In this lesson, you will master the essential rules of table etiquette that allow you to dine with confidence in any setting, and discover the unique nutritional fuel your body demands during adolescence!

Core Concept

1. Meaning and Social Importance of Table Manners

Healthy Feeding and Eating Practices II: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration

Table manners are not stiff, artificial rituals invented to restrict freedom; they are practical rules rooted in hygiene, mutual respect, safety, and consideration:

Hygiene and Health: Prevents the transmission of germs, bacteria, and saliva between diners sharing food or dining spaces.
Safety at Mealtime: Eliminates life-threatening accidents such as airway choking, scalding from hot liquids, and accidental cuts from knives.
Social Respect and Culture: Eating with decorum makes communal dining pleasant and enjoyable for everyone present, honoring family and cultural traditions.
Executive and Professional Confidence: Mastering dining etiquette prepares students for future academic dinners, job interviews, diplomatic banquets, and leadership roles.

2. Core Rules of Table Manners

A. Preparation & Commencing the Meal

1.
Hygiene First: Always wash hands with clean running water and soap before touching dining chairs or tableware.
2.
Seating & Posture: Sit upright with your back supported by the chair. Keep your feet resting flat on the floor. Never slouch, slump, or place bare elbows on the table while eating.
3.
Waiting for Elders: Allow parents, elders, or the host to be seated and served first. Never rush ahead to start eating before grace is said or the host signals the start of the meal.

B. During the Meal

1.
Manageable Portions: Take moderate, manageable forkfuls or spoonfuls. Do not overload your mouth until your cheeks bulge.
2.
Chew Silently: Chew food slowly and quietly with your lips closed. Never smack your lips, slurp soup loudly, or gulp water noisily.
3.
Never Talk with Food in Your Mouth: Swallow completely before answering questions or speaking. Talking with food in the mouth is repulsive to fellow diners and presents an acute, life-threatening choking hazard if food enters the windpipe.
4.
Passing Dishes: If an item (such as salt, pepper, or water) is out of reach, politely ask: "Please pass the salt." Never stretch your arm across another person's plate or body to grab dishes. Always pass items to the right.
5.
Handling Cutlery:
Hold the fork in the left hand (prongs pointed down for cutting) and the knife in the right hand.
Use the knife strictly for cutting food into bite-sized pieces or guiding food onto the fork; never put a knife into your mouth.
Spoons are held in the right hand. Soup should be scooped gently away from you across the bowl.
1.
Toothpick Etiquette: If meat or food becomes trapped between teeth, use a clean wooden toothpick discreetly while shielding your mouth completely with your other hand or a napkin. Never use fingernails, sewing needles, broomsticks, or safety pins!

C. Concluding the Meal

1.
Finishing and Excusing Yourself: When finished eating, place your fork and knife parallel to each other across the center of your plate (handles facing four o'clock) to signal you have completed your meal. Politely say: "Thank you, may I please be excused?" before rising from the table.

1.
Energy / Caloric Requirements:
Active adolescents require high daily caloric intake (2,200 to 2,800 calories) to fuel rapid physical development, walking to school, and participation in vigorous sporting activities.
Energy must come from complex unrefined carbohydrates (brown rice, yams, oats, sweet potatoes, whole maize), not refined confectionery.
1.
High-Biological-Value Protein:
Crucial for synthesizing new muscle tissues, enzymes, and hormones.
Rich sources: Eggs, fish, cowpea beans, lean meat, dairy, and soybeans.
1.
Iron (Essential for Adolescent Girls):
Adolescent growth expands blood volume and muscle mass in all adolescents.
Adolescent girls have significantly higher iron requirements due to monthly blood loss during menstruation. Inadequate iron leads to iron-deficiency anemia, causing severe lethargy, pale palms and conjunctiva, dizziness, and low scholastic focus.
Rich sources: Beef liver, dark green leafy vegetables (Ugwu, waterleaf), beans, fish, and iron-fortified cereals, ideally consumed with vitamin C (citrus) to maximize absorption.
1.
Calcium, Phosphorus, and Vitamin D:
Over 40% of adult skeletal bone mass is deposited during the adolescent growth spurt. Inadequate calcium leads to stunted bone elongation and increases future risk of osteoporosis.
Rich sources: Milk, yogurt, bone-in fish (like small sardines and crayfish), dark leafy greens, and moderate sunlight exposure for endogenous vitamin D synthesis.
