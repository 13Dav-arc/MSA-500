Why This Matters to You

Have you ever wondered why whenever a trailer packed with hundreds of cattle, sheep, or goats arrives at the cattle depots of Berger in Lagos, Bodija in Ibadan, or Artisanal Market in Enugu, the animals almost always began their journey in states like Katsina, Sokoto, Borno, or Kano? Why aren't there massive herds of White Fulani cattle naturally grazing across the lush, green mangrove forests of Delta State or the dense rain forests of Cross River? After all, the South has more rain and greener vegetation all year round! The answer lies in a fascinating mixture of ecology, microscopic parasites, stinging flies, and climate. In this lesson, we will uncover why over 85% of Nigeria's ruminant livestock lives in the Northern savanna, examine the deadly threat of the tsetse fly belt, and discover how special indigenous animals survive in the humid South!

Livestock Population Patterns in Nigeria

Geographic Distribution of Livestock in Nigeria and Ecological Determinants: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration

In Nigeria, farm animals are not evenly spread across the thirty-six states and the Federal Capital Territory. Instead, there is a stark geographic divide:

Over 85% to 90% of all cattle, sheep, goats, donkeys, and camels are reared in the Northern Savanna belts (Sudan Savanna, Sahel, and Northern Guinea Savanna).
The Southern Rainforest and Mangrove zones maintain relatively small populations of specialized dwarf livestock and intensively managed poultry and commercial swine farms.

Key Determinants of Livestock Distribution in Nigeria

1. The Tsetse Fly Belt and Trypanosomiasis (Nagana)

The single greatest biological factor limiting cattle distribution in Southern and Central Nigeria is the *tsetse fly (Glossina spp.)*.

Tsetse flies require warmth, high humidity, and dense woody vegetation along river banks and forests to breed.
When an infected tsetse fly bites an animal, it injects microscopic protozoan parasites called Trypanosoma into the bloodstream.
In susceptible breeds like White Fulani (Bunaji), Red Bororo, and Sokoto Gudali, trypanosomiasis causes destruction of red blood cells (anemia), extreme weight loss, loss of milk, abortion, and rapid death.
Because the Northern Sudan and Sahel savannas are too dry and open for tsetse flies to thrive, humped Zebu herds flourish there safely.

2. Trypanotolerant Breeds of the South

Over centuries of natural selection, indigenous livestock in the South developed genetic resistance known as trypanotolerance:

Muturu Cattle: A dwarf, humpless taurine breed capable of thriving in tsetse-infested rainforests without succumbing to Nagana.
N'Dama Cattle: A medium-sized humpless breed originally from West Africa's Fouta Djallon highlands, also resistant to trypanosomiasis.
West African Dwarf (WAD) Goats and Sheep: Short-legged, highly resilient small ruminants kept across southern villages that tolerate trypanosome bites and humid conditions.

3. Vegetation and Pasture Availability

Northern savannas feature vast stretches of open grassland dominated by nutritious grasses like Guinea grass (Panicum maximum), Elephant grass (Pennisetum purpureum), and Gamba grass (Andropogon gayanus).
Animals can graze freely over miles of open country without becoming tangled in dense forest undergrowth.
In contrast, Southern rainforests are characterized by towering timber trees and dense canopies where edible grazing grasses cannot grow easily.

4. Climate and Relative Humidity

High relative humidity (above 80%) in the South promotes the proliferation of pathogenic fungi and bacteria, leading to frequent skin diseases, pneumonia, and debilitating foot-rot in cattle and sheep.
The dry, sunny climate of the North allows animals' hooves to remain hard and dry, minimizing fungal foot infections.

5. Religious and Cultural Traditions

The centuries-old pastoralist culture of the Fulani, Kanuri, and Shuwa Arab peoples in the North has created an established heritage of nomadic herding, animal husbandry, and selective breeding.
Islamic dietary laws (Halal) widely observed across Northern states support large-scale cattle, sheep, and goat production, while restricting commercial swine (pig) rearing. Swine production is therefore concentrated heavily in non-Muslim communities across Central and Southern Nigeria (e.g., Plateau, Benue, Kaduna South, Ogun, Oyo, and Edo).

6. Feed Distribution for Monogastrics (Poultry and Swine)

Unlike grazing cattle, commercial poultry and pigs are fed concentrated grain rations (maize, soybeans, groundnut cake, wheat offal).
Consequently, commercial poultry and pig farms can be set up anywhere in Nigeria where feed ingredients can be transported and where large consumer populations exist (e.g., peri-urban belts of Lagos, Ibadan, Port Harcourt, and Abuja).
