Why This Matters to You

Picture two very different farming scenes in Nigeria. In a small rural village in Benue State, a farmer named Baba Audu wakes at 5:00 AM with his wife and three teenage children. Armed with hand hoes and cutlasses, they walk to a small, fragmented two-acre plot to weed cassava, plant a few lines of maize, and tend three local goats tied to a tree. Everything they harvest will go straight into their family kitchen pot, with barely a small basket of peppers left over to sell at the local weekly market. Now travel to an expansive 2,000-hectare agricultural estate in Ogun or Nasarawa State. Here, massive 150-horsepower diesel tractors and computerized combine harvesters sweep across endless golden fields of maize and soybeans. Sophisticated center-pivot irrigation rigs spray water across thousands of rows, and articulated semi-trucks line up to transport grain to industrial flour mills in Lagos! These two scenes represent the two great systems of farming on our planet: Subsistence Agriculture and Commercial Agriculture. In this lesson, you will contrast their characteristics, analyze their economic advantages and disadvantages, and discover how Nigeria is transitioning toward modern commercial food security!

In-Depth Characteristics of Subsistence Agriculture

1.
Primary Objective: Feeding the farmer, spouse, and children. Any sale of produce occurs only when there is an accidental small surplus.
2.
Small and Fragmented Farm Size: Farmlands typically range from 0.5 to 2 hectares, often scattered in fragmented parcels across different village locations due to inheritance divisions.
3.
Reliance on Manual and Family Labor: Heavy reliance on manual human muscle. The farmer's wife, children, and relatives supply virtually all the labor; children weeding before and after school.
4.
Rudimentary Hand Tools: Operations are carried out with simple hand tools: traditional short-handled hoes, cutlasses, axes, digging sticks, and baskets.
5.
Low Capital Requirement: Requires minimal financial capital; farmers often save seeds from previous harvests and cannot afford commercial bank credit.
6.
Mixed Cropping (Intercropping): Planting yam, maize, cassava, and fluted pumpkin together on the same mounds to maximize diverse household food and insure against the failure of any single crop.
7.
Low Yield and Productivity: Yields per hectare are low due to poor seed genetics, lack of inorganic fertilizers, and uncontrolled pest attacks.

In-Depth Characteristics of Commercial Agriculture

1.
Primary Objective: Maximizing financial profit and returns on invested capital by selling vast quantities of agricultural commodities to domestic agro-allied factories and foreign export markets.
2.
Massive Land Holdings: Farms span hundreds or thousands of contiguous hectares (e.g., commercial oil palm plantations in Edo State, sugar cane estates in Kwara, or grain farms in Kaduna).
3.
Extensive Mechanization: Operations are mechanized from land clearing, plowing, harrowing, and planting to weeding, spraying, and combine harvesting.
4.
Hired Skilled Labor: Employs salaried professionals: agronomists, agricultural engineers, farm accountants, tractor drivers, and marketing specialists.
5.
Enormous Capital Investment: Requires tens or hundreds of millions of Naira in commercial bank loans, equity capital, high-yield hybrid seeds, heavy tractors, irrigation dams, and climate-controlled storage silos.
6.
Monoculture System: Focuses strictly on cultivating a single high-value crop (e.g., pure maize, pure rubber, pure cocoa, pure oil palm) to streamline mechanical harvesting and processing.
7.
High Yield and Quality Control: Yields per hectare are exceptionally high due to certified hybrid seeds, soil-tested fertilizer blends, automated drip irrigation, and strict agrochemical pest management.
