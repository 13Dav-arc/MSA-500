Why This Matters to You

When the house lights dim in a grand auditorium and the heavy velvet curtains sweep open, your eyes are immediately captivated by the actors commanding the stage. You watch kings issue decrees in shimmering robes, warriors clash in epic battles, and village seers deliver chilling prophecies under moonlit spotlights. But behind that dazzling performance, out of sight in the wings and the lighting booth, an entire invisible army has been working tirelessly for months! Who found the money to rent the hall and build the sets? Who coached the actors through every tear and laugh? Who built the royal throne out of plywood and gold paint? And who took a 12-year-old JSS 1 student and transformed him into an 80-year-old wrinkled elder with white hair and facial scars using a brush and cosmetics? That miracle is called Play Production! In this lesson, you will step behind the scenes to discover how a script is brought to life, master the vital roles of the Cast and Crew, and unlock the artistic secrets of Costuming and Stage Makeup!

2. The Director and Stage Manager: Captains of the Ship

The Director (The Artistic Visionary):
Analyzes the script to uncover themes, rhythms, and historical context.
Conducts auditions to select the right actors for each role.
Directs blocking—deciding when an actor enters from Stage Left, crosses to Center Stage, or sits on a throne.
Integrates the set, lights, sound, and costumes into one unified visual masterpiece.
The Stage Manager (The Operational Commander):
Keeps the Prompt Book (the "bible" of the production containing the script with every actor move, light cue, and sound effect penciled in).
During the live show, the Director steps back into the audience! The Stage Manager wears a headset and gives the commands: "Light Cue 14... GO! Sound Cue 7... GO! Cue King's entrance... GO!"

3. Costumes in Theatrical Storytelling

Costumes do much more than simply clothe an actor; they are visual shorthand that tells the audience who the character is before they even speak:

1.
Historical Period: Distinguishes an ancient 15th-century Benin Kingdom warrior from an oil merchant in 2026 Lagos.
2.
Social Status & Wealth: Rich brocades, royal coral beads (Iyun), and flowing embroidered Agbadas signify kings and chiefs; coarse homespun cloth or ragged tunics identify impoverished servants.
3.
Cultural and Ethnic Identity: Distinctive traditional attires identify specific cultures (e.g., Yoruba Aso-Oke, Igbo Isiagu with red chieftaincy cap, Hausa Babariga and turban).
4.
Character Psychology & Symbolism: A character wearing dark, sharp-edged garments conveys menace, while a character in clean white communicates innocence or spiritual purity.

4. Stage Makeup: The Two Grand Divisions

Theatrical spotlights are so fiercely bright that an un-made-up human face looks pale, flat, featureless, and dead. Stage makeup solves this problem:

```

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ STRAIGHT VERSUS CHARACTER MAKEUP │

├───────────────────┬──────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┤

│ PARAMETER │ STRAIGHT MAKEUP │ CHARACTER MAKEUP │

├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤

│ Primary Objective │ Enhance natural features │ Radically alter/trans- │

│ │ and neutralize glare. │ form actor's appearance.│

├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤

│ Target Features │ Eyebrows, cheekbones, │ Wrinkles, scars, age, │

│ │ lips, natural skin tone. │ ethnic marks, monsters. │

├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤

│ Common Tools │ Foundation base, powder, │ Crepe hair, liquid │

│ │ eyeliner, blush/rouge. │ latex, spirit gum, wax. │

├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤

│ Typical Usage │ Worn by ALL actors │ Worn by actors playing │

│ │ under bright spotlights. │ specific eccentric roles│

└───────────────────┴──────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┘

```

Techniques in Character Makeup:

Aging Techniques: Applying brown highlight lines along forehead creases, nasolabial folds, and crow's-feet, accented with white/cream highlights, combined with dusting talcum powder or gray paint into the actor's hair.
Traditional Scarification: Drawing authentic traditional ethnic facial markings (Ila in Yoruba, Gishiri / facial marks in Hausa and Igbo) using dark cosmetic liner pencils.
Wounds and Scars: Building three-dimensional cuts and scars using nose putty, cotton soaked in liquid latex, and artificial blood made of corn syrup and food coloring.
