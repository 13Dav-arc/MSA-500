Why This Matters to You

Walk down any busy street in your neighborhood, town, or city in Nigeria—whether in Aba, Ibadan, Kano, or Port Harcourt. You will see hundreds of vibrant businesses: the tailor stitching traditional attire in a small shop, the corner provision store selling bread and milk, the pharmacy dispensing medication, and the local law chambers with two partners' names boldly painted on the brass signboard. Have you ever wondered who owns these businesses? Who provided the startup money to rent the shop and buy the goods? If the business goes bankrupt and owes ₦5,000,000 to suppliers, who is legally forced to pay the debt? And if the owner suddenly falls ill or passes away, what happens to the shop? The answers to these crucial questions depend entirely on the legal structure chosen by the founders, known as the Form of Business Ownership! In this lesson, you will explore the two most widespread foundational forms of business organization: the Sole Proprietorship (One-Man Business) and the Partnership, uncover the legal concept of unlimited liability, and learn how business partnerships are forged!

2. The Sole Proprietorship (One-Man Business)

The sole proprietorship is the oldest, simplest, and most common form of business enterprise in Nigeria and worldwide (e.g., local barbershops, grocery stores, vulcanizing stands, private tutoring services):

A. Key Characteristics

Single Ownership: Established and owned by exactly one individual.
Easy Formation: Requires little legal formality to start; simple registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) if operating under a business name.
Source of Capital: Relies primarily on personal savings, gifts or loans from family and friends, and trade credit from wholesalers.
Unlimited Liability: The business and the owner are legally one and the same (no separate legal entity). If the business incurs debts, creditors have the legal right to seize the owner's personal home, land, and furniture to recover their money!
Lack of Continuity: The survival of the business is tied directly to the owner's lifespan and health. If the sole proprietor dies or becomes permanently incapacitated, the business frequently collapses.

B. Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages:
Quick Decision-Making: The owner acts instantly without consulting committees or partners.
100% of Profits: All net financial surplus belongs entirely to the sole proprietor.
Close Customer Relations: Direct, personal contact builds deep customer loyalty and tailored service.
Complete Business Secrecy: Financial records and trade recipes remain strictly confidential.
Disadvantages:
Unlimited Liability: Extreme personal financial risk.
Limited Capital: Capital is restricted to what one person can save or borrow.
Heavy Workload & Stress: The owner must handle all roles—buyer, salesman, accountant, cleaner.
Lack of Managerial Specialization: One person cannot be an expert in accounting, marketing, and legal affairs.

3. The Partnership

When an enterprise outgrows the financial or managerial capacity of a single individual, two or more people join forces to form a Partnership:

A. Key Characteristics

Membership Size: Minimum of 2 persons; maximum of 20 persons (extended up to 50 for specialized professional partnerships like law firms and accounting practices).
Formation via Partnership Deed: While oral agreements are legally valid, prudent partners draft a written Partnership Deed (Articles of Partnership) to prevent future disputes.
Capital Contribution: Partners pool their personal wealth, skills, and connections to raise substantially larger capital than a sole proprietor.
Mutual Agency: Every partner acts as an agent of the firm; the commercial actions and contracts signed by one partner legally bind all other partners!

B. Types of Partners

1.
Active (Managing) Partner: Contributes capital, participates daily in operations, and receives a salary in addition to profit share. Has unlimited liability.
2.
Dormant (Sleeping) Partner: Contributes financial capital and shares profits/losses, but takes no active part in daily management. Retains full liability.
3.
Nominal (Quasi) Partner: Contributes no capital and takes no part in management; merely lends their famous name, prestige, or goodwill to help the firm attract clients. However, they are legally liable to third parties who trusted the firm because of their name!
4.
General Partner: Has unlimited liability for all debts of the firm.
5.
Limited Partner: A partner whose liability is strictly limited to the amount of capital they invested. (In Nigeria, a limited partnership must have at least one general partner with unlimited liability).

C. Advantages & Disadvantages of Partnership

Advantages:
Greater Capital: Combining wealth allows partners to buy larger inventories and modern equipment.
Specialization of Labor: One partner manages accounting, another handles sales, and a third directs technical operations.
Shared Risk & Losses: Financial losses are divided among partners rather than crushing one person.
Better Credit Standing: Commercial banks lend money more readily to partnerships than sole proprietors.
Disadvantages:
Unlimited Liability: General partners risk losing personal property.
Disagreements & Friction: Quarrels, jealousy, and policy clashes can destroy the business.
Action of One Binds All: A reckless contract signed by an incompetent partner legally binds everyone.
Instability & Dissolution: The death, bankruptcy, or withdrawal of a key partner automatically dissolves the partnership unless the deed provides otherwise.
