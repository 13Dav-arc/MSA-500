Why This Matters to You

Imagine you give your younger brother a crisp ₦1,000 note to buy a loaf of bread for the family breakfast. What happened in that simple exchange? Your brother received ₦1,000, while your pocket gave out ₦1,000. When he gets to the bakery, the baker receives the ₦1,000, and gives your brother a warm loaf of bread. Notice that in every single financial transaction on Earth, value is never created out of thin air: whenever one party receives a benefit, another party gives an equivalent benefit! In 1494, Italian mathematician Fra Luca Pacioli realized that this universal law of balance could be turned into a flawless financial record-keeping system. He codified what is known across the world as the Double Entry System of Bookkeeping. By following one immortal rule—"Debit the receiver and Credit the giver"—businesses can track billions of Naira with mathematical perfection. In this lesson, you will master the foundational mechanics of double entry, learn the anatomy of a T-Account, and record assets and capital like a professional accountant!

Core Concept

1. The Fundamental Principle of Double Entry

Double Entry Bookkeeping I: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration

The core concept of double entry is straightforward: Every transaction involves two sides—a giving side and a receiving side.

```

┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ THE GOLDEN RULE OF BOOKKEEPING │

└───────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘

│

┌───────────────────┴───────────────────┐

▼ ▼

┌───────────────────────┐ ┌───────────────────────┐

│ DEBIT (DR) │ │ CREDIT (CR) │

│ "Debit the Receiver"│ │ "Credit the Giver" │

│ Left-Hand Side │ │ Right-Hand Side │

└───────────────────────┘ └───────────────────────┘

```

Because every debit entry must have an equal credit entry, the sum of all debits in an organization's accounting books will always equal the sum of all credits! If the two sides do not balance, an error has occurred.

2. The Anatomy of a T-Account (Ledger Account)

In the ledger, every distinct asset, liability, and equity stream receives its own dedicated page called a T-Account.

```

Name / Title of Account

DR (Left Side) CR (Right Side)

┌──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┼──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┐

│ Date │ Particulars │ Folio │Amount (₦) │ Date │ Particulars │ Folio │Amount (₦) │

├──────────┼──────────────┼───────┼───────────┼──────────┼──────────────┼───────┼───────────┤

│ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │

└──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┘

```

Title: Sits centered at the top (e.g., `Cash Account`, `Capital Account`, `Motor Vehicle Account`).
Date Column: Records the exact date the transaction occurred.
Particulars Column: States the name of the corresponding account that completed the other half of the double entry.
Folio Column: Cross-references the page number of the corresponding book or ledger.
Amount Column: Records the monetary value in Naira.

3. Double Entry Treatment of Capital

When an entrepreneur starts a new business or injects additional funds into an existing enterprise, the business receives value from the owner:

Rule for Capital Introduction:
1.
The account receiving the money (e.g., Cash Account or Bank Account) receives value: DEBIT.
2.
The Capital Account represents the owner's stake giving value to the business: CREDIT.

Example: Alhaji Sani starts business on January 1 with ₦500,000 cash.

Cash Account is DEBITED with ₦500,000 (Cash received).
Capital Account is CREDITED with ₦500,000 (Capital provided).

```

Cash Account

DR CR

┌──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┼──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┐

│ Jan 01 │ To Capital │ GL 01 │ 500,000 │ │ │ │ │

└──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┘

Capital Account

DR CR

┌──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┼──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┐

│ │ │ │ │ Jan 01 │ By Cash │ CB 01 │ 500,000 │

└──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┘
```

4. Double Entry Treatment of Assets

An Asset is any property or economic resource owned by a business:

Tangible Assets: Cash in hand, bank balances, office furniture, motor vehicles, computers, buildings, land, inventory.
Intangible Assets: Brand trademarks, software licenses, goodwill.

The Golden Rule for Assets:

INCREASE in an Asset: When a business acquires or buys an asset, that asset account receives value: DEBIT the Asset Account.
DECREASE in an Asset: When a business pays out money or sells an asset, the paying asset account gives out value: CREDIT the Paying Asset Account.

Example: On January 5, Alhaji Sani buys office furniture for ₦120,000 paying with cash.

1.
The Furniture Account receives value (a new asset enters the firm): DEBIT Furniture Account ₦120,000.
2.
The Cash Account gives out value (cash leaves the firm): CREDIT Cash Account ₦120,000.

```

Furniture Account

DR CR

┌──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┼──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┐

│ Jan 05 │ To Cash │ CB 01 │ 120,000 │ │ │ │ │

└──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┘

Cash Account

DR CR

┌──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┼──────────┬──────────────┬───────┬───────────┐

│ Jan 01 │ Capital │ GL 01 │ 500,000 │ Jan 05 │ Furniture │ GL 04 │ 120,000 │

└──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┴──────────┴──────────────┴───────┴───────────┘

```
