Foundational curriculum module in Business Studies covering "Double Entry Bookkeeping II: Treatment of Liabilities, Expenses, Revenue, and Balancing Accounts" for JSS 1.

Why This Matters to You

Imagine that an ambitious young merchant in Kano opens an electronics shop. He buys 20 refrigerators on credit from a wholesale distributor, promising to pay ₦4,000,000 in two months. Next, he rents a shop in the market for ₦300,000, hires two sales assistants whom he pays ₦80,000 in monthly wages, and pays ₦15,000 for electricity to run the shop lights. At the end of the month, his cash box has ₦1,200,000 from customer sales. Does that ₦1,200,000 belong to him as pure profit? Absolutely not! If he foolishly celebrates and spends all that cash on personal parties, the wholesale distributor will drag him to court, the landlord will lock his shop doors, and the electricity company will disconnect his wires! To know the real financial truth, every merchant must master the double-entry treatment of Liabilities (what you owe), Expenses (what you spend to run the business), and Revenue (what you earn), and learn how to Balance accounts at month-end. In this lesson, you will master the full spectrum of double entry and become an expert in balancing accounts!

Core Concept

1. The Double Entry Treatment of Liabilities

Double Entry Bookkeeping II: Core Concepts and Pedagogical Illustration

A Liability is money owed by the business to an outside party. Common examples include trade creditors, bank loans, and unpaid accrued bills.

```

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ DOUBLE ENTRY RULES: LIABILITIES │

├────────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┤

│ INCREASE IN A LIABILITY │ DECREASE IN A LIABILITY │

│ When the business borrows money or │ When the business pays off or │

│ buys goods on credit: │ settles part of its debt: │

│ • CREDIT the Liability / Creditor │ • DEBIT the Liability / Creditor │

│ Account (Gives credit benefit) │ Account (Receives debt relief) │

└────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘

```

A. Credit Purchases of Trading Goods:

When a business buys merchandise for resale on credit from a supplier (e.g., Alhaji Bello):

1.
DEBIT Purchases Account: Represents the acquisition of trading stock (merchandise asset).
2.
CREDIT Alhaji Bello (Creditor) Account: Represents the supplier who gave the credit value.

Example: On January 10, bought goods on credit from Amadu Electronics for ₦100,000.

Debit: Purchases Account ₦100,000.
Credit: Amadu Electronics Account ₦100,000.

B. Credit Sales of Trading Goods:

When a business sells merchandise on credit to a customer (e.g., B. Solape):

1.
DEBIT B. Solape (Debtor) Account: Represents the customer who received the goods and now owes money.
2.
CREDIT Sales Account: Represents the revenue generated by the business.

Example: On January 24, sold goods on credit to B. Solape for ₦25,000.

Debit: B. Solape Account ₦25,000.
Credit: Sales Account ₦25,000.

2. Double Entry Treatment of Expenses

Expenses are the operational costs required to keep the business running. Common examples include shop rent, staff wages, electricity tariffs, telephone bills, motor vehicle fuel, and advertising.

```

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ DOUBLE ENTRY RULES: EXPENSES │

│ Every expense consumed represents a operational value received: │

│ │

│ • DEBIT the Specific Expense Account (Rent, Wages, Electricity) │

│ • CREDIT the Cash Account (if paid in cash) or │

│ CREDIT the Bank Account (if paid by cheque / electronic transfer) │

└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```

Example: On June 12, paid shop rent of ₦60,000 by cheque, and paid cleaner's wages of ₦20,000 in cash.

1.
Rent Transaction:
Debit: Rent Account ₦60,000.
Credit: Bank Account ₦60,000.
1.
Wages Transaction:
Debit: Wages Account ₦20,000.
Credit: Cash Account ₦20,000.

---

3. Double Entry Treatment of Revenue

Revenue is the money earned from commercial activities:

When cash is received from direct sales: DEBIT Cash Account, CREDIT Sales Account.
When commission is received by cheque: DEBIT Bank Account, CREDIT Commission Received Account.
When rent is received from subletting a warehouse: DEBIT Cash/Bank Account, CREDIT Rent Received Account.

4. Summary of the Universal Double Entry Rules (DEAD CLIC)

Accountants use the popular mnemonic DEAD CLIC to memorize double entries instantly:

```

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ D E A D │

│ DEBIT: Expenses (E) | Assets (A) | Drawings (D) │

│ (These accounts increase with a DEBIT entry) │

├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤

│ C L I C │

│ CREDIT: Liabilities (L) | Income/Revenue (I) | Capital (C) │

│ (These accounts increase with a CREDIT entry) │

└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```

5. Step-by-Step Guide to Balancing Ledger Accounts

At the end of every week, month, or financial year, accounts must be balanced to determine their closing position:

```

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│ 5 STEPS TO BALANCE A LEDGER ACCOUNT │

│ │

│ Step 1: Add up the Debit side and Credit side on scrap paper. │

│ │

│ Step 2: Determine which side is GREATER and which is SMALLER. │

│ │

│ Step 3: Subtract the smaller side from the greater side. │

│ │

│ Step 4: Insert the difference on the SMALLER side as "Balance c/d" │

│ (carried down), dated on the last day of the month. │

│ │

│ Step 5: Sum both sides on the same horizontal line, seal with double │

│ underlines, and bring down the balance to the OPPOSITE side as │

│ "Balance b/d" (brought down) dated the 1st of the next month. │

└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```
